High flow nasal cannula and continuous positive airway pressure therapy in treatment of viral bronchiolitis: a randomized clinical trial

Signe Vahlkvist 1,2 & Louise Jürgensen 2 & Amalie la Cour 1 & Simone Markoew 1 & Thomas Houmann Petersen 2 & 2

Poul-Erik Kofoed

Received: 9 July 2019 /Revised: 14 November 2019 /Accepted: 19 November 2019 # Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2019 / Published online: 11 December 2019

Abstract

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) has been used in infants with bronchiolitis for decades. Recently, high flow nasal cannula (HFNC) therapy was introduced. We conducted a trial of 50 children with bronchiolitis who were randomized to treatment with CPAP or HFNC. Objectives were to compare the development in respiratory rate, pCO2, and Modified Woods Clinical Asthma Score (M-WCAS) in young children with bronchiolitis, treated with CPAP or HFNC. Secondarily, to compare Neonatal Infant Pain Score (NIPS), treatment duration, treatment failure, and hospitalization length. Median age at inclusion was 2.8 (CPAP group) vs 2.1 months (HFNC group). Mean baseline pCO2 was 6.7 in both groups and mean respiratory rate was 60 vs 56 in the CPAP and HFNC group respectively. No differences were observed in development of respiratory rate, pCO2, or MWCAS.NIPSwashigher in the CPAP group. Treatment failure was scarce in both groups. No significant differences in treatment duration or length of hospitalization were observed.

Conclusion : In infants and young children with bronchiolitis, HFNC may be an effective and pleasant alternative to CPAP. Larger multicenter studies are needed to further explore differences in treatment failure and treatment duration.

Trial registration : www.clinicaltrial.gov. id NCT02618213, registration date December 1, 2015.

Introduction

Viral bronchiolitis is often caused by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and is a major cause of disease burden in infants and young children. RSV infections present with symptoms from mild coryza to fulminant respiratory failure and are a major cause of hospitalization in young children. Thus, in Denmark, approximately 3% of children under 2 years of age experience at least one hospitalization due to RSV [4]. Because of airway obstruction, alveolar atelectasis, decreased oxygenation, and increased respiratory work, some children progress to severe respiratory distress and need for noninvasive respiratory support or mechanical ventilation.

In Denmark, the standard treatment for infants and young children with respiratory distress due to moderate to severe bronchiolitis has for many years been continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). Although not fully understood, CPAP may work by small airway distension, mucus wash out, and delivery of high flow oxygen [13]. CPAP has been shown to lower respiratory rate and pCO2 better than standard care [14] and has been associated with decreased intubation rates [3]. However, the therapy requires special staff skills, close monitoring, and can be stressful to the child.

Recently, high flow oxygenation therapy (HFNC) was introduced as an alternative tool in the management of respiratory distress due to bronchiolitis. HFNC delivers a heated, humidified gas with a mixture of oxygen and air, washes out dead space, and has some CPAP effect [11]. In a few bronchiolitis studies, HFNC proved to be better than standard care [1] [7]. Furthermore HFNC has been shown to give a better patient tolerance, less nose damage, and less work for the staff than CPAP [12].

We conducted a two-center prospective open randomized trial of HFNC and CPAP to explore effects of treatment with these two systems on respiratory variables in infants and young children with bronchiolitis.

Materials and methods

Patients

Bronchiolitis was defined as an acute respiratory illness in children < 2 years of age presenting with characteristic patterns of wheeze and cough. Criteria for respiratory support were based on clinical and paraclinical signs of respiratory failure: High respiratory rate (RR), retractions, recurrent apnea, hypercapnia, and acidosis.

Inclusion criteria for this study were children with bronchiolitis and need for respiratory support as assessed by the pediatrician in charge. Furthermore, informed consent from all parents/legal guardians should be obtained before randomization.

Exclusion criteria were previous chronic disease or severe respiratory insufficiency with decreased consciousness, need for urgent treatment, and/or a capillary pCO2 > 9.0.

The patient registration system was searched to evaluate the frequency of children with an ICD10 diagnose code of DJ960*, DJ21*, DJ205*, or DJ12* and the procedure code BGFC32 during the study period included.

Settings

The study was conducted between December 2015 and May 2018. During the first two seasons, the study took place at the pediatric department at the Hospital of South West Jutland, Denmark, and during the last seasons also at the department of pediatrics at Kolding Hospital, Denmark.

All children fulfilling the inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria were invited to participate. Because of the nature of this study, blinding was not possible. Randomization was conducted in blocks of four, stratified per center, and treatment allocation envelopes were stored in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, prefabricated by a person outside the study. By mistake, the first 20 patients were however randomized by simple lot, conducted by a person outside the study.

For CPAP, a binasal prong with a Benveniste valve connected to a humidifier (Fisher & Paykel Healthcare® Auckland, New Zealand) was used. The initial flow was 12 -14 L/min.

For HFNC, we used Optiflow Junior (Fisher & Paykel Healthcare® Auckland, New Zealand). Three sizes of nasal prongs were used, according to the weight of the child. The initial flow was 2 l/kg/min.

In both systems, flow could be increased to a maximum of 15/l/min and oxygen supply was delivered as needed to maintain a SpO2 above 92%.

Observations and paraclinical examination

PCR analysis of nasopharyngeal mucus for RSV was routinely analyzed in all patients. Chest x-ray was conducted prior to treatment and described by a radiologist. Capillary gas values (pH, pCO2) were conducted prior to treatment and after 6, 12, 24, and 48 h of treatment.

All children were intensively observed by a trained nurse. For this study, heart rate (HR), RR, and oxygen supply were registered at treatment start and every 6 h for the first 24 h and then once daily.

Modified Woods Clinical Asthma Score (M-WCAS) [15] is a combined score (0 -10) used to evaluate the degree of respiratory distress in children with bronchiolitis. M-WCAS has been validated for use in hospitalized children with bronchiolitis and includes evaluation of oxygen saturation, inspiratory breath sounds, expiratory wheezing, use of accessory respiratory muscles, and cerebral function [2].

The physician in charge assessed all the included children with the M-WCAS before initiation of treatment and then once daily.

Neonatal Infant Pain Score [5] (NIPS) was used in the last season of inclusion in children younger than 2 months. The score is a validated system based on facial mimic, cry, breathing habitant, arm and leg tonus, and consciousness. A total of 0 -8 points can be achieved, where 0 represents no pain, and eight maximal pain. The children were scored shortly after initiation of therapy and then once daily. For children older than 2 months, the 0 -10 point Face Leg Activity Cry and Consolability (FLACC) score [6] was used.

For this study, scores were also given as percent of maximum score in order to merge data in a combined pain score regardless of age.

Treatment length was recorded as net time, excluding breaks. Breaks mainly occurred when the condition of the child improved, as an attempt to discontinue treatment.

Treatment failure was assessed by the physician in charge who also decided whether it should lead to a switch of system (CPAP to HFNC/HFNC to CPAP) or to transmission to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) with or without initiation of mechanical ventilation prior to transportation.

Retrospectively, we searched the electronic patient database for infants and young children hospitalized with RSVrelated disease (DJ121, DJ205, DJ210) during the study period. The purpose of this was to estimate the fraction of CPAP/ HFNC support, and the fraction of study participants in this population.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were change in pCO2, RR, and M-WCAS from time 0 to 48 h after initiation of the treatment. Change in fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) was added as an unplanned analysis.

Secondary outcomes were differences in pain scores, treatment duration, and frequency of system failure.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was conducted based on the primary outcome pCO2. Assuming a difference of one kPa at 48 h and considering an α error of 5% and a β error at 20%, a sample size of 48 participants was calculated.

For group comparisons, two-sided Student ' s t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for normally and nonnormally distributed data respectively. Chi 2 test was used for binary outcomes.

Developments in RR, pCO2, M-WCAS, FiO2, and pain scores (NIPS, FLACC, and combined pain score) were measured using a random intercept model with maximum likelihood estimation. Device, time, and interaction between these were chosen as explanatory variables. Post estimation analysis was conducted using residual diagnostics. For the primary outcomes, the model was then extended with season and site, to explore effects of these.

Comparisons of treatment duration and length of hospitalization were conducted using log rank test for equality.

For all analyses, significance level was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA10 statistical software.

Ethics

The study was prospectively approved by the Regional Committee on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark, the Danish Data Protection Agency, and registered as a clinical trial at Clinicaltrials.gov. A protocol amendment was approved prior to the third season, regarding extension with another center and the use of pain scores. Written informed consent was collected from all legal guardians before any study-related procedures took place. In cases of only one present guardian, a prospectively written authorization from the absent guardian to the present guardian, regarding decision about participation, was accepted.

Results

During the study period, a total of 266 children were hospitalized with RSV-related disease. Of these, 99 children were treated with CPAP/HFNC for viral bronchiolitis at the two sites and 50 children were included in the study (22 HFNC/ 28 CPAP). During the first 48 h, ten children left the study. Eight children (5 CPAP/3 HFNC) left due to cessation of need for respiratory support and two children (both CPAP) left because of disease progression leading to transmission to the PICU. Four of the ten children were withdrawn during the first 24 h, including the two children in need of intensive care and one from each group due to cessation of need for respiratory support.

Table 1 describes the 50 children included. No differences were seen between the groups in baseline demographic or respiratory variables.

RR, pCO2, FiO2 (Fig. 1), and M-WCAS (Table 2) all declined during the first 48 h of treatment. No differences between the groups were seen. FiO2 showed a tendency towards faster decline in the CPAP group; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance ( p = 0.07).

Including season and site in the model had no effect on pCO2, M-CWAS, or FiO2. There was effect of site on the development in RR (coefficient 7.3, p > 0.05) with the children from Kolding having a lower RR. However, this did not affect the development in RR in the model.

During the first treatment day, NIPS was lower in the HFNCgroup right after initiation of therapy but declined with time in both groups (Table 2). There was however an overall effect of system in the regression model (coefficient -0.94. p < 0.05) meaning that NIPS was overall lower in the HFNC group. FLACC score was not analyzed, due to the few children assessed with this score. The combined pain score showed similar results as NIPS (Table 2).

The CPAP group had a median treatment time of 70 (6 -240) hours and the HFNC group of 95 (27 -193) hours. However, this difference of 25 h did not reach statistical significance. A similar and non-significant difference was seen in length of hospitalization.

There were few treatment failures in both groups. Four children (two from each group) were switched to the opposite system because of disease progression (HFNC) or poor system tolerance (CPAP). In addition, two CPAP children were transferred to the PICU because of treatment failure. Between groups, there were no statistical differences in frequency of treatment failure or in PICU transferal.

Discussion

In this study, no differences were found in the primary outcomes, suggesting that HFNC may be a good alternative to CPAP in children with bronchiolitis. Our study suggests a better patient acceptance of HFNC compared with CPAP as measured by the NIPS and the combined pain score, which corroborate earlier findings [9, 12]. Sarkar et al. [12] also found less nose damage with HFNC, an outcome not investigated in the present study.

To our knowledge, only few studies compared CPAP and HFNC in management of bronchiolitis. In a retrospective study, Pedersen et al. found that CPAP was superior to HFNC in lowering RR and FiO2 [10]. A multicenter study including 142 children found a higher success rate in the group treated with CPAP [9]. However, two other small studies [8, 12] found the two systems equally effective in lowering of RR, PCO2, etc.

present literature, there might be an indication of superiority of CPAP over HFNC, when treating severely ill children with bronchiolitis [9, 10]. In the present study, and in the study by Sarkar et al. [12], an upper pCO 2 limit may have excluded more severely ill children, whereas Milesi et al. [9] only excluded children with urgent need for intubation or radiologistverified atelectasis. This may account for some of the differences in results and could indicate that HFNC mainly is an alternative tool in less severe bronchiolitis.

In Denmark, there is a strong tradition for CPAP, and it is likely that it is used in less sick children than elsewhere, which could hamper the comparison between studies. However, CPAP/HFNC was not used more frequently in the years of this study, compared with the two previous seasons [10]. Whether some of the less sick children would have benefited equally to conservative treatment (oxygen, feeding supply, etc.) is a question that goes beyond the purpose for this study.

The use of the change in pCO2, a highly variable parameter of modest clinical relevance, for sample size calculation could be considered as a weakness in the study design. However, we see no signs of clinically relevant differences in our primary outcomes (Fig. 1). Moreover, the size of our study population is comparable with or bigger than for other studies [8, 12].

Only half of the children admitted with viral bronchiolitis and treated with CPAP/HFNC were included in the study. The major cause was failure to obtain informed consent from both parents because only one of the parents/legal guardians was present at the hospital. Others declined participation or meet an exclusion criterion.

The 48-h observation period in our study was chosen to minimize treatment termination within the period, as it was expected that most children would need at least 48 h of treatment. Moreover, we expected that the main effects would occur during this time span. The termination of treatment before 48 h in ten children may have affected the results. However, the dropouts were relatively equally distributed between the two groups, and furthermore, it was only children from the CPAP group who left due to a deterioration of the condition resulting in intensive care. Overall, we do not consider that dropouts during the first 48 h affected the results in favor of HFNC.

Despite a clinically relevant difference of 25 h, no statistically significant difference in treatment length between the groups was found. We suppose that this is primarily due to the lack of power in our study. However, the transferal of two children from the CPAP group to PICU (who therefore were withdrawn from the analysis) may have contributed to the difference in average treatment length. In the present study, treatment failures were few and equally distributed in the two groups. This is in contrast to earlier findings. In a retrospective study, Pedersen et al. [14] found a higher failure rate within the HFNC group, and in a multicenter study including 142 children, Milesi et al. found a higher success rate in the group treated with CPAP [9].

As our study was not powered to show non-inferiority, our results should be followed up, preferably by larger multicenter studies with statistical power to explore this. Also, clinically relevant outcomes like treatment duration, treatment failure, and intubation rates could not be investigated sufficiently in the present study. Hereby, possible differences in patient acceptance, side effects, and staff resource costs within the different treatment systems could be the aim for further investigation.

In conclusion, we found HFNC to be a safe and effective tool for respiratory support in young children with moderatesevere bronchiolitis. We found similar effects on RR, MWCAS, and pC02 compared with CPAP. Pain scores were significantly lower in the HFNC group indicating better patient acceptance. More research is warranted to further explore target groups for each of the two systems.
