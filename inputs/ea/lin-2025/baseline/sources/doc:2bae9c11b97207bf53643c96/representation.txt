The Imaging-Neuropathological Gap in Acute Large Vessel Occlusive Stroke

Ahmad A. Ballout , MD; David S. Liebeskind , MD; Tudor G. Jovin , MD; Souhel Najjar , MD

While imaging has traditionally played a fundamental role in the selection of patients undergoing endovascular thrombectomy, recent thrombectomy trials involving patients with large ischemic strokes demonstrated a consistent benefit of endovascular thrombectomy across all imaging strata, suggesting that reperfusion benefit may exist independent of current imaging constructs. Although these findings attest to the uniformly beneficial effects of reperfusion, they also shed doubt on the accuracy and utility of our imaging modalities in defining reversible versus irreversible ischemia and challenge the premise of imaging-based selection. We aimed to review the histopathologic studies and clinical trials that have shaped our understanding of current imaging constructs aiming to outline the existing imaging-neuropathological gap that may be far wider than previously perceived.

I maging-based selection for the endovascular treatment of large vessel ischemic stroke has been premised on imaging constructs of the core and penumbra. These entities were first postulated from early animal studies and later refined in humans using various hemodynamic, metabolic, and noninvasive imaging models. 1,2 Enthusiasm surrounded these constructs after studies suggested that these concepts may have the capability of ascertaining the degree of clinical benefit derived from reperfusion. 3 Momentum was later gained following a wave of positive endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) trials for which imaging-based selection was believed to play a pivotal role in the trials' success. 4,5 The recent large-core trials, however, have challenged the accuracy of these constructs and the very premise of imaging-based selection, as these studies have suggested that the benefit of EVT may exist independent of core size or penumbral volume. 6-13 While these findings may testify to the power of the treatment, they equally contest the accuracy of our imaging constructs in predicting the viability of tissue and their clinical utility in determining the extent of clinical benefit of reperfusion. 12,13 Indeed, the imagingneuropathological gap inherent to our imaging may be far wider than we had previously perceived. Our aim was to review the histopathologic studies and clinical trials that have shaped our understanding of core and penumbra as a preliminary step to hopefully narrow the imaging-neuropathological gap.

DEFINING THE ISCHEMIC CORE AND PENUMBRA

The ischemic core represents hypoperfused brain regions that have undergone morphologically irreversible damage (infarction), while the penumbra represents functionally impaired but salvageable tissue. 1,2 Although the penumbra is an elusive phenomenon that is in constant flux between its recruitment into the core and its escape into a band of oligemia, 1 our operational models have reduced it to a static definition usually based on a snapshot in time of computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) derived thresholds that attempt to delimit these imaginary boundaries. 14,15 These transition zones are based on animal and human studies that have largely used quantitative hemodynamics, molecular, and noninvasive imaging to define them.

Quantitative Hemodynamics and Molecular Imaging

Hemodynamic definitions derived primarily from positron emission tomography studies have estimated that cerebral infarction (core) occurs at cerebral blood flow (CBF) of <10 mL/100 g, functionally impaired but salvageable tissue (penumbra) occurs between CBF levels of 10 to 20 mL/100 g, and hypoperfused but not imminently at-risk tissue (noncore nonpenumbra) exists between CBF levels of 20 to 30 mL/100 g. 16-18 Molecular definitions of the ischemic core and penumbra have focused on protein synthesis, ATP production, and HSP (heat-shock protein) induction. A decrease in protein synthesis occurs when the relative CBF value drops to 30% to 50% of baseline, potentially reversible with ATP preservation (penumbra) but leading to irreversible translation blockade and infarction (core) with complete ATP loss. 2,18,19 HSP70 (heat shock protein 70), crucial for protein repair, is expressed postischemia, but only regions with adequate energy reserves can translate it into functional protein, suggesting potential recovery. Given the differential ischemic vulnerabilities between cell lines, 20 a tissue sample may exhibit heterogeneous infarction patterns due to variations in various energy reserves and protein synthesis capabilities (Figure 1).

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Animal Studies

The spatial resolution of diffusion MRI limits its capability to directly analyze morphological changes that occur on a cellular level. Therefore, the viability of tissue can only be inferred based on previous histopathologic correlations. We will analyze the literature to assess the evidence that supports or negates the hypothesis that diffusion restriction, or specific apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) thresholds, can be used to predict irreversible infarction.

The quality of evidence supporting the correlation between diffusion restriction and histopathologic infarction was assessed in a systemic review of focal ischemic stroke models using a predefined methodological quality scoring system. 21 Of the 1579 papers screened, 141 met inclusion for review, with only 13 (9.7%) meeting their prespecified quality threshold, and only 3 studies (2% of total animals) had performed blinded analyses. Less than a quarter of studies quantified the correlation between diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and histological analysis beyond stating that they correlated well and only 13% examined subregions of the DWI lesion or histopathologic abnormality. and distilled the key takeaway points pertinent to the hypothesis.

In the absence of reperfusion, specific ADC thresholds can approximate the macroscopically visible boundaries of stained tissue infarction, but heterogeneous ADC values likely exist within these boundaries, suggesting spatial variance in energy failure across the demarcated area.

In 1990, Moseley et al 35,36 conducted 2 studies involving permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) showing a close correlation between the diffusionrestricted and histopathologic volumes at 24 hours. Subsequent studies of permanent MCAO found significant heterogeneity of ADC existing within the histopathologic margins of infarction. 37,38

Before reperfusion, the DWI region appears to approximate the macroscopically visible boundaries of stained tissue infarction with relative accuracy. Overestimation may occur with ultraearly reperfusion, while slight underestimation or overestimation may occur with delayed reperfusion.

In a temporary MCAO model, transient ischemia of <20 minutes did not result in any apparent changes in ADC or histopathology, suggesting that ultraearly reperfusion may prevent histopathologic damage. 39 In a study involving 45 minutes of transient MCAO, DWI lesions underestimated infarction margins by 6%, 30 contrasted by another study demonstrating a 6% overestimation of the infarction margins when reperfusion occurred within 120 minutes. 34 Another study showed less than a 15% volume difference between the DWI estimated and histopathologic infarction margins but did not specify whether this difference resulted from underestimation or overestimation. 24

Discordance may exist between the ADC signal intensity, the extent of histopathologic injury, and functional outcomes. Regional variability of the ADC value may exist transiently but is rapidly abolished and may not necessarily correlate with the degree of histopathologic injury.

In a study of rats undergoing transient MCAO for 30 minutes, investigators found a 5× greater proportion of neuronal necrosis in the striatum compared with the cortex, despite similar ADC values between regions, with complete functional recovery in all rats. 26 Conversely, in a study of permanent MCAO for 90 minutes, a higher proportion of shrunken neurons were found in the cortex compared with the striatum, despite the cortex exhibiting nearly half of the ADC decrease. 29 Regional ADC variability was found between cortical and striatal regions following 36 minutes of MCAO, but levels became comparable after 48 minutes of ischemia, highlighting the short-lived ADC variability that may exist. 29

ADC normalization, whether transient (pseudonormalization) or persistent, cannot reliably be used to predict histopathologic recovery.

Following rapid reperfusion, ADC normalization can either persist or result in a secondary decline several hours after reperfusion (pseudonormalization). 26,28,32 Notably, both neuronal and astrocytic injuries appear to progress despite transient or persistent ADC normalization, and the extent of injury does not seem to temporally align with the ADC value. 26,27 Compared with secondary ADC decline, persistent ADC normalization may be associated with less neurological injury; however, significant neuronal injury may still be identified by using immunohistochemical stains, such as MAP2 (microtubuleassociated protein 2), which may be missed on macroscopic histopathologic stains. 28,32

There is likely a link between neuronal shrinkage and astroglia swelling that exists during acute ischemia. The initial ADC decline may indicate astroglia swelling as a compensatory response to neuronal shrinkage, while ADC normalization could signal a temporary balance occurring post-reperfusion. The secondary decline in ADC may reflect compensatory failure, resulting in recurrent glial swelling or disintegration (Figure 2).

Multiple studies consistently show astroglia swelling alongside neuronal shrinkage during ischemia or reperfusion. 25,27,29,32 For instance, Ringer et al 32 investigated immunohistopathological changes during transient ischemia lasting 30 minutes. They found partial or complete DWI reversal in all 11 rats within 5 hours, with recurrence at 24 hours, indicating DWI pseudonormalization. Despite reversed DWI signals, significant loss of neuronal MAP2 was observed, affecting about a third of the initial DWI lesion. HSP72 signaling, a neuroprotectant indicative of ischemic stress, 19 persisted in the reversed DWI region. In addition, GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein) staining revealed astroglia reactivity and swelling in DWI lesions, which normalized alongside diffusion reversal, illustrating the crucial osmoregulatory role of astroglia during cerebral perfusion compromise. 40

The morphological extent of cell injury depends on the duration of ischemia, although heterogeneous patterns of injury may exist for any given time.

In the acute stage of ischemia, neuronal cell bodies may seem shrunken and triangular, along with neuronal perikarya surrounded by vacuoles. 24 These features may become intermixed with cytoplasmic eosinophilia (red neurons) in the subacute stage, characteristics of delayed neuronal injury. Heterogeneous morphological features may become evident in the chronic stage, such as the abundance of ghost neurons in some tissues, with progression to pan-necrosis in others. This time dependence was further shown in a study demonstrating necrosis in 95% of neurons following 30 minutes of ischemia, while only 17% of neurons were deemed necrotic after 10 minutes of ischemia. 28

Human Studies

Given the absence of human studies directly correlating MRI and histopathology during acute ischemic stroke, the translational use of MRI in acute ischemic stroke has been largely founded on the animal studies above, as well as the clinical, hemodynamic, and radiographic validation following reperfusion. 41 This has largely been premised on the reversibility of DWI and the correlation between initial DWI and final T2 volume. An ADC threshold of <620×10 -6 mm 2 /s has been identified as the optimal threshold at which restricted diffusion is unlikely to reverse, and this threshold has been used by automated software to define the core in modern reperfusion trials. 42

In an evidence-based systemic review assessing the level of evidence supporting the hypothesis that DWI hyperintensity represents irreversible infarction, DWI reversal was estimated to occur at a mean rate of 24% of patients with great variability of lesion reversal (0%83%). 43 These findings, coupled with the absence of any single tissue-outcome correlation study, merited the lowest possible evidence grade of a D based on the Oxford Center for Evidenced-Based Medicine. Further studies have suggested that ADC coefficient thresholds may not predict the response to acute stroke thrombolysis. 44 The DEFUSE 2 study (MRI Profile and Response to Endovascular Reperfusion) 3 proposed diffusion-perfusion mismatch profiles that could potentially differentiate between effective and ineffective reperfusion and was later adopted as inclusion criteria in many contemporary EVT trials. 5 While these target mismatch profiles seem to be Several hours following reperfusion, ADC levels normalize, likely representing a transient state of homeostasis where the astroglia have returned to their near-baseline morphological state. Noncontrast CT

Animal Studies

CT-based animal studies focused primarily on how blood flow affects water density and the correlation between x-ray hypoattenuation and final histopathologic volume. Investigators demonstrated that x-ray hypoattenuation likely begins below a critical CBF threshold of 15 to 20 mL/100 g per min, coinciding with the threshold of electrical failure and potential breakdown in synaptic transmission. 46 This occurred above the expected threshold of ionic membrane failure ( ≈ 9-11 mL/100 g per min), suggesting that x-ray hypoattenuation may partially reflect alternative mechanisms that precede ion-pump failure. Another study revealed that the duration of ischemia impacts brain edema development, with cerebral water content increasing from 78% to 80.5% within 3 hours of permanent arterial occlusion and up to 83% within 9 hours. 47 Restoring CBF reduced edema after <1 hour of ischemia but exacerbated it after >3 hours, indicating a critical window for reperfusion effects. 47

Researchers have used CT to measure water content in early ischemic edema. An in vitro model estimated that a 2.6-Houndfields unit decrease in x-ray hypoattenuation likely occurs per 1% increase in water (r=0.992; P <0.0001). 48 A similar relationship was later shown in a rat model undergoing up to 6 hours of MCAO. 49 In this study, a 1% increase in tissue water content correlated with a 1.8-Houndfields unit decrease in attenuation (r=0.55; P <0.001). 49 A study that induced severe focal ischemia in nonhuman primates for 6 hours showed that CT density changes decrease immediately following occlusion, followed by a transient return to normal within 30 to 60 minutes, followed by a secondary decrease beginning at 2 hours and plateauing as it reaches 6 hours. 50 These findings suggested that CT density changes likely lag behind severe focal ischemia by 2 hours. In a reperfusion experiment with rats, x-ray attenuation stabilized after 1 hour of MCAO but steadily decreased after 2 and 4 hours post-reperfusion. 51 In cases where reperfusion occurred after 1 hour, a third of the rats exhibited complete reversal of hypoattenuation with no evidence of macroscopic histopathologic changes. 51 However, histopathologic changes were observed in rats where hypoattenuation either halted or progressed, suggesting that x-ray hypoattenuation may occasionally recover with ultrarapid reperfusion within 1 hour but tends to progress if reperfusion occurs 2 hours or later.

Human Studies

Given the absence of tissue-level validation studies, human studies have relied on radiographic validation of CT hypodensity by comparing the prereperfusion and postreperfusion scans. One of the largest studies investigating the relationship between early ischemic changes and follow-up imaging was performed using the data from the prospective thrombolytic trial, ECASS II (Randomized Double-Blind PlaceboControlled Trial of Thrombolytic Therapy With Intravenous Alteplase in Acute Ischemic Stroke), in patients presenting within 6 hours of symptom onset. 52 In this study, ischemic edema identified on baseline CT was 96% specific for final infarction, and the presence of ischemic edema was a poor prognostic marker. Interestingly, the infarction volume grew by a mean of 13 mL between day 1 and day 7 follow-up scans, despite a 2-point improvement in the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale, highlighting the discrepancy that may exist between volumetric infarction and clinical outcome.

Summary

In summary, these studies highlight the connection between x-ray hypoattenuation, the severity and duration of ischemia, and the presence of histopathologic infarction.

The inverse linear relationship between x-ray hypoattenuation and net water concentration allows for noninvasive quantitative measurement of tissue water. However, correlating this with the degree of tissue infarction would require assuming a linear relationship between tissue water and the morphological extent of tissue infarction, for which there is limited evidence. While hypodensity on CT is the lesion least prone to reversibility following reperfusion and may, perhaps, characterize pan-necrosis with the highest specificity, the morphological extent of infarction within a hypodense voxel remains largely unknown. Furthermore, x-ray hypoattenuation likely develops below the threshold of electrical failure, rather than the expected threshold of ion-pump failure, which may, in part, explain the narrow window of potential reversibility of x-ray hypoattenuation (Figure 3). The mechanisms underlying x-ray hypoattenuation that occurs above the threshold of ion-pump failure remain largely unknown. 2 Drawing from the correlative MRI studies above, it seems plausible that this initial decrease in x-ray hypoattenuation could represent, at least in part, a compensatory astroglia response.

CT Perfusion

Perfusion imaging relies on radiotracers as a blood proxy to quantify hemodynamics and can broadly be categorized based on the permeability of the tracer across the blood-brain barrier. 53 Current technologies use iodine or gadolinium, nondiffusible tracers that are confined to the intravascular space, limiting the hemodynamic outputs to indirect approximations. This differs from diffusible tracers, such as Xenon CT, which freely penetrates tissue allowing for direct, absolute, quantitative hemodynamic measurements. 53 The constraints associated with the insolubility of iodine limit the mathematical calculations of CBF to relative CBF values that depend on contralateral hemispheric flow. 53 Nonetheless, a relative CBF value of <30% (using current nondiffusible tracers) has been shown to be least prone to reversibility, thus, best approximates the infarction volume as assessed on DWI as a reference, and has been used widely in contemporary reperfusion trials. 15 Despite its limitations, including the possibility of core overestimation in early presenting patients, 54 the CBF approximated core has been shown to be a strong predictor of outcome following reperfusion therapy. 45 However, its prognostic value does not equate to treatment effect modification, and in several large-core trials, the CBF-derived estimated infarct volume did not modify the treatment effect of EVT. 12,45,55 The clinical use of CTP will be discussed in the reperfusion trials section below.

ENDOVASCULAR REPERFUSION TRIALS

Endovascular treatment trials, while primarily addressing reperfusion safety and efficacy, indirectly serve as valuable platforms to assess the accuracy of our imaging modalities in predicting tissue infarction before reperfusion. Our focus will be on discussing the evidence for or against our hypothesis that the benefit of reperfusion may partly exist due to salvageable tissue concealed within the margins of the radiographically estimated core or beyond the penumbra.

Small-to-Moderate-Size Core Trials

The EVT after large vessel ischemic stroke metaanalysis demonstrated a treatment benefit of EVT in early presenting patients with small-to-moderate-sized core volumes, as the participating trials largely excluded patients with the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) of 0 to 5. 4 However, some patients who were enrolled based on local investigator adjudicated ASPECTS scores of >5 were subsequently found by core laboratory review to have lower ASPECTS scores. Subsequent analyses of those patients with core laboratory adjudicated ASPECTS 0 to 5 in EVT after large vessel ischemic stroke revealed a beneficial effect of endovascular therapy compared with standard care. 56 A subsequent meta-analysis revealed that larger ischemic core volumes ( ≥ 70 mL) were independently associated with worse functional outcomes but did not negate the treatment benefit of EVT. 45 Subanalyses of MR CLEAN (A Randomized Trial of Intraarterial Treatment for Acute Ischemic Stroke) 57 and ESCAPE (Randomized Assessment of Rapid Endovascular Treatment of Ischemic Stroke) 55 supported these findings, showing no significant interaction between perfusion core volume (as assessed by CT perfusion) and the treatment effect. In addition, the treatment effect persisted even without a target mismatch profile in the MR CLEAN analysis. 57 The AURORA (Thrombectomy for Anterior Circulation Stroke Beyond 6 h From Time Last Known Well) meta-analysis, 5 like EVT after large vessel ischemic stroke, linked lower baseline ASPECTS scores to less favorable outcomes in late-presenting patients but not necessarily to lack of benefit from EVT. Benefits were comparable between ASPECTS 8 to 10 and ASPECTS 6 to 7 groups, as well as between ASPECTS 8 to 10 and ASPECTS 0 to 7 groups. Firm conclusions for ASPECTS 0 to 5 were hindered by their <10% representation; however, no heterogeneity of treatment effect could be demonstrated when this baseline imaging category was specifically analyzed in subgroup analysis compared with ASPECTS 6 to 7 and 8 to 10.

Large-Core Trials

Five randomized controlled trials unequivocally demonstrated the safety and efficacy of EVT in patients with large ischemic strokes based on parenchymal and perfusion imaging. 6-8,10,11 TESLA (Thrombectomy for Emergent Salvage of Large Anterior Circulation Ischemic Stroke) aligned with the results of the other trials but fell short of statistical significance (under review). 9

A post hoc subanalysis of RESCUE-Japan LIMIT (Recovery by Endovascular Salvage for Cerebral UltraAcute Embolism Japan-Large Ischemic Core Trial) 58 showed that EVT resulted in higher odds of survival with less disability compared with best medical therapy in patients with baseline ASPECTS of 4-5 (common odds ratio, 4.48 [95% CI, 2.07-9.71]) but no statistically significant evidence of clinical benefit noted with EVT in patients with baseline ASPECTS ≤ 3 (common odds ratio, 1.56 [95% CI, 0.79-3.10]) although, nominally, the association between ASPECTS and EVT benefit was in the same direction between patients with ASPECTS ≤ 3 and ASPECTS 4 to 5. Conversely, the magnitude of treatment effect was similar in ASPECTS 0 to 2 and ASPECTS 3 to 5 groups in both LASTE (Trial of Thrombectomy for Stroke With a Large Infarct of Unrestricted Size) and TENSION (EVT for Acute Ischaemic Stroke With Established Large Infarct). 8,10 In addition, the TENSION study showed no significant difference in mean infarct volume at 24 hours between patients undergoing EVT and medical therapy. 8 Although RESCUE-Japan LIMIT suggested a possible treatment interaction with ASPECTS occurring at an inflection point of 3, this could not be confirmed by a prespecified subgroup analysis from LASTE, which included a far greater number of patients, indicating the benefit of EVT that is similar to that seen in patients with small-to-moderate-sized cores and is present regardless of infarct size in patients who could be treated within 7 hours.

In an ANGEL-ASPECTS (Trial of Endovascular Therapy for Acute Ischemic Stroke With Large Infarct) prespecified subanalysis categorizing patients into 3 groups defined by ASPECTS and core volumes, the treatment effect did not significantly differ across the subgroups. 59 In SELECT-2 (Trial of EVT for Large Ischemic Strokes), the treatment effect of EVT persisted across all prespecified imaging subgroups, encompassing all ASPECTS scores, core volumes of ≥ 150 mL, and notably also in cases lacking a target mismatch profile. Furthermore, no treatment effect heterogeneity was observed in patients with a large core estimated by CT perfusion ( ≥ 70 mL) and a low ASPECTS (<6). These findings suggest that the benefits of EVT may extend up to 24 hours from symptom onset in patient populations exclusively composed of those with low ASPECTS, individuals with low ASPECTS and large perfusion core volumes, and even in patients with very small areas of reversible ischemia as defined by CT perfusion (penumbra). Although the TESLA 9 trial showed a trend toward benefit with endovascular treatment, the study narrowly missed the probability threshold for statistical efficacy. Nonetheless, no treatment effect heterogeneity was observed between patients with ASPECTS 2 to 3 and ASPECTS 4 to 5, nor was there a significant decrease in the size of the infarct volume or difference in ASPECTS score at 24 hours between endovascularly treated and medical groups. Although, formally, no treatment effect heterogeneity could be demonstrated in early (0-6) versus late (6-24) hour presenting patients, nominal differences in response to EVT were noted in these 2 groups of patients.

Across these studies, the treatment effect appears to persist across all imaging subgroups except possibly in late-presenting patients with ASPECTS <3. This challenges the conventional idea that existing imaging constructs can delineate patients for whom EVT might be ineffective or harmful, questioning the fundamental basis of imaging-based selection. Individual studies were not powered to allow definitive conclusions from subgroup analyses and, therefore, must be interpreted with caution as we await the results of individual pooled metaanalysis data. Despite these limitations, several speculations can be drawn to support our hypothesis.

First, in LASTE where more than half of the patients had ASPECTS 0 to 2 (in most cases by MRI), there was no difference in treatment effect compared with patients with ASPECTS 3 to 5. Despite the expectation of near complete or complete MCA territory involvement in ASPECTS 0 to 2, suggesting less penumbral volumes than in patients with small- or moderate-sized cores, the treatment effect remained robust and in the same range as in all other patients with proximal anterior circulation large vessel occlusion (numbers needed to treat, 4). If the benefit of reperfusion was exclusively occurring due to penumbral salvage, as currently ascertained by imaging studies in routine clinical care and, thus, proportional to the volume of mismatch, one would expect lower treatment effects with increasing core as the mismatch volumes diminish with increasing core (Figure 4). Nonetheless, in LASTE, a difference in follow-up infarct volume was noted between EVT and control patients, suggesting that even in patients with large infarct volumes, some reversible ischemic tissue (by volume) is still present and can be salvaged with reperfusion. Secondly, however, TENSION and TESLA showed similar follow-up infarction volumes between endovascular and medically treated patients, along with similar follow-up ASPECTS scores in TESLA. This contradicts the anticipated variability in final infarction volumes between EVT and control groups if reperfusion benefits were solely from penumbral salvage. Finally, in SELECT-2, nearly half of the patients had low CTP estimated penumbral volumes (<15 mL), yet the clinical benefit persisted. This aligns with a previous MR CLEAN subanalysis indicating persistent treatment benefits in patients without target mismatch profiles. Despite statistical limitations in using trials not explicitly designed to answer this question, these studies raise the possibility that salvageable penumbral tissue may exist within the confines of our radiographically estimated core, and a benefit of reperfusion may exist irrespective of the presence of imaging-defined penumbra.

BRIDGING THE IMAGINGNEUROPATHOLOGICAL GAP

In conclusion, both animal and human studies suggest that current neuroimaging methodologies, utilizing quantitative ADC, ischemic lesion hypodensity on CT, or CTP-derived perfusion thresholds, are not fully reliable in excluding microscopically salvageable ischemic tissue confined within the radiographically defined margins of ischemic injury. As these modalities lack the spatial resolution to detect the cellular biomarkers of potential ischemia reversibility, their validity in quantifying infarction depends on radiological-histopathologic correlative studies. Unfortunately, the overemphasis of early animal studies on the volumetric and macroscopic histopathologic features of infarction, rather than microscopic and molecular ischemic changes, contributes to the existing imaging-neuropathological gap of ischemia. This limitation is now being revealed by emerging data from the large-core EVT trials that demonstrated a treatment benefit of reperfusion that may exist irrespective of infarction volume, underscoring the drawbacks of relying solely on the volume of infarction, neglecting the underlying cellular and metabolic characteristics of ischemia that may greatly influence treatment response and functional recovery. Furthermore, recent studies suggest that diffusion restriction during acute focal ischemia may primarily represent increased astroglia volume, a compensatory and protective mechanism to regulate osmotic balance and shelter shrunken neurons from immediate osmotic injury (Figure 2). 40 Insights into glial osmoregulation could hold prognostic significance alongside infarction volume in acute ischemia and encourages the exploration of glial-targeted neuroprotective agents. Indeed, these imaging constructs may

(at best) predict the margins of infarction but cannot quantify nor distinguish between incomplete and complete infarction.

Current definitions of core and penumbra are likely limited by their oversimplification. First, considering the differential ischemic vulnerabilities between cell types (Figure 1), relying solely on a single hemodynamic marker such as CBF or early ischemic changes on CT may only predict infarction occurrence without accurately assessing the presence of viable tissue at a microscopic level within the ischemic core. 60 Recent models propose a more nuanced representation of this heterogeneity, suggesting the existence of minicores interspersed among minipenumbras. 61 Second, the static approach reduces the dynamic and interwoven metabolic, molecular, and cellular processes unfolding during acute ischemia, impacting both neurons and supporting structures. These processes encompass disruptions such as impaired mitochondrial ATP production, ionic imbalance, blood-brain barrier breakdown, aberrant cross-talk between brain innate and peripheral adaptive immunities, leukocyte infiltration, aggregation of activated platelets, upregulation of proinflammatory mediators, tissue remodeling and angiogenesis-related to matrix metalloproteinases, complement activation, glutamate excitotoxicity, and impaired nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidative pathways, among others. 62,63 Given the prognostic relevance of both early and delayed pathological changes alongside infarct size, reperfusion therapy may not only salvage the targeted penumbra but also attenuate the secondary injury cascade. Specifically, it may mitigate harmful processes such as glial transformation and neuroinflammation, which extend beyond the core and penumbra into surrounding tissue. This is crucial as neuroinflammation is linked to poorer functional outcomes and is mechanistically associated with neurocognitive decline and neuropsychiatric disorders. 64,65 Therefore, current core and penumbral constructs must be viewed in a fluid manner as dynamic entities for which the metabolism and function of the underlying cellular and molecular machinery are in constant flux and, in practice, provide unreliable information about tissue fate following reperfusion (Figure 5).

Recent expert opinions at the 12th Stroke Treatment Academy Industry Roundtable highlighted the absence of a specific core and penumbral volume that could determine treatment response following EVT, emphasizing the need for quantitative definitions of infarction. 66 Shifting from static and binary definitions to dynamic and quantitative ones may be essential for narrowing the imaging-neuropathological gap and defining imaging constructs with treatment-modifying properties. This may entail a multiparametric approach, by combining other relevant imaging parameters, such as collaterals and clot composition, with current or future imaging constructs. While these imaging variables are prognostically relevant, their ability to predict treatment response (in isolation) remains largely undefined. 67 If imaging is to remain a major factor in prereperfusion selection, next-generation imaging constructs should be merited by their ability to predict treatment response rather than prognosticate, irrespective of their pathophysiological validity. 12

Given the demonstrated safety and efficacy of EVT in large-core trials, the precision of future imaging constructs must be balanced against acquisition time. This may require the creation of separate imaging constructs: fast and practical for prereperfusion versus precise and granular for postreperfusion analyses. For instance, automated quantification of net water uptake on noncontrast CT in the emergency department (or even directly in the neuroangiography suite) could quantify prereperfusion infarction without significantly delaying treatment, while diffusible radiotracers can possibly enhance the hemodynamic precision of perfusion outputs. 53,68 Postreperfusion imaging may assist in individualizing neuroprotection, neuromodulation, and blood pressure augmentation in efforts to optimize reperfusion. 69 This may require disentangling the core by creating fingerprints of neuronal and astroglial responses using diffusion tensor imaging (as performed in recent neuroimmunological models) to stratify the diffusion signal and quantify the proportion of ischemic cell death to that of compensatory ischemic mechanisms. 70 In parallel, translational animal models should emphasize dynamic, voxel-based experiments that are conducted using quantitative perfusion measures, such as positron emission tomography or positron emission tomography magnetic resonance imaging (MRI PET), to quantify the molecular and microscopic neuropathological changes that occur within the macroscopic boundaries of infarction.

Anticipation surrounds the results of future metaanalyses that might help define the relationship between our imaging constructs and reperfusion outcomes. If future meta-analyses align with individual trials, indicating that none of our imaging surrogates, in isolation or combination, can effectively identify patients for whom EVT is ineffective or harmful, this supports the aggressive simplification of care systems. This shift from imagingbased selectivity toward speed and inclusivity raises a critical dilemma: how do we reconcile existing systems of imaging-based selection while acknowledging the profound limitations of current imaging constructs? Is the simplification in patient selection justified not only by faster reperfusion but also by the evident imprecision in our imaging constructs?

Imaging-Neuropathological Gap in Stroke

Our current imaging constructs have been critical in guiding us toward the forest but may have blurred the diversity of trees. Optimizing reperfusion therapies beyond mere recanalization will likely require entering and exploring the terrains of this uncharted territory. As we step into the era of precision medicine, we must reimagine our constructs by zooming in on the cellular and rebuilding them from the first-principal level. This journey must also be met with a psychological shift, as we must reconcile our desires for precision with the exquisite time dependence of treatment and the immense difficulty in predicting the behavior of complex biological processes. Nevertheless, continued efforts to narrow the imaging-neuropathological gap are critical to ensure that our diagnostics keep up with our therapeutics, in that our imaging constructs evolve and remain in stride with our reperfusion therapies.
