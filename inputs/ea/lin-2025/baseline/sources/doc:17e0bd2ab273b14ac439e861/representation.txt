Keywords

Endovascular therapy; large core; stroke imaging; CT perfusion

The six randomized large core stroke trials reported or published between 2022 and 2024 have expanded the selection criteria for endovascular therapy (EVT) demonstrating that patients with lower Alberta Stroke Program Early CT (ASPECT) scores and larger CTperfusion (CTP) or diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) core volumes benefit from EVT [1-6]. There are, however, several patient subgroups that were underrepresented in the randomized trials, as well as heterogeneity in study designs and treatment responses. This brings into question which subgroups of patients with large cores benefit from EVT. Some trials focused on MRI-based selection (DWI-ASPECTS), others included patients who had both non-contrast CT (NCCT) and CTP, while others required only a NCCT and enrolled based on ASPECTS criteria (see Table 1). Some studies were restricted to early time windows, while others treated patients up to 24 hours after last know well. Subgroups with an uncertain response to EVT include CT ASPECT or DWI-ASPECT scores between 0-3 in in the early time window, large core volumes on CTP or MRI (especially in later treatment windows), and those with no perfusion imaging mismatch in any time window. Patients with baseline disability were not represented as a premorbid modified Rankin Score (mRS) ≤ 1 was required for all trials except TENSION, which allowed mRS 0-2. Those of age greater than 80 years were also under-represented as the mean ages of the patients enrolled was in the mid-late 60s in TESLA, SELECT2, and ANGEL-ASPECT and in the mid 70s in the other three studies.

MRI based studies in the early time window

Among the six trials, two (LASTE and RESCUE-Japan LIMIT) predominantly used MRI DWI-ASPECTS selection within the early time window but reached different conclusions [1,6]. In LASTE, DWI-ASPECT scores from 0 to 5 were included but also required the corresponding FLAIR to be negative if unknown onset time. While all DWI-ASPECTS subgroups regardless of volume appeared to benefit, volumes in LASTE were calculated from DWI b1000 data, rather than apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) calculated diffusion thresholds, which results in over-estimation of core volumes. In contrast to LASTE, RESCUE-Japan LIMIT demonstrated EVT benefit for DWI-ASPECTS 4 to 5, but not 0-3. In RESCUE-Japan LIMIT, DWI volumes were calculated post-hoc using an ADC threshold of 620 X 10 -6 mm 2 /s, which leads to smaller core volumes estimates compared to than those calculated from b1000 maps and more specifically identifies parenchyma unlikely reverse following reperfusion. The median core volume in the EVT group of RESCUE-Japan LIMIT was 94 ml, below the one-third of the middle cerebral artery territory (100 ml of 300 ml) threshold for the classical definition of large core size [7]. A pre-specified sub-analysis of RESCUE-Japan LIMIT demonstrated that the predictive marginal probabilities for achieving mRS 0-3 intersected at 128 ml, but there were only 8 patients randomized to EVT with core volumes between 100 and 128 and the potential for a non-linear relationship between core volume and benefit was not explored. Subgroup analysis of patients with core volumes ≥100 ml was also underpowered with only 40 patients randomized to EVT; the point estimate was non-significantly favorable for EVT [8].

In summary, LASTE did not demonstrate an upper core volume limit to EVT response while RESCUE-Japan LIMIT suggested a potential upper limit of about 100-125 ml. Because ASPECTS on MRI are typically lower than ASPECTS on NCCT given the greater sensitivity of DWI to early ischemia, these results cannot be directly compared to studies utilizing CT ASPECTS. Based on these results, we remain uncertain whether an upper limit to core volume or a lower limit to DWI-ASPECTS can be eliminated for EVT efficacy in patients in the early time window. This could be addressed in a clinical trial randomizing patients with core volumes greater than about 100-125 ml based on an ADC threshold and/or DWI-ASPECTS 0-3 patients.

CT based studies with longer time windows

Beyond six hours, there was a demonstrated EVT benefit with ASPECTS of 3 to 5 in TENSION (randomized up to 11 hours), and SELECT2, and ANGEL-ASPECT (randomized up to 24 hours). TESLA, which included patients with CT ASPECTS 2-5 randomized up to 24 hours, did not meet the prespecified efficacy endpoint (posterior probability of superiority of 97.5% and one-sided p-value of 0.025) but demonstrated a strong trend. A subgroup analysis suggests that treatment within 6 hours was beneficial whereas treatment after 6 hours was not. Thus, there are four studies suggesting that patients with ASPECTS 3 to 5 benefit in early time windows and two that demonstrate benefit up to 24 hours.

The two late window studies required a core volume assessment based on CTP (or rarely MRI) as well as ASPECTS. Both studies demonstrated that ASPECTS of 3 to 5 can have a wide range of core volumes. In addition, patients with the larger core volumes had a numerically lower treatment response. For example, in ANGEL-ASPECT, the odds ratio for a favorable mRS shift was 1.40 (95% confidence interval (CI) 1.06-1.85, P = 0.01) in patients with ASPECTS 3 to 5 and core volumes <70 ml but only 1.22 (95% CI 0.81-1.83, P = 0.23) in patients with ASPECTS 3 to 5 and core volumes of 70 ml or larger [9]. In the SELECT2 trial, the original publication suggested no relationship between core volume and treatment response; however, the core volumes reported represented a combination of either NCCT hypodensity volumes or CTP core volumes (see published correction [10]). When the treatment response was subsequently based on CTP core volumes alone and corrected for baseline imbalances, patients with core volumes of 100 ml or larger had a numerically lower odds ratio for 90-day mRS improvement than those with volumes less than 100 ml [11]. Few patients (N=21) with core volumes greater than 150 ml were enrolled in the EVT arm of SELECT2 (the median core volume in the thrombectomy group was 74 ml (IQR 50-111.5), and although the nominal direction of effect was toward a favorable treatment response, the confidence intervals were wide (0.84-2.56), leaving uncertainty. ANGEL-ASPECT had even fewer patients with a core over 150 ml (N=15, with only 2 randomized to EVT); the median core volume was 60.5 ml (IQR 29-86) in the thrombectomy group.

In both SELECT2 and ANGEL-ASPECT, more than 90% of the patients enrolled had a perfusion mismatch (91% in SELECT 2 and 93% in ANGEL-ASPECT). Therefore, there is uncertainty regarding the EVT response in perfusion mismatch patients who have very large (>100-150ml) core volumes.

Mismatch vs No mismatch

In contrast to the population of patients enrolled in the late window trials, cohort studies demonstrate that large core patients often present with the No mismatch profile in both early and later time windows. For example, in the FRAME study in which investigators were blinded to the results of perfusion imaging and patients were enrolled within 6 hours of stroke onset, 23 (50%) of the 46 patients with a core volume >70 ml had No mismatch [12]. As stroke onset time increases, the No mismatch profile typically becomes more common; about half of the late window patients with MRI ASPECTS 3-5 present with No mismatch (see Figure and [13]). Despite this known time-dependency of the mismatch profile, the late window patients enrolled in both ANGEL-ASPECT and SELECT2 virtually all had the Mismatch profile (see eFigure5 that appears in the supplement to reference 11). In both ANGEL-ASPECT and SELECT2, investigators were required to review both the CTP outputs, as well as the ASPECT score, before enrolling a patient. These data raise the question of whether investigators preferentially enrolled mismatch patients. Only 23 No mismatch patients were randomized to the EVT arms of the two trials that required perfusion imaging [2,3], and they showed no evidence of EVT treatment effect (see Table 2).

Therefore, there is uncertainty regarding the benefit of EVT in No mismatch patients regardless of time window or core size, yet mismatch status has independently predicted poor outcome following endovascular reperfusion in other cohorts [14]. It is likely that early window No mismatch patients were enrolled in some of the large core studies; however, no data that describe these patients have been published.

Conclusions

The large core trials support expanding the indications for thrombectomy to wider patient populations. However, translation of these results into clinical guidelines is challenging. The study results do not support the conclusion that EVT benefit has been established for unlimited core volumes. Additional studies are needed to clarify the outer limits of EVT safety and efficacy. Mismatch Patients with core lesion volumes greater than about 100-125 ml:

For early window patients (0-6 hours), there are conflicting data regarding whether all DWI-ASPECT scores and DWI volumes respond favorably. For early window patients selected by CT, current data support EVT efficacy with an ASPECT score of three or higher with limited data regarding responses based on large CTP core volumes.

In the later time window (>6-24 hours), the large core studies focused on either ASPECTS or CTP selection. Data from these studies suggest that patients with ASPECTS of 3 to 5 potentially respond differently based on the size of the ischemic core on CTP. Hence, additional data are desirable for mismatch patients with very high core volumes based on CTP or MRI in the late window. Uncertainty is particulary high with core volumes greater than 100-125 ml, as very few patients with core volumes above this range were randomized to EVT.

No mismatch patients with core lesion volume greater than about 70 ml:

For patients with No mismatch, there is uncertainty in both the early and late time windows because of a lack of data. . Patients who present with large core volumes in early time windows likely have extremely poor collaterals and may be at high risk for malignant reperfusion edema and worse outcomes. Additional data regarding both early and late presenting No mismatch patients are needed.

Patients over the age of 80 and/or with baseline disability.

Elderly patients were underrepresented in the large core studies and patients with baseline disability were excluded; these subgroups may respond differently to EVT.

Finally, core volume estimates are only one factor influencing treatment decisions. Better understanding of the role of additional factors in predicting treatment response would help us optimize patient selection. In addition, alternative neuroimaging approaches might better predict the response to EVT. For example, net water uptake has been identified as a predictor of malignant infarction and reperfusion related edema [15]. Measures of collateral status, such as the cerebral blood volume index, hypoperfusion intensity ratio, and venous outflow might also identify patients with a differential response to reperfusion therapies [16-19].

The ischemic core identified on DWI, CT perfusion and NCCT differs because these modalities are measuring fundamentally different pathophysiologic processes. The regions identified as 'core' by these imaging modalities are also heterogenous, likely related to the severity of ischemia within the lesion [20]. For example, recent data suggest a more stringent ADC threshold of < 520 X 10 -6 mm 2 /s was more accurate than higher thresholds for identification of irreversibly injury tissue in patients treated with mechanical thrombectomy [21]. In addition, using an all or none approach to ischemic core identification may not adequately identify treatment responses. Patients with substantial amounts of moderate ischemic injury are likely to benefit more from EVT than those with large amounts of severe ischemic injury on pretreatment imaging studies. There may also be a benefit to decoupling the established biological definition of 'core' from the various unique imaging biomarkers that likely discriminate parenchyma at varying stages of risk for injury. Future studies should consider incorporating novel imaging approaches and analyses to help refine patient selection and responses to treatment. Additional randomized data in underrepresented patient subgroups, along with novel approaches to imaging analyses, will help establish the boundaries for the efficacy and safety of EVT.
