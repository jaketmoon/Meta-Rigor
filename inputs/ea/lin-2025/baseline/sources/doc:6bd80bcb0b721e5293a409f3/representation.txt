Purpose of review

In this review, we summarize current evidence regarding potential benefits and limitations of using perfusion imaging to estimate presence and extent of irreversibly injured ischemic brain tissue ( ' core ' ) and severely ischemic yet salvageable tissue ( ' penumbra ' ) in acute stroke patients with large vessel occlusion (LVO).

Recent findings

Core and penumbra volumes are strong prognostic biomarkers in LVO patients. Greater benefits of both intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular therapy (EVT) are observed in patients with small core and large penumbra volumes. However, some current definitions of clinically relevant penumbra may be too restrictive and exclude patients who may benefit from reperfusion therapies. Alongside other clinical and radiological factors, penumbral imaging may enhance the discussion regarding the benefit/risk ratio of EVT in common clinical situations, such as patients with large core -- for whom EVT ' s benefit is established but associated with a high rate of severe disability -- , or patients with mild symptoms or medium vessel occlusions -- for whom EVT ' s benefit is currently unknown. Beyond penumbral evaluation, perfusion imaging is clinically relevant for optimizing patient ' s selection for neuroprotection trials.

Summary

In an emerging era of precision medicine, perfusion imaging is a valuable tool in LVO-related acute stroke.

Keywords

ischemic stroke, neuroprotection, penumbra, perfusion imaging, thrombectomy

INTRODUCTION

In acute ischemic stroke (AIS), therapeutic trials have shown that the benefit of reperfusion therapies -either intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy - decreases as time elapses [1,2]. This observation is mostly due to the progression of the irreversibly injured ischemic brain tissue ('core') over time, at the expense of the severely ischemic yet salvageable tissue ('penumbra'): as time elapses and unless reperfusion is obtained, the penumbra proceeds to core until it has disappeared [3-5]. Importantly, the velocity of core growth over time - and its underpinning penumbra decrease - is highly heterogeneous across individuals [5,6 & ,7 & ]. Some patients, manifest large core and minimal penumbra within a very short time, while others still have small core and substantial penumbra several hours after symptom onset. Mapping the ischemic core and penumbra in AIS patients may therefore be of clinical interest to identify the patients who may benefit from reperfusion therapies.

In this review, we will discuss potential benefits and limitations of core/penumbra imaging for acute stroke patients. We will focus on patients with large vessel occlusion (LVO), where the core/penumbra model has been primarily studied.

KEY POINTS

 Core and penumbra volumes are strong prognostic biomarkers in large vessel occlusion patients and may enhance the discussion regarding the benefit/risk ratio of thrombectomy in common clinical situations, alongside other factors.

Using core/penumbra imaging to select patients for reperfusion therapies in the early time window

The use of perfusion imaging is not required by international guidelines for intravenous thrombolysis decisions within 4.5 h from symptoms onset, and for thrombectomy decisions within 6 h [8,9], because perfusion imaging was not required for patient enrollment in most pivotal trials in the early time window. Nevertheless, perfusion imaging may bring additional prognostic and therapeutic information which can help to support individualized clinical decision making. Successful recanalization is now achieved in 85% to 90% of EVT-treated patients, yet half of these patients are still significantly disabled 3 months later, and many EVT procedures may not lead to meaningful improvement in clinical outcome. Large ischemic core volume (and the lack of substantial penumbra) at the time of recanalization is a major determinant of poor functional outcome, alongside other factors such as premorbid disability, cognitive impairment and post-EVT complications. Several cohort studies have recently confirmed that the lack of penumbra on pre-EVT imaging was independently associated with poor 3-month functional outcome, even in patients achieving fast successful reperfusion [10 & -13 & ]. Furthermore, a larger therapeutic effect has been observed in the pivotal early time-window EVT trials that used perfusion imaging for patient selection, compared with other trials [9], while two EVT prospective cohort studies reported that reperfusion was associated with a higher rate of functional recovery in patients with substantial penumbra but not among those without [11 & ,14]. Importantly, lack of substantial penumbra in the early time window is not rare, and has been observed in  30% of AIS-LVO patients [10 & ,11 & ,15].

However,  20% of patients without substantial penumbra still reach good functional outcome following EVT [10 & ,11 & ], fueling the debate on the risk of over-selection of EVT candidates using perfusion imaging, that is, improving outcome by selecting patients with substantial penumbra may be at the expense of excluding others who could still benefit from EVT. The potential benefit of EVT in patients without penumbra may have several explanations. First, the estimation of core or penumbral volumes on pretreatment imaging may be inaccurate in some patients due to the limited spatial resolution, but also technical or physiological issues, such as motion artifacts, poor contrast bolus injection, or low cardiac output. For instance, recent studies have suggested that core thresholds on CT-perfusion may be time-dependent, and stricter core thresholds -for instance CBF < 20% - should be used for patients imaged with CT-perfusion very early after symptom onset (e.g. within 90 min) to avoid core overestimation [16,17]. Similarly, some studies have outlined the occurrence of diffusion weighted-imaging hyperintensities reversal in 10-20% of patients following early complete reperfusion, mostly the regions with higher apparent diffusion coefficient values, suggesting that DWI hyperintensities include some penumbra [18,19]. The hyperintense DWI lesion likely contains tissue with variable degrees of injury, as suggested by its frequently observed patchy pattern, making manual core volume measurement difficult. Core estimation on DWImayrequire thresholding for increased reliability, using apparent diffusion coefficient [20]. Several software enable the generation of maps outlining the estimated core and penumbral brain regions using thresholded maps on MRI or CT-perfusion, allowing their use for urgent therapeutic decisions in clinical practice. However, substantial differences in core or penumbra volumes estimation across software have been pointed using either CT or MRI, highlighting the need for technique standardization in the future [21-24]. Second, the post-EVT reperfusion of even small penumbra volumes may still have clinical relevance depending on the 'eloquence' of the affected brain area. Two recent MRIbased studies showed that the penumbra located in eloquent brain regions was positively and independently associated with good functional outcome following EVT, while noneloquent penumbra was not [25 & ,26 & ]. The region-specific penumbra assessment may contribute to further refinement of AIS-LVO selection for EVT in the future. Last, clinical benefits of EVT over best medical management alone may be due to other parameters than penumbra salvage, such as prevention of vasogenic edema [27 & ,28].

Clinical decisions in medicine are complex, and there will always be a zone of uncertainty that has to be negotiated. Core and penumbral imaging are one of the numerous prognostic factors that can be integrated to enhance discussion with relatives regarding the expected outcome, and therefore influence the decision to proceed with of EVT.

Large core patients

Several randomized controlled trials have recently shown a substantial benefit in terms of 3-month functional outcome of EVT over best medical management alone in LVO-AIS with a large core, despite higher rates of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage [29,30 && ,31,32]. However, an independent ambulation at 3-month [modified Rankin score (MRS) 0 to 3] was reached in only 38% of patients treated with EVT in these trials, and functional independence (mRS 0 to 2) in 21% [33]. Considering such low rate of good outcome together with the costs of and burden involved by EVT procedures - in particular for patients referred from an outside hospital -, refining the selection of large core patients who may reach a good functional outcome following EVT is of importance, and core/perfusion imaging may play a role alongside other factors.

In a recent MRI-based observational study, roughly one in two patients with baseline core > 50mlshowednoremainingpenumbra[34 & ]. Interestingly, consistent with the core/penumbra model and with another study [34 & ], EVT was associated with a significantly higher rate of good functional outcome in the subgroup with substantial penumbra, but not in the subgroup without [35 & ]. Moreover, EVT was associated with higher odds of parenchymal hemorrhage regardless of the presence or extent of penumbra, which however did not outweigh the benefits from EVT in patients with penumbra [35 & ]. As consistent indirect evidence, a subgroup analysis from the RESCUE-Japan LIMIT trial showed that EVT was associated with improved 3-month functional outcomes in patients with diffusion-weighted imaging ASPECTS 4 to 5 - that is, where a substantial penumbra is expected -, but not in those with ASPECTS 3 or less - where minimal penumbra is usually observed [36 && ]. In contrast, however, there was no clear upper limit to the core volume in the SELECT2 trial associated with thrombectomy benefit [30 && ], whereas the recently presented LASTE trial showed improved 3-month functional outcomes following EVT in patients with ASPECTS 0-5 who were treated in the early window, with similar benefits in the 0-2 vs. 3-5 ASPECTS subgroups. A substantial reduction in infarct growth at 24 h was observed with EVT in LASTE, suggesting that many patients with a very large core may still have substantial penumbral tissue in the early time window. Individual patient data meta-analysis of large core trials with perfusion imaging should provide important data on the potential benefit of EVT

in patients with very large core and/or without salvageable penumbra, for example, via edema prevention.

Medium vessel occlusions

With advances in thrombectomy devices and techniques, the use of EVT is progressively expanding to medium vessel occlusions (MeVO), such as second or third segment middle cerebral artery occlusion. However, whether EVT is beneficial in this situation is unknown, considering the absence of randomized evidence. The benefit/risk ratio may be less favorable in this population -as compared to more proximal occlusionfor several reasons. First, MeVo usually shows better response to intravenous thrombolysis, due to smaller clots. Second, they usually present with milder stroke severity and smaller penumbral volumes. Last, their smaller vessel diameter and more tortuous shape may carry a higher risk of procedural complications. Nevertheless, it is highly plausible that MeVO patients with severe strokes and large penumbra may benefit for EVT, as recently suggested by a multicenter observational study [37 & ]. The authors retrospectively compared the clinical and safety outcome of AIS patients with M2 occlusion treated with medical management alone with ( n ¼ 195) or without ( n ¼ 322) additional EVT, using a propensity-score methodology to reduce baseline between-group differences [37 & ]. They found that thrombectomy was associated with better outcome in patients with substantial penumbra, but not in those without [37 & ], suggesting that penumbral imaging may be a therapeutic biomarker in AIS with MeVO. Several ongoing trials, mostly not requiring perfusion imaging as a selection criterion, compare EVT and best medical management alone in this population. The above results suggest that this design will inevitably result in merging patients with distinct underlying perfusion profiles predicting potential distinct responses to EVT.

Large vessel occlusion patients with mild symptoms

Likely owing to good leptomeningeal collaterals, a sizeable fraction of AIS LVO patients presents with only mild neurological deficit, commonly defined as a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score below 6. Whether EVT is safe and effective in LVO patients with mild stroke is currently debated since these patients were typically excluded from the pivotal EVT trials. Current observational studies suggest that in unselected mild stroke with LVO thrombectomy is not associated with better functional outcome, as compared with best medical management alone [38]. Moreover, EVT may be associated with a higher risk of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage in this population [38]. Interestingly, two recent observational studies have shown that, in this population, the presence of a substantial penumbra volume may modify the effect of EVT over best medical management alone [39 & ,40 & ]. Specifically, EVT was associated with better functional outcome in patients with substantial penumbra [40 & ], and was not - and was even associated with worse functional outcome and higher hemorrhagic risk -in those without [39 & ,40 & ]. In line with these reports, two additional studies have recently reported higher risk of early neurological deterioration or poor functional outcome in medically-treated mild strokes with LVO presenting with a large penumbral volume at baseline [41 & ,42 & ].

Using core/penumbra imaging to select patients for reperfusion therapies in the late time window

Since 2018, trials using 'penumbra' selection criteria (i.e. presence of penumbral profile or surrogates thereof) have demonstrated that patients with evidence of salvageable tissue on perfusion imaging benefit from EVT and intravenous thrombolysis even in the late time-window [43-45]. However, because of the very large treatment effect observed in these trials and the quite strict penumbral imaging criteria used, it has been hypothesized that patients presenting in the late window with broader imaging criteria could benefit from EVT. Recently, the MR CLEANLATEtrialshowedasubstantialbenefitofEVTvs.best medical management alone in AIS-LVO admitted in the 6-24 h time-window with collateral flow on CTangiography, but who otherwise did not meet the imaging criteria used in the DAWN and DEFUSE 3 trials [46 && ]. The therapeutic effect observed in MR CLEAN-LATE was lower than that observed in DEFUSE3andDAWN,whichsuggeststhat perfusion imaging identifies better candidates for reperfusion therapies, but current criteria may be too stringent and may miss patients who still derive substantial benefit from EVT. Current guidelines recommend using imaging to determine the presence of penumbral profile in patients who present in the late time window and who may benefit from EVT [8,9]. However, these recommendations may change based on the results of the recent MR CLEAN-LATE. Nevertheless, as discussed above for the early time window, theselectionofpatientsusingperfusionimagingmay still be valuable in certain situations, such as elderly patients or patients with baseline disability who were typically excluded from clinical trials.

The benefit of intravenous thrombolysis with alteplase vs. placebo in patients with substantial penumbra has been recently proven in the extended timewindow(4.5-9h)andforpatientswithwake-up strokes in an individual patient's data meta-analysis of randomized trials [adjusted odds ratio (OR) for mRS 0-1: 2.06, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.173.62] [45]. The rate of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage was higher with alteplase, but this increase did not negate the overall benefit of thrombolysis. Interestingly, no benefit of thrombolysis was observed in the group of patients without penumbra, again arguing in favor the therapeutic-modifying effect of penumbral imaging [45]. International guidelines now recommend perfusion imaging for thrombolysis decision in the late time window [47].

Additional interests of perfusion imaging beyond penumbral volume estimation

Identifying large or medium vessel occlusion

The identification of a large or medium arterial occlusion is critical for thrombectomy decision, primarily using CT- or MR-angiography. However, arterial occlusion can be missed on arterial imaging, especially when readings are performed in a very urgent setting, by less experienced readers, or when the affected vessel locates in the distal rather than the proximal intracranial circulation. Recent studies have suggested that perfusion imaging increases the indirect detection of large and medium vessel occlusion, and in turn the rate of EVT (Fig. 1) [48 & ,49 & ].

Identifying optimal candidates for neuroprotection trials

Limiting infarct core growth in the crucial timeperiod before recanalization has potential to substantially improve clinical outcome, and therefore is among the next major therapeutic frontiers [50,51]. To optimally select patients for trials of neuroprotection, it is important to identify those at high risk of large core growth, who may be most likely to benefit from these promising therapies (i.e. patients for whom a substantial reduction in core growth could be achieved with an effective agent). Patients first evaluated at a primary stroke center -which represent up to 70% of EVT-treated patients-, are likely ideal candidates for such therapies because substantial core growth is expected during the often protracted inter-hospital transfer. Recent studies have shown that leptomeningeal collateral circulation evaluated on perfusion imaging using the Hypoperfusion Intensity Ratio (HIR, defined as the proportion of T max > 6s over T max > 10s volume) was the primary factor associated with fast infarct growth, using either CT or MRI, and may therefore be a useful tool to select appropriate candidates for neuroprotection trials [7 & ,52 & ,53 & ,54]. This has been specifically studied in patients transferred for EVT from a primary to a comprehensive stroke center, with 'fast progressors' observed in  15% of patients with low HIR vs.  80% with high HIR [7 & ,53 & ]. Prediction of post-endovascular therapy intracerebral hemorrhage

The occurrence of post-EVT hemorrhagic transformationis known to negatively impact clinical recovery. Being able to predict the risk of post-EVT hemorrhagic transformation based on pretreatment data is of major importance for guiding individualized treatment strategies (e.g. to determine when additional fibrinolytic or antithrombotic drugs can be safely administered), as well as to select optimal candidates for clinical trials aiming to reduce hemorrhagic transformation [55]. Studies have recently shownthatthe evaluation of hypoperfusion severity or blood-brain barrier rupture on pretreatment perfusion imaging may help identify the subset of patients at very high risk of post-EVT hemorrhagic transformation [55-58].

CONCLUSION

Ischemic core and penumbra volumes are major prognostic factors in LVO-related AIS. Alongside other clinical and radiological factors, penumbral imaging may enhance the discussion regarding the benefit/risk ratio of EVT in many clinical situations, such as patients with very large core, mild symptoms, or distal occlusion. Beyond penumbral evaluation, perfusion imaging is relevant for optimizing patient selection for neuroprotection trials.
