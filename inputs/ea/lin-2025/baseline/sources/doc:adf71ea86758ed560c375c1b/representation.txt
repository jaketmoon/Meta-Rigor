Endovascular thrombectomy plus medical care versus medical care alone for large ischaemic stroke: 1-year outcomes of the SELECT2 trial

Summary

Background Multiple randomised trials have shown efficacy and safety of endovascular thrombectomy in patients with large ischaemic stroke. The aim of this study was to evaluate long-term (ie, at 1 year) evidence of benefit of thrombectomy for these patients.

Methods SELECT2 was a phase 3, open-label, international, randomised controlled trial with blinded endpoint assessment, conducted at 31 hospitals in the USA, Canada, Spain, Switzerland, Australia, and New Zealand. Patients aged 18-85 years with ischaemic stroke due to proximal occlusion of the internal carotid artery or of the first segment of the middle cerebral artery, showing large ischaemic core on non-contrast CT (Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomographic Score of 3-5 [range 0-10, with lower values indicating larger infarctions]) or measuring 50 mL or more on CT perfusion and MRI, were randomly assigned, within 24 h of ischaemic stroke onset, to thrombectomy plus medical care or to medical care alone. The primary outcome for this analysis was the ordinal modified Rankin Scale (range 0-6, with higher scores indicating greater disability) at 1-year follow-up in an intention-to-treat population. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03876457) and is completed.

Findings The trial was terminated early for efficacy at the 90-day follow-up after 352 patients had been randomly assigned (178 to thrombectomy and 174 to medical care only) between Oct 11, 2019, and Sept 9, 2022. Thrombectomy significantly improved the 1-year modified Rankin Scale score distribution versus medical care alone (WilcoxonMann-Whitney probability of superiority 0·59 [95% CI 0·53-0·64]; p=0·0019; generalised odds ratio 1·43 [95% CI 1·14-1·78]). At the 1-year follow-up, 77 (45%) of 170 patients receiving thrombectomy had died, compared with 83 (52%) of 159 patients receiving medical care only (1-year mortality relative risk 0·89 [95% CI 0·71-1·11]).

Interpretation In patients with ischaemic stroke due to a proximal occlusion and large core, thrombectomy plus medical care provided a significant functional outcome benefit compared with medical care alone at 1-year follow-up.

Introduction

Several randomised controlled trials have, in the past 2 years, established the efficacy and safety of endovascular thrombectomy for patients with large ischaemic stroke, reporting improved functional status at 3 months as the primary outcome. 1-6 However, long-term outcomes for these patients remain unknown, and whether thrombectomy has a long-term treatment benefit is still uncertain. Research in context

Evidence before this study

On Nov 15, 2023, we searched PubMed to identify randomised clinical trials published since database inception evaluating endovascular thrombectomy versus best medical care in patients with large ischaemic stroke, using the keywords ['Thrombectomy' OR 'EVT' OR 'Endovascular'] AND ['Large core' OR 'Large stroke' OR 'Large infarct' OR 'Low ASPECTS']. Four published trials were identified: the RESCUE Japan-LIMIT, the ANGEL-ASPECT, the SELECT2, and the TENSION trial, and all showed primary efficacy outcomes favouring thrombectomy. Additionally, two further trials (TESLA and INEXTREMIS LASTE) were identified, but are yet to publish their primary findings. All these trials used a 3-month window to obtain their clinical outcomes, but no long-term outcomes and treatment effect estimates were reported. Thus, in patients with large ischaemic stroke, whether thrombectomy treatment benefit is preserved in long-term follow-ups remains unknown.

Methods

Study design

SELECT2 was a prospective, open-label, phase 3, adaptive, multicentre, international, randomised controlled trial with blinded outcome assessment conducted at 31 hospitals across the USA, Canada, Spain, Switzerland, Australia, and New Zealand. 3 All participating hospitals obtained ethics approval from local institutional review boards or equivalent entities before start of patient enrolment. The study protocols and amendments are available in the appendix (pp 47-243). This trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03876457) and is completed.

Participants

The trial design and procedures, including detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria, have been reported previously. 3,10 Briefly, patients aged 18-85 years without pre-stroke disability (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score of 0-1) and an acute ischaemic stroke caused by occlusion of the internal carotid artery or of the first part (M1) of the middle cerebral artery (defined as the segment of middle cerebral artery proximal to the genu adjacent to the limen insulae) and a large ischaemic core on non-contrast CT (defined as an Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score [ASPECTS] of 3-5), CT perfusion imaging (defined as tissue volume with effect was preserved in patients with large ischaemic stroke up to 24 h from when they were last known to be well. Functional outcomes were improved and a higher proportion of patients achieved functional independence in the endovascular thrombectomy plus medical care group, compared with patients in the medical care only group. Furthermore, patients receiving thrombectomy had higher quality-of-life scores than their counterparts who received best medical care only. Treatment effect estimates favoured thrombectomy across subpopulations.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together with previous evidence, this analysis shows that endovascular thrombectomy improves clinical outcomes and quality-of-life scores in patients with large ischaemic stroke not only in the short term but also in the long term (at 1 year), and provides strong evidence to support the use of endovascular thrombectomy in patients with extensive ischaemic changes on CT or perfusion imaging.

Self-reported sex data were collected. All participants or their surrogates provided written informed consent for participating in the trial. Randomisation

A central web-based randomisation module used mini­ misation (covariate-adaptive randomisation) to assign values to each participant based on the observed characteristics and attempted to balance the weighted values across the treatment groups. Covariates used in the randomisation algorithm were age, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score at presentation (with scores ranging from 0 to 42 and higher scores indicating worse neurologic deficits), occlusion location, time window (the interval between the time that the patient was last known to be well and randomisation), ischaemic-core volume estimate, ASPECTS, presence or absence of target perfusion-diffusion mismatch profile (mismatch ratio [ie, the ratio of critically hypoperfused tissue to the ischaemic-core estimate] of ≥1·8 with a mismatch volume [ie, the volumetric difference between critically hypoperfused tissue and the ischaemic-core estimate] of ≥15 mL), affected brain hemisphere, and participating centre. No masking was used for this openlabel trial.

Procedures

Patients were randomly assigned to receive best medical care only versus endovascular thrombectomy plus best medical care in a 1:1 ratio. Endovascular thrombectomy was provided with stent retrievers, aspiration devices, or both. Anaesthesia, choice of access site, and intra-procedure and post-procedure management of haemodynamics were deferred to the local intervention team. Immediate stenting of carotid lesions was discouraged but deferred to the interventionalist's preference. All patients received best medical manage­ ment according to local institutional protocols based on American Heart Association and American Stroke Association, 11 European, 12 and Australian guidelines. 13 Hemicraniectomy was performed according to local protocols, after discussions with family and in accordance with the patients' wishes.

All patients received uniform imaging evaluation before randomisation, including non-contrast CT, CT and magnetic resonance angiography, and CT perfusion and magnetic resonance diffusion-perfusion imaging. All neuroimaging data acquired during index hospitalisation were collected and evaluated by a core imaging laboratory, including adjudication of CT ASPECTS and occlusion location. CT perfusion and magnetic resonance diffusion-perfusion imaging were processed first at each centre at the time of acquisition and reprocessed at the core laboratory for central review and final read with RAPID 5.1.1R2 (research version), with corrections for motion, bolus timing, or input and output function errors applied as deemed appropriate. Ischaemic core on CT perfusion was defined using a relative cerebral blood flow threshold of less than 30% and MR diffusion was defined by an apparent diffusion coefficient of less than 620 x 10 -⁶ m²/s. CT hypodensity volume was quantified by manual delineation on preprocedure non-contrast CT by three vascular neurologists (VY, FN, and BCVC) using an iterative review with ITKSNAP software. Composite core volume (defined as the larger of CT perfusion and MRI core and CT hypodensity volumes) was also calculated to better represent the full extent of ischaemic changes, given potential underestimation of ischaemic change by non-contrast CT in the early time window and potential underestimation by CT perfusion in the later time window, when clot migration and partial reperfusion could have occurred (appendix p 3).

Outcomes

Trained assessors masked to treatment and imaging findings collected outcomes by means of a standard questionnaire, 14 administered in person (preferred) or over the telephone if an in-person visit was not feasible. The primary endpoint of the trial, mRS score at 90 days, has been previously published. 3 In this Article, we report a prespecified analysis of the SELECT2 trial, 3,10 with 1-year functional outcome as the primary outcome. The functional outcome was measured by the mRS (an ordinal scoring system ranging from 0 to 6, with a higher score for higher disability [0 indicating no symptoms and 6 indicating death]). Scores 6 and 5 (bedridden with constant care required) were merged for analysis, to avoid considering a shift in score from 6 to 5 as a significant improvement in functional outcome status. Patients with a score of 5 (completely bedridden requiring constant care) and 6 (death) are both assigned utility value of 0 and are considered equivalent in terms of patient-reported quality-of-life scores. Secondary outcomes (all at 1 year of follow-up) were functional independence (mRS 0-2), independent ambulation (mRS 0-3), complete dependence or death (mRS 5-6), mortality, stroke recur­ rence (defined as new ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke after the index stroke), and domainspecific quality-of-life scores measured on the NeuroQOL scale.

Statistical analysis

A statistical analysis plan was created before the dataset lock for the 1-year outcome and was approved by the trial steering committee and the data and safety monitoring board (appendix p 215-43). The analyses were performed under the intention-to-treat principle, with patients classified based on the treatment group that they were randomly assigned to, regardless of the treatment received. Per-protocol and as-treated analyses were done as additional prespecified sensitivity analyses. Missing data were imputed by multiple imputations under the missing-at-random assumption, with additional sensitivity analyses to assess potential departure from missing-atrandom by means of pattern-mixture analysis. 15

The primary outcome was evaluated with the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, with ties equally split. The Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney measure of superiority with 95% CI was reported. Alternative measures such as generalised odds ratio (OR) with 95% CIs were also reported. Adjusted analyses were conducted by means of probabilistic index models. 16,17 Adjusted probabilistic index models estimate the odds of a random patient from the endovascular thrombectomy group having a better mRS outcome than a random patient from the medical care only group, adjusted for the differences in covariates between these two patients. For dichotomous secondary outcomes, we report relative risk and its corresponding 95% CI, obtained through Zou's modified Poisson regression 18 with robust SEs. For continuous secondary outcomes, median regression was used, and coefficients with 95% CIs were reported. Analyses adjusted for age, stroke severity, time from when the patient was last known to be well to randomisation, and CT ASPECTS, and ischaemic-core volumes were also reported. Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Between Oct 11, 2019, and Sept 9, 2022, 958 patients were screened for eligibility and 352 were enrolled in the trial. Median age was 66 years (IQR 58-75) in the endovascular thrombectomy plus medical care group and 67 years (58-75) in the medical care only group (table 1). 178 patients were randomly assigned to receive endovascular thrombectomy plus best medical care and 174 were randomly assigned to receive best medical care only. Two patients crossed over from the medical care only group and received thrombectomy. For the first crossover, the patient was in the angio suite waiting for randomisation. The coordinator randomisly assigned the patient, who was allocated to medical care only but there was a miscommunication with the interventionalist. For the second crossover, the patient's wife chose to continue with the trial but was insistent on proceeding with thrombectomy and the site investigators accepted the family wishes. Both crossovers occurred at the same site. The crossovers were reviewed by the steering committee and the data and safety monitoring board and appropriate corrective measures were put in place. Since then no crossovers have occurred. 11 patients had an ASPECTS of 6 or higher and an ischaemic-core estimate of less than 50 mL from core laboratory reading, and were excluded from the per-protocol analysis, along with two patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis outside of eligibility criteria and one patient with baseline disability (mRS>1).

Data for the primary outcome were available for 329 (93%) of 352 participants at 1-year follow-up (completed in October, 2023), compared with 348 (99%) of 352 participants at the 90-day follow-up (appendix p 5). 23 (7%) of 352 patients had missing data for the 1-year mRS outcome. The distribution of mRS scores at 1-year follow-up (figures 1, 2) favoured thrombectomy (median mRS score 5 [IQR 3-6] in the thrombectomy group vs 6 [4-6] in the medical care only group; Wilcoxon-MannWhitney probability of superiority 0·59 [95% CI 0·53-0·64]; p=0·0019; generalised OR 1·43 [95% CI 1·14-1·78]). The average risk difference between a randomly selected patient from the endovascular thrombectomy group and a randomly selected patient from the medical care only group was 0·18 (95% CI 0·07-0·28), with ties split equally between the two groups. The number needed to treat for one patient to see a 1-point or greater improvement in functional outcome on mRS score with endovascular thrombectomy at the 1-year follow-up was 6 (95% CI 4-15). 1-year follow-up, 45 (24%; 28 [28%] of 101 in the thrombectomy group and 17 [19%] of 88 in the medical care only group) showed improvement of 1 point or more in the mRS score (appendix p 26). Of these, 15 patients (11 in the thrombectomy group and four in the medical care only group) had an mRS score of 3-5 at 90-day followup and recorded an mRS score of 0-2 at 1-year follow-up. A post-hoc illustration of baseline characteristics based on patients' transition between 90-day and 1-year follow-up is provided in the appendix (p 7).

Quality of life was higher in patients randomly assigned to receive thrombectomy (table 2; appendix pp 13-15). The missingness of Neuro-QOL outcomes was associated with treatment allocation and baseline covariates (appendix p 18). Nine patients withdrew from the trial, 14 patients could not be reach at 1-year follow-up, 160 patients died, and 58 additional patients did not answer questions for one or more NeuroQoL domains (appendix p 18). However, additional post-hoc sensitivity analyses also showed similar results (appendix p 20). In 168 patients with recorded information, stroke recurrence was similar between treatment groups (appendix p 21). However, systematic missingness of stroke recurrence could not be ruled out (appendix p 22).

We did not find any evidence of significant difference in thrombectomy treatment effect based on age, stroke severity, time from last known to have been well to randomisation, ASPECTS score, core volume estimates, and mismatch status, although patients enrolled at non-US sites had lower point estimates of treatment effect than those enrolled at US sites (figure 3; appendix pp 29-31).

Of 17 patients with documented dissection or perfor­ ation, 15 had 1-year outcomes available, with three (20%) achieving functional independence (mRS 0-2) and four (27%) achieving independent ambulation (mRS 0-3; post-hoc analysis). Evolution of infarction or cerebral oedema with midline shift was the most common serious adverse event recorded up to the 90-day follow-up visit (table 3).

Discussion

When followed-up for 1 year after the index stroke, patients randomly assigned to receive endovascular throm­ bectomy plus medical care showed significantly better functional outcomes than did patients randomly assigned to receive medical care only. No evidence of a significant difference in thrombectomy treatment effect was seen across prespecified subgroups on the basis of various clinical and imaging characteristics. Results were similar regardless of the choice of core volume measures. The proportion of patients reaching functional independence and independent ambulation was higher in the endovascular thrombectomy group. Patients randomly assigned to receive thrombectomy also showed higher quality-of-life scores at 1-year follow-up. All-cause mortality was non-significantly lower with thrombectomy than with medical care only. To our knowledge, SELECT2 is the first trial showing a 1-year benefit of thrombectomy in patients with large ischaemic stroke. The results complement the secondary long-term outcome analyses of the MR CLEAN 19 and REVASCAT trials, 20 which predominantly enrolled patients with a limited extent of ischaemic change on pretreatment neuroimaging, before the era of large core trials.

The proportion of patients treated with thrombectomy who had independent ambulation was similar at 1 year compared with 90 days (data not shown; appendix p 26). Rates of functional independence (mRS 0-2) and freedom from disability (mRS 0-1) were numerically higher in patients treated with thrombectomy at 1-year follow-up. These results show that the proportion of patients who regain independence is sustained at least up to 1 year. As expected, given the severity of illness, a substantial proportion of patients had died by the 1-year follow-up. However, the proportion of patients requiring continuous nursing care (mRS 5) remained lower in patients receiving thrombectomy (8%) than in those receiving medical care only (14%), and of similar magnitude to the proportion of mRS 5 outcomes after endovascular thrombectomy at 90 days (8%).

Recovery of patients with small core stroke tends to occur faster than for those with large core strokes, 3 with a substantial proportion of patients showing neurological improvement as early as within the first 24 h after stroke onset. 7 In our study, more than a quarter of the patients with continued follow-up beyond 3 months showed at least a 1-point improvement in functional status on the mRS at 1 year. These findings provide evidence of continued improvement beyond the 3-month window. Additionally, quality-of-life scores were better with thrombectomy at 1-year follow-up, suggesting not only long-term functional but also long-term social benefits of thrombectomy.

Older age is usually associated with worse clinical outcomes after stroke. In our study, a lower proportion of patients aged 70 years and older had functional independence (13% vs 29%) and independent ambulation (24% vs 44%), when compared with patients younger than 70 years after endovascular thrombectomy (appendix pp 29-30), but treatment effect estimates still favoured thrombectomy. A noteworthy exception is for patients enrolled at non-US sites compared those enrolled at US sites. However, given that only 72 (20%) of 352 were enrolled at non-US sites, further analyses of pooled data from this trial and other randomised trials in independent patient-level data meta-analyses would be necessary to provide definitive evidence of this potential treatment heterogeneity.

Our study has several limitations. The proportion of patients with missing primary outcome data (1-year functional outcomes) in our trial (7%) was higher than in the REVASCAT study, 20 but lower than in the MR CLEAN trial. 19 However, the thrombectomy benefit was consistent regardless of different missing data patterns under a pattern-mixture model and in per-protocol and as-treated analyses. The trial was not powered to evaluate treatment effect differences across subgroups. Neuro-QOL scores were also missing for a considerable number of patients. However, additional post-hoc sensitivity analyses supported higher quality-of-life scores after thrombectomy. Data related to recurrent strokes were missing for approximately 168 (50%) patients, primarily due to the absence of these data from patients who had died. Adverse event monitoring was completed only up to 90-day followup and, as such, data related to adverse events other than stroke recurrence were not collected. We also did not collect data on the cause of mortality beyond 90 days or the duration and type of rehabilitation received by individual patients.

In conclusion, the benefit of endovascular throm­ bectomy compared with medical care only in patients with large ischaemic stroke was maintained at 1-year follow-up. Patients randomly assigned to receive endovascular thrombectomy showed higher functional independence, independent ambulation, and lower mortality at 1 year than did those receiving medical care only. These findings provide evidence of a durable benefit of endovascular thrombectomy in patients with strokes with a large core, and show that a higher quality of life for these patients persists for at least 1 year after intervention.
