| Li et al. (2000) 26 | 30 min | 12 h | Rats (n = 14) | • Secondary ADC reduction, starting as early as 2.5 hours post-reperfusion and evolving gradually, is linked with neuronal injury, while the renormalization and subsequent decline in ADC do not correlate with neurological recovery or deterioration. • There was >50% neuronal necrosis in the caudate, while the cortex had <10% neuronal necrosis, despite similar ADC values. • After 30 minutes of MCAO, ADC values initially recovered within 1 hour of reperfusion but began to decrease again at 2.5 hours, with this secondary decline accompanied by increased T2 values and neuronal damage. • Most rats showed complete resolution of neurological deficits between 2.5 and 6 hours after reperfusion, with no secondary neurological deficits observed at 24 hours, despite the secondary ADC decline. • Compared with the initial ADC decline during occlusion, this secondary ADC decline occurred in a much slower fashion both temporally and spatially. For example, it took about 10 hours for the magnitude of the ADC values during the secondary ADC decline to reach almost the same values as occurred initially, and the extent of secondary ADC decline that developed in 10 hours was just half of that which occurred initially over 30 minutes. • The more severe the secondary ADC decline, the more severe the neuronal injury, indicating that ADC-defined secondary lesions are likely in vivo signs representing postischemic secondary injury. • Only 1 of the 8 rats had complete resolution of neurological deficits between 1 to 2 hours after reperfusion with ADC values renormalized, suggesting that ADC renormalization does not correlate with full neuronal recovery, while 6 rats showed complete neurological recovery between 2.5 to 6 hours despite an ongoing secondary ADC decline, indicating that this secondary decline does not necessarily correlate with clinical functioning. |

These findings suggest that ADC changes do not reliably reflect the underlying cellular condition post-ischemia. |
