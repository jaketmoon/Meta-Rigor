Thrombectomy for Stroke With Large Infarct on Noncontrast CT The TESLA Randomized Clinical Trial

The Writing Committee for the TESLA Investigators

IMPORTANCE Recent large infarct thrombectomy trials used heterogeneous imaging modalities and time windows for patient selection. Noncontrast computed tomographic (CT) scan is the most common stroke imaging approach. It remains uncertain whether thrombectomy is effective for patients with large infarcts identified using noncontrast CT alone within 24 hours of stroke onset.

OBJECTIVE To evaluate the effect of thrombectomy in patients with a large infarct on a noncontrast CT scan within 24 hours of onset.

DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS Open-label, blinded-end point, bayesian-adaptive randomized trial with interim analyses for early stopping (futility or success) or population enrichment, which was conducted at 47 US academic and community-based stroke thrombectomycenters. Three hundred patients presenting within 24 hours with anterior-circulation, large-vessel occlusion and large infarct on noncontrast CT scan, with Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Scores of 2 to 5, were randomized to undergo thrombectomy or usual care. Enrollment occurred July 16, 2019 to October 17, 2022; final follow-up, January 25, 2023.

INTERVENTION The intervention patients (n = 152) underwent endovascular treatment using standard thrombectomy devices and usual medical care. Control patients (n = 148) underwent usual medical care alone.

MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES The primary efficacy end point was improvement in 90-day functional outcome measured using mean utility-weighted modified Rankin Scale (UW-mRS) scores (range, 0 [death or severe disability] to 10 [no symptoms]; minimum clinically important difference, 0.3). A bayesian model determined the posterior probability that the intervention would be superior to usual care; statistical significance was a 1-sided posterior probability of .975 or more. The primary adverse event end point was 90-day mortality; secondary adverse event end points included symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage and radiographic intracranial hemorrhage.

RESULTS The trial enrolled 300 patients (152 intervention, 148 control; 138 females [46%]; median age, 67 years), without early stopping or enrichment; 297 patients completed the 90-day follow-up. The mean (SD) 90-day UW-mRS score was 2.93 (3.39) for the intervention group vs 2.27 (2.98) for the control group with an adjusted difference of 0.63 (95% credible interval [CrI], -0.09 to 1.34; posterior probability for superiority of thrombectomy, .96). The 90-day mortality was similar between groups: 35.3% (53 of 150) for the intervention group vs 33.3% (49 of 147) for the control group. Six of 151 patients (4.0%) in the intervention group and 2 of 149 (1.3%) in the control group experienced 24-hour symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. Fourteen patients of 148 (9.5%) in the intervention group vs 4 of 146 (2.7%) in the control group experienced parenchymal hematoma type 1 hemorrhages; 14 (9.5%) in the intervention group vs 5 (3.4%) in the control group experienced parenchymal hematoma type 2 hemorrhages; and 24 (16.2%) in the intervention group vs 9 (6.2%) in the control group experienced subarachnoid hemorrhages.

CONCLUSIONS AND RELEVANCE Amongpatients with a large infarct on noncontrast CT within 24 hours, thrombectomy did not demonstrate improvement in functional outcomes. But the width of the credible interval around the effect estimate includes the possibility of both no important effect and a clinically relevant benefit, so the potential role of thrombectomy with this imaging approach and time window will likely require additional study.

Clinical uncertainty regarding the benefit of thrombectomy for patients with large infarcts has been based on the presumed reduced potential for functional recovery and increasedhemorrhagerisk. 1-4 Threerandomizedtrialshavedemonstratedtheefficacyofthrombectomyforpatientswithlarge infarcts identified by advanced imaging using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomographic (CT) perfusionimaging,eitherprimarilyorincombinationwithnoncontrast CT. 5-7

Theavailablestrokeimagingapproaches(noncontrastCT, CT perfusion, and MRI) have well-recognized differences in sensitivity and specificity of infarct detection. Noncontrast CT has lower sensitivity than CT perfusion and MRI but higher specificity than CT perfusion and similar specificity to MRI, with1studyreportingapproximately70%sensitivityand100% specificity within the first 6 hours of onset. 8 Previous studies havedemonstratedthatupto40%to50%ofpatientswithlarge infarcts detected by MRI and CT-perfusion imaging have small infarctsonconcurrentnoncontrastCT. 9,10 Consequently,populations whose large infarcts have been imaged using different modalities are distinct.

NoncontrastheadCTisthemostwidelyusedimagingtechniqueforstrokeandisavailableatallstrokecenters.Inthepivotal trials, infarct evaluation was performed using noncontrastCTfor80%ofpatients. 11 Forthisreason,currentguidelines advocate noncontrast CT to identify patients presenting with strokewithin6hourswithsmallinfarcts,definedbytheAlberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) between 6 and 10 (0 to 10 scale, with lower scores indicating larger infarcts). 12 Recently, a randomized trial has shown thrombectomy benefit for patients with ASPECTS between 3 to 5 based mostly (82%) on noncontrast CT and within 12 hours of onset. 13

This trial aimed to evaluate the effect of endovascular thrombectomywithmedicalcarevsmedicalcarealoneamong patients with anterior-circulation, large-vessel occlusion, and large infarct (ASPECTS,2-5)onnoncontrastCTonlyandwithin 24 hours from stroke onset. This pragmatic trial was designed to optimally identify the large infarct population excluded from the pivotal trials and provide evidence generalizable to routine clinical practice.

The study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Study Population

Eligible patients were aged 18 to 85 years, who presented with acute stroke symptoms within 24 hours of symptom onset or when they were last known well, had a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 6 or higher (range, 0 to 42;higherscoresindicategreaterneurologicaldeficit),hadintracranial internal carotid artery or middle cerebral artery M1-segment occlusion on CT angiography, and had excellent prestroke functional status defined as modified Rankin Scale (mRS)scoreof0to1(range,0to6;higherscoresindicategreater disability). Eligible patients had a large infarct on noncontrast CT, defined as ASPECTS 2 to 5. ASPECTS is a topographic map dividing the middle cerebral artery territory into 10 regions, with 1 point deducted for each region with acute ischemic changes. 15 ASPECTS was determined by local site investigators trained and certified in its scoring (details in Supplement 2). Intravenous alteplase, if indicated, had to be administered within 3 hours of onset per US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance.

Exclusion criteria included presence of intracranial hemorrhage;masseffectwithventriculareffacement,midlineshift, orherniation;andpresenceoftandemcervicalinternalcarotid artery occlusion. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the trial protocol (Supplement 1).

Race and ethnicity data were collected to assess the representativeness of the study population. Data were selected fromfixed categories based on self-report from study participants or their families.

Randomization

Treating-physician investigators assessed eligibility and randomly assigned patients, in a 1:1 ratio, to either endovascular thrombectomy or no endovascular thrombectomy, in addition to usual medical care. Patients were assigned to study groupsusinganinternet-based,stratified-randomizationprocedure with permuted-block technique with randomly selected block sizes of 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12. Randomization was stratified for age (≤70 or >70 years), baseline ASPECTS (2 to 3 or 4 to 5), baseline NIHSS score (≤16 or >16), and time from stroke onset to imaging (0-6 hours or >6-24 hours; Figure 1 ).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed according to the prespecified statistical analysis plan. With 150 patients per group and assumed utility-weighted effect size of 1.25 for improvement in the 90-day UW-mRS score with the intervention (based on anassumedoddsratioof2.0forimprovementin90-daymRS score), the power was estimated to be 90% under a 1-sided α of .025. The sample size was determined based on trial simulations (5000 to 50 000 runs) in each of a variety of scenarios, as described in the statistical analysis plan (Supplement1);mRSscoredistributionsintheinterventionandcontrol groups were based on patients who had a low ASPECTS in the MRCLEANtrialandregistry. 25,26 Theadaptivetrialdesignpermitted a sample size of between 150 to 300 patients. Interim analyses were performed at 150 and 225 patients randomized. At each interim analysis, based on the protocol's prespecified rules, the trial could stop enrollment for futility or expectedsuccessoritcouldenrichthepopulationbyrestricting future randomization to patients with ASPECTS of 4 to 5. The possibility of removing patients with scores of 2 to 3 was based on their presumed lower likelihood of benefit. No allowance was made for population enrichment based on the timewindowtorandomization(0-6hoursvs>6-24hours).The futility rule was binding. Control of type I error was determined through simulation. Full details are in the statistical analysis plan (Supplement 1).

In the primary efficacy analysis, patients were analyzed according to their randomization group. The primary analysis used a bayesian statistical model adjusting for baseline ASPECTStodeterminetheposteriorprobabilitythatendovascularthrombectomyplususualmedicalcareissuperiortousual medical care alone with respect to the mean 90-day UW-mRS score. A posterior probability of at least .975 (analogous to 1-sided P value of 1 minus .975, or .025) was required to declare a statistically significant benefit from thrombectomy. A 1-sided hypothesis was used because we were only interested in a treatment benefit from thrombectomy relative to medicalcarealone.Theanalysispopulationwasthefullpopulation (all 300 randomized patients with ASPECTS of 2-5) unless the trial had enriched to ASPECTS of 4 to 5, in which case the analysis population would be patients with ASPECTS of 4 to 5 only. For patients with missing 90-day primary end point data, bayesian multiple imputation was used to impute the missing data based on baseline covariates and observed 30day mRS scores. There were no patients missing both the 30- and 90-day mRS scores.

Secondaryanalyseswereperformedusingfrequentisttests in the primary efficacy analysis population, with no imputationsofmissingdata.Continuousendpointsweretestedusing a t test assuming unequal variances. Ordinal end points were analyzed using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Binary end points were evaluated using a χ 2 or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Like the primary analysis, a 1-sided hypothesis was used for the secondary analyses because we were only interested in a treatmentbenefitfromthrombectomyrelativetomedicalcare alone. Adverse events analyses were performed in the astreated population, where crossover patients were analyzed based on the treatment received, and endovascular treatment was defined as undergoing arterial puncture. Sensitivity analyses were performed to repeat the main analyses in the per-protocolpopulation,definedaspatientswithoutmajorprotocol deviations, and in the primary efficacy analysis population with core laboratory-determined ASPECTS 2 to 5.

Treatment effect on the UW-mRS score was analyzed in prespecified subgroups based on age, sex, ASPECTS, NIHSS score,occlusionlocation,intravenousalteplasetreatment,time from stroke onset to randomization, COVID-19 status, firstline thrombectomy approach, and presence of CT perfusion mismatch.Eachsubgroupanalysisaddedasubgroupeffectand anarm-subgroupinteractionparametertotheprimaryanalysis model. A significant interaction was defined as a bayesian posterior probability of greater than .975 or less than .025, that the interaction parameter was positive.

Aprespecified site poolability analysis was performed in the primary efficacy analysis population to assess whether there was heterogeneity in site treatment effects. This analysis was performed by fitting a general linear model for the weightedmRSoutcome,withcovariatesforthetreatmentarm, site, and the interaction of treatment arm and site. Sites with fewer than 10 patients were combined and treated as a single site when examining the pooling. A P value less than .025 for the interaction term was considered indication of a difference in site treatment effects.

Analysesofadverseeventsandprespecifiedinterimanalyseswereperformedduringthetrialbythedataandsafetymonitoringboard.Therewerenoadverseeventconcernsduringthe trial, and all investigators were masked to results of the interim analyses.

A post hoc analysis was performed in the primary efficacy analysis population to evaluate between-group differencesin90-dayscorerangesofmRS0to2andmRS0to3and mortality in the early (0-6 hours) vs late (>6-24 hours) windows based on time to randomization.

P values and 95% CIs of effect estimates in the secondary, adverse events, and subgroup analyses were not corrected for multiplicity and should be interpreted as exploratory or hypothesis generating in nature. Reported P values are 1-sided. The prespecified statistical significance threshold for exploratory testing of secondary end points was a 1-sided P value <.025 (equivalent to a 2-sided P value <.05). Efficacy analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.2 for bayesian analyses and version 4.0.3 for frequentist analyses). Adverse events analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

BetweenJuly16,2019,andOctober17,2022,302patientsunderwent randomization at 44 centers (eFigures 1 and 2 in Supplement 2). At each interim analysis, the trial continued without enrichment. Two patients were withdrawn from the study. One patient's representative withdrew consent immediately after notification of being randomized to the control group. In the second case, consent was obtained from the wrongpatientwhosharedthesamelastnameanddateofbirth as the patient who was randomized; this patient was withdrawn immediately.

Of the remaining 300 patients (primary efficacy analysis population), 152 (50.7%) were assigned to the intervention groupand148(49.3%)tothecontrolgroup.Onepatientinthe control group received endovascular thrombectomy. Two patients in the intervention group did not receive endovascular treatmentbecausetheangiographysuitewasnotavailablefor one and the other had undergone spontaneous recanalization. Two patients, 1 in each group, were lost to 90-day followup; 30-day mRS scores were used to impute the missing data. Onepatientinthecontrolgroupwithdrewconsentbeforeday 6andwasexcludedfromprimaryandsecondaryanalyses.The primary outcome was evaluated in 299 of 300 primary efficacy analysis patients (Figure 1; eFigure 3 in Supplement 2).

Baseline characteristics were mostly similar between groups, except a greater proportion of patients in the intervention group had diabetes ( Table 1 and eTable 1 in Supplement 2). The median age was 66 years (IQR, 54-74 years) in the intervention group and 67.5 years (IQR, 57.5-73.5 years) in the control group. Seventy-six patients (50.0%) in the intervention group and 84 (56.8%) in the control group were male. Median time from stroke onset to randomization was 652.5 minutes (IQR, 333-942 minutes) in the intervention group and 754 minutes (IQR, 337-1027 minutes) in the control group. Endovascular treatment details are provided in eTable 2 in Supplement 2.

Primary Outcome

In the primary efficacy analysis population, the primary end point of the mean (SD) 90-day UW-mRS score was 2.93 (3.39) in the intervention group vs 2.27 (2.98) in the control group (adjusted difference, 0.63 points; 95% credible interval [CrI], -0.09 to 1.34 points; posterior probability of superiority, .96) ( Table 2 ; eTable 3 in Supplement 2).

Adverse Event Outcomes

In the as-treated adverse events population, there was no significant between-group difference in 90-day all-cause mortality (35.3% [53 of 150] in the intervention group vs 33.3% [49 of 147] in the control group; absolute difference, 2.0%; 95% CI, -8.8%to13%)norintheratesofsymptomaticintracranialhemorrhage at 24 hours, neurological deterioration within 6 days, or decompressive hemicraniectomy ( Table 3 ). Endovascular thrombectomy resulted in higher rates of parenchymal hematomas and subarachnoid hemorrhage. Endovascular procedure complications in the intervention group included 5 patients (3.3%) with emboli to new territory, 5 (3.3%) with arterial perforation, and 2 (1.3%) with arterial dissection. Overall, 9 patients (6%) had a procedural complication; 1 patient had all 3 complicationtypes,andanotherhadarterialperforationanddissection (eTable 4 in Supplement 2).

A summary of all site-reported adverse events is provided in eTables 5 and 6 in Supplement 2. There were 334 serious adverse events in total, with 175 occurring in the intervention group and 159 occurring in the control group. Nine serious adverse events were related to the thrombectomy procedure. One hundred patients in each group experienced at least 1 serious adverse event.

Theseresultsapplytothepopulationofpatientswithlarge infarcts that were identified using noncontrast CT alone within 24 hours from stroke onset. Given the widespread availability and routine use of noncontrast CT in stroke evaluation, the study findings are generalizable to most if not all stroke centers performing stroke thrombectomy, including community hospitals. The TENSION trial also used mostly (82%) noncontrast CTscansforpatientselection.However,theTENSIONtrial limited enrollment to randomization within 11 hours, whereas almost half of the patients in the current study were enrolled in the 12- to 24-hour window (median time to randomization, ≈ 11.5 hours). As such, these results are further generalizable to areas where stroke thrombectomy access is delayed and not feasible within earlier time windows. Determination of ASPECTSeligibility by local site investigators and broad enrollment across 94% (44 of 47) of participating sites also support the generalizability of the results.

Despiteasimilarimagingselectionapproachandasmaller sample size (253 patients) than the 300 patients in the currenttrial, the TENSIONtrialdemonstratedsignificantimprovements in ordinal mRS scores and dichotomized mRS score ranges of 0 to 2 (15% absolute difference) and 0 to 3 (18% absolute difference) and had significantly lower mortality (11% absolute difference) at 90 days. 13 The main differences in selection criteria between the trials were that the current trial included patients with an ASPECTS of 2 and allowed for a longer time window. The rates of ASPECTS that ranged from 0 to 2asdeterminedbytherespectivecorelaboratoriesweresimilar (15.1% in TENSION vs 17.1% in this trial). When comparing timetorandomization,160patients(63%)intheTENSIONtrial vs 82 patients (27%) in the current trial were in the earlyenrollment window, a marked proportional difference. In the 0- to 6-hour window, patients undergoing thrombectomy in the current trial had a 22% higher rate of having an mRS score range of 0 to 2, a 21% higher rate of an mRS score range of 0 to 3, and a point estimate that was not statistically significant but was suggestive of 17% lower mortality than the patients receiving medical therapy alone, results that appear similar to those of the TENSION trial. As such, the approximately 2-fold difference in early-window enrollment might explain the discrepancy in the trial results.

Although these results did not show a significant treatment effect modification by early (0-6 hours) vs late (>6-24 hours)windowpresentation,thepointestimateofbenefitwas reducedinthelatewindowandapproachedtheminimumclinically important difference (0.3). 24 This contrasts with results from the ANGEL ASPECTS, SELECT 2, and TENSION trials, wherepointestimatesweresimilarbetweenearlyandlatewindows. One factor that might influence thrombectomy benefit beyond 6 hours is vasogenic edema. Recent work has shown

jama.com that large infarcts with greater edema are less likely to benefit from endovascular reperfusion. 27 Because edema develops over time and the current trial had the longest times to randomizationandenrolledthehighestproportionofpatientsduring the 12- to 24-hour window ( ≈ 70% of patients in the late window vs ≈ 25% for ANGEL ASPECTS and ≈ 55% for SELECT 2), it is possible that a higher proportion of patients in the late window for this trial had more pronounced infarct edema. An imaging analysis of the pooled late window, large core patients should investigate this idea.

Theseresultsshowasimilartreatmenteffectbetweenthe ASPECTS2to3and4to5groups,withpointestimatesof0.57 and 0.65, respectively, analogous to ANGEL ASPECTS and SELECT 2. 6,7 However, a RESCUE-Japan LIMIT secondary analysis showed no benefit of thrombectomy among patients with ASPECTS 3 or less. 28 Ameta-analysis of the large infarct trials may clarify treatment effect at individual ASPECTS levels based on imaging modality.

Inapproximately85%ofpatientsinthistrial,thecorelaboratory agreed with the site investigators on ASPECTS between 2 and 5 (eTable 6 in Supplement 2). Therefore, at the level of study eligibility, scoring agreement was good. This tolerance range is acceptable considering the similar treatment effect observed between ASPECTS 2 to 3 and 4 to 5 patients. The good ASPECTS agreement, despite expected heterogeneity from manyenrollinginvestigators, likely reflects the rigorous ASPECTS training in the study. Interobserver reliability is likely worse in a real-world setting, which may limit the generalizability of the results. However, the online training software in this trial was also used in the TENSION study, supporting the generalizability of this training approach across a large numberofinvestigators,invaryinghospitalsettings(academic and community) and countries.

There were no concerns that there might be more major adverse events related to endovascular treatment. Mortality at 90 days and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhages were similar between groups, despite higher rates of parenchymal hematoma and subarachnoid hemorrhage with thrombectomy. Similar findings were reported in the ANGEL ASPECTS andRESCUE-JapanLIMITstudies,inwhichtherewerehigher rates of radiographic intracranial hemorrhages with thrombectomy but no significant difference in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhages. 5,6

Site poolability analysis demonstrated significant heterogeneity in site treatment effects. There were a couple of sites where outcomes were better in the control group, more sites wheretherewasabenefitofthrombectomytoasmallorlarge extent,andthepooledsiteslightlyfavoredcontrol.Thisheterogeneity may reflect the mix of academic and community hospitals that enrolled in the trial and may be a characteristic of real-worldclinicalpractice.Strokethrombectomycenterswith higher cumulative case volumes have been shown to have higher rates of recanalization and good outcome and lower rates of symptomaticintracranialhemorrhageandmortality. 29

TheTESLAtrialdidnotshowasignificantbenefitofendovascular thrombectomy for patients with large infarcts identified by noncontrast CT alone and presenting within 24 hours. However,thepotentialroleofthrombectomywiththisimaging approachandtimewindowmaybefurtherevaluatedwithadditional studies.
