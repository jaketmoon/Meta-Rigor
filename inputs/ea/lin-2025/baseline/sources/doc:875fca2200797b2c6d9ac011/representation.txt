Commentary

Time- to- treatment with endovascular thrombectomy in patients with large core ischemic stroke: the 'late window paradox'

Fawaz Al- Mufti, 1 Mohamed Elfil , 2 Hazem S Ghaith, 3 Sherief Ghozy , 4 Ahmed Elmashad, 5 Ashutosh P Jadhav Chirag D Gandhi, 7 Stephan Mayer 7

The results of the recently published randomized clinical trials of endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) for large vessel occlusion (LVO) in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients with a large ischemic core: ANGEL- ASPECT, 1 SELECT- 2, 2 and the RESCUE- Japan LIMIT trials, 3 are promising and are expected to lead to more AIS- LVO patients undergoing EVT.

Prior studies have demonstrated that shorter time- to- treatment has been found to be directly associated with better functional outcomes in AIS- LVO patients undergoing EVT. 4 In this short commentary, we have pooled the available data from the abovementioned trials to conduct a subgroup analysis of AIS- LVO patients with large ischemic area undergoing EVT according to the time from stroke onset to randomization (<6 vs ≥6 hours).

Functional independence was pooled as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) in a meta- analysis using a DerSimonian- Laird randomeffect model and generic inverse- variance method. Statistical analysis was done using Stata/MP software version 17 for Microsoft Windows.

The pooled analysis of the functional outcome reported by all studies favored the EVT group over the medical management group (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.66, P=0.03) (figure 1). The pooled studies were homogenous (I 2 =0%).

By contrast, in the ≥6 hours subgroup, EVT- treated patients showed a better functional outcome than the medical management group (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.80, P=0. 01) (figure 1).

Our subgroup analysis showed that the benefit of EVT for achieving favorable functional outcome among large- core LVO stroke patients is stronger among patients undergoing EVT in ≥6 hours from the symptom onset. This 'late window paradox 5 ' was also noted when the DEFUSE trial included AIS- LVO patients with a median of 11 hours from symptom onset to treatment, and showed that EVT achieved functional independence in 28% of those patients, 6 while the pooled analysis of the early window trials showed that EVT achieved functional independence in 19.5% of AIS- LVO patients overall. 7 A

crucial factor that might explain such an interesting finding, at least partially, is the natural evolution of the infarct according to the collateral status in the late window of AIS- LVO.

Fast progressors who present with large ischemic core volumes represent 25% of AIS- LVO stroke patients presenting within 6 hours and 40% of patients presenting between 3 and 4.5 hours. Slow progressors account for 55% of AIS- LVO patients and, despite delayed presentation after onset, they tend to experience slow infarct growth and have smaller ischemic core volumes. 8

Prior studies using MRI to follow growth of the ischemic core in AIS- LVO patients found that patients with poor collaterals tend to be fast growers and develop large ischemic areas within 2-3 hours, while patients with favourable collaterals are usually slow growers. 9 A better collateral status is known to be associated with a higher likelihood of achieving favorable functional outcome and lower mortality rates. 10-12

This concern stems from the fact that in our pooled analysis, there was a substantial difference between the number of patients in each group, 424 patients in the ≥6 hours group and 252 patients in the <6 hours group. Moreover, there is a diversity among the three trials regarding the imaging inclusion criteria including the imaging modality (CT vs MRI), the median volume of the infarcted area, and the definition of the large core infarction according to either the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) or the core volume per CT/MRI perfusion. Apart from these trials- related factors and from a physiological standpoint, it is difficult to certainly consider the collateral status as a surrogate for early versus late presentation of all AIS- LVO patients. In other words, not all patients within the late window (≥6 hours from symptom onset) fall into the category of slow progressors, and the same concept applies to patients within the early window (<6 hours from symptom onset) who should not all be considered as fast progressors.

Further data and post- hoc analyses are expected to be published from these trials which might provide a clearer explanation for the late window paradox among large- core LVO patients treated with EVT at later time points.
