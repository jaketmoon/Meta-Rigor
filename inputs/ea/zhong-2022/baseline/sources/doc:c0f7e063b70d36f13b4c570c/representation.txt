Pragmatic Randomized Trial of Corticosteroids and Inhaled Epinephrine for Bronchiolitis in Children in Intensive Care

Ben Gelbart, MBBS 1,2,3,4 , Brent McSharry, MBBS 5 , Carmel Delzoppo, BSc 1,4 , Simon Erickson, MBBS 6 , Katherine Lee, PhD 3,7 , Warwick Butt, MBBS 1,2,3,4 , Miriam Rea, BN 5 , Xiaofang Wang, PhD 3,7 , John Beca, MBChB 5 , Alex Kazemi, MBBS 8 , and Frank Shann, DMedSc 1,3

Objective To determine whether the combination of systemic corticosteroids and nebulized epinephrine, compared with standard care, reduces the duration of positive pressure support in children with bronchiolitis admitted to intensive care.

Study design We performed a pragmatic, multicenter, open-label, randomized trial between July 2013 and November 2019 in children younger than 18 months old with a clinical diagnosis of bronchiolitis. The intervention group received the equivalent of 13 mg/kg prednisolone over 3 days, then 1 mg/kg daily for 3 days, plus 0.05 mL/kg of nebulized 1% epinephrine made up to 6 ml with 0.9% saline via jet nebulizer and mask using oxygen at 12 l/min every 30 minutes for 5 doses, then 1-4 hourly for 3 days, then as required for 3 days. The primary outcome was clinician-managed duration of positive pressure support in intensive care defined as high-flow nasal-prong oxygen, nasopharyngeal continuous positive airway pressure, or mechanical ventilation.

Results In total, 210 children received positive pressure support. In the corticosteroid-epinephrine group, 107 children received positive pressure support for a geometric mean of 26 (95% CI, 22-32) hours compared with 40 (95% CI 34-47) hours in 103 controls, adjusted ratio 0.66 (95% CI 0.51-0.84), P = .001. In the intervention group, 41 (38%) children experienced at least 1 adverse event, compared with 39 (38%) in the control group.

Conclusions In children with severe bronchiolitis, the duration of clinician-managed pressure support was reduced by regular treatment with systemic corticosteroids and inhaled epinephrine compared with standard care. (J Pediatr 2022;244:17-23) .

V iral bronchiolitis is the most common cause of nonelective admission to pediatric intensive care 1 and places a high burden on patients, families, and health services. 2 The pharmacologic treatment of bronchiolitis remains controversial, but a recent review reported that none of 32 guidelines recommend routine use of corticosteroids, with 19 guidelines recommending against using epinephrine and 10 recommending epinephrine be used either routinely or as a trial. 3 A randomized trial of the treatment of bronchiolitis with a systemic corticosteroid plus nebulized epinephrine vs neither was performed in emergency departments in Canada. 4 The relative risk of hospital admission with dexamethasone-epinephrine vs placebo-saline was 0.65 (95% CI 0.45-0.95), but the P value was .07 after adjustment for multiple comparisons. The study used an oxygen flow rate of only 8 L/min with only 3 mL of fluid in the nebulizer. Penetration of aerosols into the infant lung is greatly improved with small droplet sizes of approximately 1-3 m mmass median aerodynamic diameter, 5 and the droplet size from jet nebulizers is reduced with high gas flow rates 6-8 and large volumes of fluid in the nebulizer. 9,10

Weperformed a pragmatic, multicenter, open-label randomized trial of repeated doses of corticosteroid and epinephrine to

investigate whether the combination would reduce the duration of positive pressure support (PPS) in children with bronchiolitis admitted to intensive care in Australia and New Zealand who require PPS. The intervention was started soon after intensive care admission with an initial loading dose followed by regular dosing throughout the admission. We tested this combination because it is probably superior to either agent alone. 4 Like the Canadian study, we used nebulized epinephrine rather than albuterol. Australia and New Zealand. 11,12 PPS was defined as high-flow nasal prong oxygen (HFNPO) at ³ 1 L/kg/min, nasopharyngeal continuous positive airway pressure (NCPAP), or mechanical ventilation, and its duration was determined by the unblinded treating clinician. Epinephrine was nebulized using a high gas flow with a large nebulizer fluid volume.

Methods

A pragmatic, open-label, randomized trial was performed at the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Starship and Middlemore Hospitals in Auckland, and Perth Children's Hospital. The trial was approved by the Royal Children's Hospital Ethics Committee (Study Number 32119), the Disability Ethics Committee, New Zealand (Study Number NTX/12/05/044/AM04), and the Western Australian Department of Health (Study Number 2020/EP). The trial was registered with the Australian Clinical Trials Research Network (ACTRN12613000316707).

Participants

Children were screened, consented by parents, and randomized at admission to intensive care by members of the study team. Data collection was performed by research assistants and investigators.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Children younger than 18 months old with a diagnosis of bronchiolitis were eligible up to 4 hours after admission to intensive care. The diagnosis was based on the presence of a first or second episode of wheezing, or respiratory distress associated with respiratory tract infection, plus either radiologic evidence of chest hyperinflation or clinical evidence of prolonged expiration. Children were excluded if they had received immunosuppressive agents in the previous 7 days, were less than 37 weeks of gestation at enrollment, or had laryngotracheobronchitis, previous enrollment in the study, or treatment with high frequency oscillation or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.

Randomization

Participants were assigned randomly to either the intervention or control group in a 1:1 using an online database to ensure allocation concealment. Randomization was stratified by hospital, the level of PPS at randomization (none, HFNPO-NCPAP, mechanical ventilation), and the presence of either cyanotic congenital heart disease or chronic lung disease requiring oxygen therapy for more than 14 days in the previous 6 months. Block randomization was used with a fixed block size that was not known to the clinicians or investigators. In March 2015, after 50 participants had been recruited, the large number of strata resulted in unequal numbers in the 2 treatment groups. Consequently, the randomization process was modified so the first participant in any new block was randomized with a probability inversely related to the proportion of participants in the 2 groups; for example, if 40% of participants had been randomized to the control group, the new participant would have a 60% chance of being in the control group. After randomization of the first person in a block, subsequent allocations were set to achieve an equal number of intervention and control subjects in that block. Allocation concealment was maintained.

Procedures

Thecontrol group received standard care with no placebo. PPS was administered at the discretion of the treating clinician according to the trial guidelines ( Appendix 1 ; available at www.jpeds.com). Corticosteroids and epinephrine nebulization were permitted in the control group only for postextubation stridor. Selective b -agonist therapy was not permitted. Nasopharyngeal aspirates were recommended but performed at the discretion of the clinician.

In addition to standard care, participants in the intervention group received an initial dose of dexamethasone (0.6 mg/kg parenterally) or, if this was not available, an equipotent dose of methylprednisolone (4 mg/kg intravenously) or, if there was no venous access, prednisolone (4 mg/kg enterally); subsequently, prednisolone 1 mg/kg was given enterally every 8 hours for 3 days, then daily for 3 days. With the initial dose of corticosteroid, 5 doses of nebulized epinephrine (0.05 ml/kg of 1% solution or 0.5 ml/kg of 1/1000), made up to 6 mL with 0.9% saline, was administered half hourly, then 1-4 hourly for the first 3 days, then as required for 3 days; nebulization was via MICRO MIST (Hudson RCI) using 12 L/min of oxygen through the ventilation circuit in patients receiving NCPAP or mechanical ventilation, or by mask with HFNPO reduced to 2-4 L/min ( Appendices 1-3 ; available at www.jpeds.com). Study interventions were ceased when the unblinded, treating clinician decided the child was ready for discharge from intensive care.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the duration of PPS in hours from the time of randomization to discharge from intensive care. In March 2017, the preplanned statistical analysis plan was changed to exclude participants with zero hours of PPS for whom the research question and primary outcome were not relevant (independent of treatment allocation). PPS was managed by the unblinded treating clinicians without a specific weaning protocol until oxygen saturation was 92% or more in low-flow oxygen or room air. Secondary outcomes were time from randomization until the clinician declared readiness for intensive care discharge; the time until actual discharge from intensive care; the time from randomization to hospital discharge; the duration and proportion of participants receiving HFNPO, NCPAP, mechanical ventilation, and NCPAP or mechanical ventilation or both (but not HFNPO); and the number of escalations of respiratory support (from both HFNPO and CPAP). Adverse events including tachycardia, tachyarrhythmias, and hypertension were recorded from randomization until discharge from intensive care. Episodes of hyperglycemia were recorded in the first 72 hours from randomization.

Statistical Analyses

The sample size was based on 1723 children with bronchiolitis admitted to pediatric intensive care units in Australia and New Zealand from 2001 to 2004; the mean of the natural log of the hours of PPS was 4.08 (geometric mean 59.4 hours), with an SD of 1.10. To detect a ratio of 0.60 or less in the duration of PPS (corresponding to a reduction of at least 24 hours of PPS) with 90% power and a P value of .05, 98 patients were needed per group (based on a SD in both groups of 1.10 on the log scale). As only 64% of children received PPS as NCPAP or mechanical ventilation in 2001-2004, the protocol was adjusted to allow for enrolled children who did not receive PPS; therefore, the trial aimed to recruit 153 children per group. However, HFNPO became routine just as the trial started and all but one participant received PPS, so the adjustment was not required; consequently, on November 25th 2019, the Data and Safety Monitoring Committee recommended that the trial cease recruitment because the required number of children receiving PPS (98 in each group) had been recruited. The Trial Management Committee convened on December 9th, 2019 and stopped the study.

An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Committee performed planned interim analyses at 50% and 75% of recruitment using the Haybittle-Peto stopping rule, where early stopping of the trial was recommended only if P < .001 for the primary outcome.

An intention-to-treat analysis was performed with children who did not receive PPS excluded from the primary analysis ( Appendix 4 ; available at www.jpeds.com). Baseline and demographic data were described using frequencies and proportions. The duration of PPS, HFNPO, NCPAP, mechanical ventilation, and intensive care and hospital length of stay were summarized as the geometric mean, plus the mean and SD on the natural log scale. Log-linear regression was used to compare the groups adjusted for the stratification factors used in randomization. Many participants had zero hours of NCPAP plus mechanical ventilation, so for this end point the groups were compared using zero-inflated Poisson regression with robust estimation of variance adjusted for the stratification factors at randomization.

Outcomes regarding the receipt of PPS, HFNPO, NCPAP, and mechanical ventilation from randomization to discharge from intensive care were summarized as the number and proportion of participants requiring each type of support, and the number of escalations of support (HFNPO to NCPAP 1 escalation, NCPAP to mechanical ventilation 1 escalation, and HFNPO to mechanical ventilation 2 escalations). Analysis of escalations was supplemented, post-hoc, with a Wilcoxon rank sum test. Post-hoc analyses were also performed for hours of PPS by sex and age, and for the primary outcome, but including any child who did not receive PPS, with PPS coded as 0.001 hours because the logarithm of zero is undefined. All other analyses were prespecified in the Statistical Analysis Plan.

Sensitivity analyses were performed for the primary outcome, including duration of PPS administered after discharge from intensive care, with adjustment for HPNP, NCPAP, or mechanical ventilation at randomization, and for the primary outcome without adjustment for congenital heart disease or chronic lung disease and post-hoc for the primary outcome including any child who received zero hours of PPS.

Prespecified subgroup analyses of the duration of PPS were performed by the stratification variables and by predefined subgroups: children with congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, prematurity (<37 weeks of gestation), those who tested positive for respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) or human metapneumovirus, and respiratory support mode at baseline. Treatment effects between subgroups were compared by including an interaction term in the regression model.

Results

Between July 24, 2013, and November 22, 2019, 211 children were enrolled; 107 were randomized to the intervention group and 104 to the control group. One child in the control group did not receive PPS and was excluded from the analysis but included in a post-hoc sensitivity analysis. Of the 210 remaining participants, 12 were aged <1 month and 14 were aged >12 months. The flow chart of participant recruitment is shown in Figure 1 , and protocol violations in Appendix Table I (available at www.jpeds.com). Baseline characteristics of the participants are shown in Table I . Table II shows the primary and secondary outcomes. Polymerase chain reaction testing of nasopharyngeal aspirates was performed in 197 of the 210 children: RSV was detected in 117 children (59%), human metapneumovirus in 10, and other viruses in 15 ( Appendix Table II ; available at www.jpeds.com). The primary outcome of geometric mean duration of PPS from randomization until discharge from intensive care was 26.4 hours (95% CI 21.8-31.8) in the intervention group and 39.9 hours (95% CI 33.6-47.4) in controls, with an adjusted ratio of 0.66 (95% CI 0.51-0.84), P = .001.

In a sensitivity analysis including extra PPS administered after discharge from intensive care in 6 children, the adjusted ratio of duration of PPS in the intervention compared with the control group was 0.66 (95% CI 0.52-0.85), P = .001 ( Appendix Table III ; available at www.jpeds.com). In a sensitivity analysis including the child in the control group who received no PPS, coded as 0.001 hours of PPS, the adjusted ratio of duration of PPS in the intervention, compared with the control group, was unchanged at 0.66 (0.51-0.84).

For stratification factors and in the preplanned subgroup analysis ( Table III ), there was no evidence that the intervention effect varied by hospital, prematurity (gestation <37 weeks), or infection by human metapneumovirus, but there was evidence of an interaction with chronic lung disease ( P = .025) and infection by RSV ( P = .018). In 117 children with RSV, the adjusted ratio for duration of PPS was 0.49 (95% CI 0.36-0.68) compared with 0.91 (95% CI 0.62-1.35) in those without RSV. Among children on HFNPO or NCPAP at randomization, there were 24 escalations of PPS (from HFNPO to NCPAP, or NCPAP to mechanical ventilation) in the control group (0.23 escalations per child), compared with 14 escalations in the treatment group (0.13 escalations per child), P = .124 ( Figure 2 ; available at www.jpeds.com). Between 9 AM and 6.59 PM, PPS was ceased in 58% of the 210 participants, 84% were discharged from intensive care, and 94% were discharged from hospital ( Appendix Table IV ; available at www.jpeds.com).

In the intervention group, 41 (38%) experienced at least 1 adverse event, compared with 39 (38%) in controls ( Table IV ; available at www.jpeds.com), most commonly sinus tachycardia. One episode of supraventricular tachyarrhythmia occurred in a child receiving nebulized epinephrine via NCPAP which resolved spontaneously. Another child experienced bradycardia and hypotension requiring brief cardiopulmonary resuscitation, but this occurred 48 hours after epinephrine.

Discussion

In children with bronchiolitis requiring intensive care, this pragmatic, multicenter, open-label, randomized trial found strong evidence ( P = .001) that the combination of systemic corticosteroids and nebulized epinephrine reduced the duration of PPS managed by unblinded clinicians. In prospectively planned, secondary analyses, there was strong evidence ( P = .005) that the intervention reduced the time from randomization until a clinician determined that a child was ready for discharge from intensive care, but only weak evidence ( P = .053) of a reduction in the time to actual discharge; there was no evidence of an effect on hospital length of stay; and the intervention almost halved the duration of NCPAP and mechanical ventilation in the sickest children, but they were managed by unblinded clinicians not using a specific weaning protocol. The intervention halved the duration of PPS in RSVpositive bronchiolitis but had minimal effect in RSVnegative bronchiolitis. Infants with RSV bronchiolitis have metabolome and bacterial metagenome profiles different from those in rhinovirus bronchiolitis, 14 implying that virus-specific effects might influence the response to corticosteroids and epinephrine.

Bronchiolitis is a major cause of hospitalization and admission to intensive care, so these findings have important implications for patients, families, and the cost of health care. 1,2,15 Areduction of 14 hours in the duration of PPS represents a substantial proportion of the 40 hours in the control group. The intervention had only a modest effect on intensive care length of stay ( P = .053) and no effect was observed on hospital length of stay. The 2 main reasons are that the time of discharge is influenced by other factors (such as social considerations) in addition to the need for respiratory support, and that the trial was not powered to detect differences in length of stay. Because 84% of discharges from intensive care and 94% of discharges from hospital occurred during daylight hours (between 9 AM and 6:59 PM), a child who recovered at 8 PM would usually be discharged at the same time as a child who did not recover until 12 hours later at 8 AM the next day, so length of stay in intensive care and in hospital are insensitive to changes in the duration of illness, and a larger trial would be needed to detect an effect on length of stay. There is no widely recognized objective measure of outcome in bronchiolitis 16 ; we chose, as the primary outcome, the duration of PPS as determined by unblinded clinicians. The duration of PPS is measurable and clinically meaningful to children, families, and clinicians.

The PRagmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary tool for pragmatic or explanatory trials was published after our trial started. 17,18 Wejudged that our trial was mostly 'pragmatic,' with 'explanatory' aspects because we administered the intervention with attention to adherence and recorded the primary outcome in every child.

The only previous randomized trial of corticosteroidepinephrine therapy vs no corticosteroid-sympathomimetic, in emergency departments in Canada, suggested the combination might reduce the need for hospital admission in mild-to-moderate bronchiolitis and is more cost effective than either therapy alone or neither. 19 Our findings suggest an effect in severe disease. Epinephrine may be more effective than albuterol, 20 and there is evidence that the a -adrenergic action of epinephrine may be important in bronchiolitis. 21 These doses were safe, with a similar proportion of treatment and control patients experiencing adverse events, predominantly sinus tachycardia. One brief episode of supraventricular tachycardia during inhaled epinephrine demonstrates the need for monitoring during its administration.

We gave loading and then regular doses of both medications soon after admission to maximize the benefits, and used high nebulizer flows and large volumes to deliver the small droplet sizes needed in infants. 6,7,9,10 In addition to its main role of reducing droplet size, the 0.9% saline used to dilute the epinephrine might have directly affected the outcome, however, any direct effect is likely to have been augmented by epinephrine because, in the Canadian study, epinephrine-saline-corticosteroid was superior to placebo, but saline-corticosteroid was not. 4

The pragmatic design of the trial allowed comparison of corticosteroid-epinephrine with current practice, but it had important limitations. The key limitation was the unblinded design, which might have caused bias because the duration of PPS was determined by unblinded clinicians without a strict weaning protocol (but with unit guidelines that suggest keeping oxygen saturation at 92% or more). Only 211 (25%) of 837 eligible children were enrolled because no attempt was made to recruit 383 (46%) children, and consent was obtained from only 211 (51%) of the 411 families approached. The low recruitment rate might have led to selection bias and reduced the generalizability of our findings. To assess its true effect, the intervention needed to be started soon after admission to intensive care, but the requirement that randomization occur within 4 hours challenged both researchers and families. The low consent rate reflected the substantial stress on parents when they are asked to participate in trials in intensive care. 23 Finally, clinicians may have been more likely to escalate therapy or wean slowly in the control arm, especially given that HFNPO was relatively new early in the trial and clinicians may not have yet become comfortable with treating respiratory distress with HFNPO alone.

ORIGINAL ARTICLES

In children with bronchiolitis in intensive care who required PPS and were managed by unblinded clinicians, compared with standard care, the duration of PPS was reduced by treatment with repeated doses of a combination of systemic corticosteroids and nebulized epinephrine, using a high gas flow and a large volume of fluid in the nebulizer. In prespecified secondary analyses, the effect was strongest in RSV-positive bronchiolitis, and in the sickest children who required NCPAP or mechanical ventilation (managed by unblinded clinicians). The pragmatic design allowed comparison of corticosteroid-epinephrine with current practice, but bias might have occurred because the trial was unblinded, weaning was not protocolized, and because only 25% of eligible children were enrolled. n
