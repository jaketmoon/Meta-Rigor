Respiratory Support in Bronchiolitis: Trial Evidence

Acute viral lower respiratory tract infection is frequently associated with hypoxemia and respiratory distress, sometimes progressing to hypercarbia and respiratory failure. In recent years, trials have assessed the effects of oxygen supplementation and respiratory support with high-flow oxygen therapy. An oxygen saturation target of 90% is as safe and clinically effective as 94% in infants with bronchiolitis. Trials of highflow humidified oxygen have demonstrated safety, but as yet poorly demarcated an appropriate place for use within the clinical course of the disease. Effective trials of continuous positive airway pressure are lacking and urgently required. The trials reported to date have highlighted the need for common outcomes in relation to treatment failure.

Viral lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) in infants is the mostcommonconditionassociatedwithadmissiontohospital in the first year of life. In the 80 years since its principal description, 1 bronchiolitis has changed little, with the exception of fewer deaths for children with the condition in developedhealthcarecomparedwithdevelopinghealthcare. 2 With no effective treatments, the key change over time has been improvements in supportive care, hydration, and respiratory support. As viral upper respiratory tract infection progresses toanLRTI,tachypnea,hypoxemia,hypercarbia,andrespiratory failure may require support to tide over the period of illness until recovery occurs. Hydration aside, which can be nasogastric or intravenous, both of which appearequallyeffective, 3 it is the widespread availability of supplemental oxygen, positive airway pressure, and intensive care support that has contributed most to improved outcomes over time.

Oxygen Saturation Targets and Outcome in Lower Respiratory Tract Infection

Supplemental oxygen for respiratory disease became widespread in western health care in the 1950s and was provided liberally with little ability to monitor. Supplemental oxygen has a clear benefit in improving outcomes in acute LRTI, 4 but less clear is how much supplemental oxygen is required to support children with LRTI. 5 In obstructive sleep apnea, chronic recurrent hypoxemia has been associated with cognitive deficits, 6 and infants with chronic lung disease of prematurity have higher mortality when target oxygen saturation margins differ by just 3%. 7 Hypoxemia is common in children with bronchiolitis. Arriving at the emergency department (ED), 40% of infants have an SpO2 below 94% and 10 to 16% have SpO2 below 90%. 8,9 During hospital admission, 73% of infants are provided with supplemental oxygen at a 94% SpO2 threshold and 56% of infants when a 90% target is applied. 9 The Bronchiolitis of Infancy Discharge Study (BIDS) compared a recommended normal oxygen saturation in health 10 ( > 94%) versus the World Health Organization recommended SpO2 ( > 90%), which was also adopted by the United States Academy of Pediatrics in their Bronchiolitis guideline of 2005. 11 The BIDS trial of 615 infants with bronchiolitis identified equivalence of time to symptom resolution (no cough, which is the principal symptom in bronchiolitis) in a double-blind randomized controlled trial. 9 Unexpectedly, other clinical outcomes were better in infants managed with a lower oxygen saturation target, including the time to return to adequate feeding, time back to normal, and fewer infants requiring readmission to hospital and antibiotics postdischarge. Supportive Management of Respiratory Distress

Respiratory distress is common, essentially pathognomic, in LRTI and bronchiolitis. Inflammation and mucous plugging of the bronchioles, combined with a compliant chest wall, creates additional work of breathing and retractions. Many infants, particularly over 6 months of age, often have the capacity to tolerate long periods of dyspnea before spontaneous improvement sets in. Supplemental oxygen does not relieve respiratory distress. 13

Historically, limitedcriticalcare capacityrationedthe use of (nasal) continuouspositive airway pressure (CPAP) support for infants with LRTI and respiratory distress. With options limited, infants who recovered either with low-flow supplemental oxygen or with increasing respiratory failure were trialed onCPAPtostave off intubation and ventilation. 14 Caring for an infant with respiratory distress is distressing for parents and staff, and the wish to alleviate any dyspnea is understandable. High-flow humidified oxygen (HFOT, variably termed) blends highly humidified oxygen and air at higher flow rates than traditional low-flow oxygen. The result is considered to provide greater comfort in breathing and to enable a higher inspired oxygen (typically up to 60% FiO2) when compared with low-flow oxygen (typically 30 -38% FiO2). The flow does provide some degree of positive airway pressure, but this is mostlikely in the order of 2 to 3 cm H20 and not 6 to 10 cm H2O that CPAP units would generate in the airway. HFOT is thereforeoftenprovidedtogiveperceivedbenefittopatientcomfort and to reduce work of breathing.

Supporting Respiratory Failure with Hypercarbia

With LRTI progression, tiring respiratory muscles lead to respiratory failure, heralded by hypercarbia (defined generally as pCO2 > 6.5kPa/50 mm Hg). Applying positive airway pressure can open blocked and inflamed airways, enhancing ventilation and airway clearance. CPAP can be applied by nasal maskor prongs and has been available for more than 20 years. Disappointingly, there are no effective studies of CPAP use in bronchiolitis in a developed health care setting. Observational studies suggest benefits in improving ventilation (reducing hypercarbia 14 and respiratory rate 15 ). Once again, colleagues working in developing health care may provide evidence to structure our approach, with one trial completed and one in progress to assess bubble CPAP in respiratory failure. 16,17 TherearenotrialsofmechanicalventilationinLRTIinchildren. Atrial is in progress to assess the effect of exogenoussurfactant in children ventilated with acute bronchiolitis.

Thresholds for Intervening in Bronchiolitis Are Changing

When care options were limited to low-flow oxygen or critical care CPAP, clinical thresholds were mostly objective. Hypoxemia as a driver for low flow supplemental oxygen, objectively measured by pulse oxygen saturation monitoring. A trial of CPAP to support respiratory failure, objectively measured by blood gas carbon dioxide. Variance in practice appears increasingly determined by subjective clinical interpretation of respiratory distress, which is difficult to standardize (by clinical scoring or other means).

Admissions to critical care have increased considerably over recent years (together with associated costs), despite stable mortality rates and no understood change in respiratory syncytial virus pathogenicity. 18 Rate of admission and practice is highly variable between countries (4 -18%) and between critical care units. 19,20 While some increase in critical care use relates to additional intervention in children with complex health care needs, most variability in critical care appears to be related to differing clinical thresholds for intervention. The arrival of HFOT appears to have added to this variance, as the introduction to clinical care has been more rapid than the ability to assess its merits.

HFOT is being widely introduced to the clinical space between hypoxemia controlled by low-flow oxygen and hypercarbic respiratory failure. The ability of HFOT to enhance oxygenation and improve ventilation has been demonstrated in animal models. 21 The question is not quite whether it can do these things, but what population requires to be served by something additional to lowflow oxygen through a nasal cannula (2l/minute) or CPAP. This is a health and economic consideration as HFOT may be provided in ward-based settings, whereas CPAP is most commonly provided in critical care settings. HFOT established itself in neonatal care 22 and has rapidly extended to infants with acute LRTI. Observational studies suggest that HFOT may reduce work of breathing, but that is not objectively demonstrated, and the effect of reducing work of breathing on other health outcomes (time in oxygen, time to discharge, time to resolution of symptoms) is unproven. 23

Randomized Trials of High-Flow Humidified Oxygen and Continuous Positive Airway Pressure

Thefirst randomized controlled trials of HFOT in infants with acute LRTI have recently been reported.

An open-label study assigned 202 children in hospital pediatric wards with moderate bronchiolitis to either lowflow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen (maximum 2l/minute c30 -38% FiO2) or HFOT (maximum 11/kg/minute c60% FiO2). The study identified that HFOT did not result in faster weaning from supplemental oxygen nor a faster time to discharge. 24 A significantly higher number of children had a failure of treatment in the low-flow oxygen group when compared with those in the HFOT group. However, the number requiring critical care support was equivalent. The trial demonstrates that early use of HFOT does not alter the clinical course of acute bronchiolitis.

The Tramontane study randomly assigned 142 children in a pediatric intensive care setting with moderate/severe acute viral bronchiolitis to either open-label nasal CPAP (7 cmH2O) or HFOT (2l/kg/minute). 25 This noninferiority trial demonstrated that HFOT was noninferior to nasal CPAP in terms of treatment failure: 31% failed in the nasal CPAP group (22 of 71 infants) compared with 51% (36 of 71 infants) in the HFNC group, which did not meet the prespecified limits for noninferiority. A posthoc superiority analysis suggested that CPAP was superior to HFOT. A study within an ED randomly assigned 60 children to helmet CPAP ( n ¼ 20) compared with HFOT ( n ¼ 20) or control ( n ¼ 20, presumed low-flow oxygen). 26 A large proportion of the included infants were diagnosed with bronchiolitis (77%), with the remainder with pneumonia and asthma. Children were included if they had respiratory distress on arrival at ED. CPAP had faster improvement in pH and pCO2 with a shorter duration of hospitalization, whereas HFOT had faster improvements in FiO2. Groups were not well balanced at baseline for age or weight, with HFOT allocated to older and heavier children. In a developing tertiary hospital health care setting, a trial randomly assigned 225 children under 5 years of age with pneumonia and hypoxemia to either bubble CPAP ( N ¼ 79; 35%), HFOT ( N ¼ 67; 30%), or low-flow oxygen therapy ( N ¼ 79; 35%). 16 The trial was stopped prematurely by a data monitoring committee as fewer deaths occurred in the CPAP group ( N ¼ 3, 4%) compared with the HFOT ( N ¼ 10, 13%) or low-flow oxygen therapy ( N ¼ 10, 15%) group. At the time of closure of the study, there were treatment failures in 6% ( N ¼ 5) of CPAP, 13% ( N ¼ 10) of HFOT, and 14% ( N ¼ 16) of low-flow oxygen therapy participants.

The PARIS (Paediatric Acute Respiratory Intervention Study) trial is the largest trial to date of HFOT in moderate/ acute bronchiolitis. 27 In total, 1,400 hospitalized infants have been randomized to receive either HFOT or low-flow oxygen therapy. The primary outcome is treatment failure. The study is anticipated to report within 2018. Striking about all these studies is that treatment failure is variably defined, and success or failure is predicated on nonstandardized definitions of treatment failure.

What Is Treatment Failure in Acute Lower Respiratory Tract Infection?

There are no common agreed definitions of what would constitute a failure of treatment in acute LRTI, apart from death. Death is uncommon in term and healthy infants in developed health care 28 and could not be considered except in resource-poor settings.

Future Directions

Further trials to standardize the delivery of respiratory support in bronchiolitis are required. These may include standardizing the delivery of respiratory support, including targets, weaning strategies, and stopping rules. A trial of CPAP in developed health care is urgently required, including assessments of use outside critical care areas. Thresholds for treatment failure should be defined, ensuring that they relate to risk, so that valuable resources are appropriately allocated and treatments are provided to those most in need.
