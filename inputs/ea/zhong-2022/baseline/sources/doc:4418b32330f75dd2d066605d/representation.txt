We analyzed the feeding received by 505 infants receiving HFNC therapy in the largest RCT conducted 5 : 71.3% (n = 360) received only enteral fluids, and a further 18.4% (n = 93) received some enteral feeds with intravenous fluid; there were no cases of pulmonary aspiration or other additional serious adverse events. 5 Both RCTs reported no difference between groups for duration of oxygen therapy or length of stay, suggesting that even if HFNC therapy caused considerably poorer feeding in comparison with standard oxygen therapy, it does not alter either of these important markers with respect to achieving adequate feeding for hospital discharge. These data demonstrate that enteral feeding is safe and timely for infants with bronchiolitis receiving HFNC therapy.

High-flownasalcannulatherapyisasafeandeffectiveway of delivering respiratory support to infants with bronchiolitis with minimal risks. The greatest issue with HFNC therapy, as alluded to by Ralston, 1 may be that it is initiated without any clinical utility. Increased cost, with no associated reduction in length of stay, supports limiting the use of HFNC to rescue therapy for infants with bronchiolitis.
