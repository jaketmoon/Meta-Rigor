Humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen in bronchiolitis reduces need for invasive ventilation but not intensive care admission

Chong Tien Goh , Lynette J Kirby, David N Schell and Jonathan R Egan

Paediatric Intensive Care Unit, The Children ' s Hospital at Westmead, Sydney, New South Wales, Australia

Aim: To describe the changes to paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission patterns and ventilation requirements for children with bronchiolitis following the introduction of humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen outside the PICU.

Methods: Retrospective study comparing patients <24 months of age with a discharge diagnosis of bronchiolitis admitted to the PICU. A comparison was made between those before humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen use (year 2008) to those immediately following the introduction of humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen use (year 2011) and those following further consolidation of humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen use outside the PICU (year 2013).

Results: Humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen use up to 1 L/kg/min in the hospital did not reduce PICU admission. Intubation rates were reduced from 22.2% in 2008 to 7.8% in 2013. There was a non-signi fi cant trend towards decreased length of stay in the PICU while hospital length of stay showed a signi fi cant decrease following the introduction of humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen. Age <6 months and respiratory syncytial virus bronchiolitis were associated with an increased chance of failing humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen therapy.

Conclusion: Humidi fi ed high- fl ow nasal cannula oxygen utilised outside of the PICU in our institution for children with bronchiolitis did not reduce admission rates or length of stay to the PICU but was associated with a decreasing need for invasive ventilation and reduced hospital length of stay.

Key words: bronchiolitis; general paediatrics; high- fl ow nasal cannula; intensive care.

PICU. 8,9 An American PICU 7 and an Australian PICU 10 both reported decreases in the proportion of children with bronchiolitis being intubated following the introduction of HFNC in their respective units.

HFNC has inherent advantages over nasal NIV, such as ease of setup and improved tolerance, with minimal adverse events related to its use. As a result, it is increasingly being utilised outside the intensive care unit. 8,9,11 The Children ' s Hospital at Westmead introduced HFNC outside the PICU for the treatment of children with acute bronchiolitis in 2011. Following training of medical and nursing staff, HFNC could be initiated in the ED or ward at the discretion of ward medical staff, with PICU outreach team input. Flows and FiO2 were titrated to patient ' s clinical status and to achieve an oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry of >94%, as per hospital policy. Following further consolidation of use, a policy change in 2013 led to an increase in the maximum HFNC fl ow rates allowed outside the PICU.

It is unclear as to whether the use of HFNC in the ED or wards would lead to a decreasing need for PICU admission. This study was conducted to determine whether the introduction of HFNC therapy in the wards and ED led to any changes in PICU admission patterns and outcomes for children with bronchiolitis in our institution. We hypothesised that there would be a decrease in the rate of admission to PICU for patients with bronchiolitis following introduction HFNC in the ED and wards.

Methods

Following institutional ethics committee approval, a retrospective review was conducted on three cohorts of children admitted with a diagnosis of bronchiolitis to the PICU of the Children ' s Hospital at Westmead. All patients <24 months of age admitted to the PICU from within the hospital with a discharge diagnosis of bronchiolitis were identi fi ed from the PICU database. Admissions to the PICU from outside the hospital and children who were already established on home ventilation were excluded. Patients were admitted to the PICU at the discretion of the attending intensivist for further monitoring and/or escalation of treatment as required. The study cohorts were selected over three epochs based on the timeline of introduction of the use of HFNC into the hospital and were as follows:

Year 2008: pre-HFNC introduction

Year 2011: during initial HFNC introduction. Patients requiring more than 0.5 L/kg/min or >5 L/min of humidi fi ed fl ows required admission to the PICU

Year 2013: consolidation of HFNC use. Patients requiring more than 1 L/kg/min or >15 L/min of humidi fi ed fl ows required admission to the PICU

HFNC use was initiated at the discretion of the attending physician following review of the patient in the emergency department, wards, or the PICU. Further management of the patients in the PICU was guided by the attending intensivist.

Demographic and patient data, including PICU length of stay, were extracted from the PICU database. Hospital length of stay, the presence of co-morbidities such as prematurity, chronic lung disease and congenital heart disease as well as duration and types of ventilatory support were identi fi ed from individual patient records. Patients with bronchiolitis were routinely tested for respiratory viruses via nasopharyngeal aspirate polymerase chain reaction testing, these results were noted.

HFNC was commenced using an appropriate-sized nasal cannula (Fisher & Paykel Healthcare BC2745, BC2755 or BC3780) and an appropriate circuit with the humidi fi er base (Fisher & Paykel Healthcare MR850) auto-set at 37  C. FiO2 was titrated using an air-oxygen blender to maintain oxygen saturation >94%. Weaning of HFNC was initiated when the child ' s clinical condition improved, as re fl ected by a decrease in work of breathing and a need of FiO2 < 40% to maintain oxygen saturations >94%. Establishment and weaning of HFNC was guided by a hospital wide policy on HFNC usage for bronchiolitis.

NIV was de fi ned as any form of ventilatory support, excluding HFNC, applied without the use of an endotracheal tube. NIV was delivered in the form of CPAP or bi-level positive airway pressure via an appropriately sized facial/nasal mask or nasal prongs. Initiation of NIV was at the discretion of the attending intensivist on duty.

Data were expressed as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as median (inter-quartile range) for continuous variables. For categorical variables, comparisons were performed using the χ 2 -test or Fisher ' s exact tests as appropriate. Continuous variables were analysed using the Mann -Whitney U test. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (International Business Machines Corp, Armonk, New York, USA). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically signi fi cant.

Results

There were 166 children with bronchiolitis admitted into the PICU during the three epochs. Demographic data are summarised in Table 1. There were a total of 622, 795 and 1004 patients with bronchiolitis admitted to the hospital in 2008, 2011 and 2013 respectively. The number of patients translated into a nonsigni fi cant increase in rates of patients admitted to the PICU over the three epochs. (5.7 vs. 6.7 vs. 7.7%, P = 0.33). Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) was the predominant viral pathogen across the three epochs, there was no signi fi cant difference in viral pattern over the three cohorts. Admission pattern was similar over the three periods, showing a peak in admission numbers over the months of June to August. This corresponds to the winter months in the Southern Hemisphere.

The use of HFNC was signi fi cantly higher in 2013 compared with 2011 and 2008 (Fig. 1). A total of 98.7% of patients admitted to the PICU in 2013 had HFNC as a form of ventilator support at some point during their PICU stay. This was associated with a fall in NIV usage of 32.5% in 2013 compared to 83.3% in 2008. The proportion of patients requiring intubation were significantly lower in the 2 years where HFNC was employed, with 7.8, 9.4 and 22.2% of admitted patients requiring intubation in 2013, 2011 and 2008 respectively (Fig. 1). PICU length of stay was not signi fi cantly affected by the introduction of HFNC, although there was a trend towards shorter stays. Total hospital length of stay for patients was signi fi cantly shorter in patients from the years post-HFNC use, i.e., 2011 and 2013 when compared to those in the year pre-HFNC (Table 2).

A total of 51/53 (96.2%) and 77/77 (100.0%) of patients in the 2011 and 2013 cohort had HFNC applied prior to admission to the PICU. Of the two patients who did not receive HFNC in the 2011 cohort, one had persistent recurrent apnoeas and hypoxaemia requiring intubation and the other was initiated on NIV by the attending team based on the severity of his work of breathing.

Figure 2 demonstrates the use of different ventilator modalities across the three cohorts. A total of 97 patients (72 in 2013 and 25 in 2011) had HFNC as their fi rst modality of support during their PICU stay. Maximum HFNC fl ow rates while in PICU for this subset of patients was ≤ 1 L/kg/min in 19/97 (19.6%), 1.01 -1.5 L/kg/min in 13/97 (13.4%) and 1.51 -2 L/kg/min in 65/97 (67.0%). Rates of failure of HFNC as de fi ned by need for escalation of therapy to NIV or invasive ventilation were similar between the 2 years, being 6/24 (24.0%) in the 2011 cohort and 32/73 (29.1%) in the 2013 cohort respectively. Patients failing HFNC therapy were more likely to be less than 6 months (OR 3.26 (95% CI 1.29 -8.21)) or were RSV positive (OR 3.78 (95% CI 1.45 -9.84)). Presence of co-morbidities did not predict HFNC failure. Patients failing HFNC had a signi fi cantly longer PICU length of stay (median 3.7 vs. 1.8 days). All patients were either weaned to HFNC at fl ows appropriate for ward care or just supplemental oxygen prior to discharge.

There were two cases of pressure sores and one case of barotrauma associated with invasive ventilation. No complications related to device application or barotrauma were noted in patients receiving only HFNC or NIV.

Discussion

Our study shows that use of HFNC has signi fi cantly increased in patients requiring PICU admission in our institution since its introduction in 2011. This is likely due to the ease of application of HFNC and increasing staff comfort with its use. HFNC appears to be well tolerated by infants, with minimal adverse events. 6,7,10,12

We observed a non-signi fi cant increase in PICU admission rates of infants with bronchiolitis following HFNC introduction in the ED and wards. This varies from recent fi ndings by May fi eld, 13 who documented reduced admission rates of patients with bronchiolitis initiated on HFNC in the ED. However, their study initiated HFNC at a fl ow of 2 L/kg/min, whereas our wards and ED were only allowed to titrate fl ows up to a maximum of 1 L/ kg/min. While there is currently no simple de fi nition of what constitutes ' high- fl ow ' , 14 fl ows below 1 L/kg/min are likely to be insuf fi cient in washing out pharyngeal dead space and may not provide adequate airway distending pressure. 6 Thus, it is possible that many of the patients in the wards were receiving insuf fi cient fl ows for it to be bene fi cial. This is supported by the fi nding that majority of our patients were maintained on HFNC following their admission to the PICU, the only difference being an increase of fl ow rates to a maximum of 2 L/kg/min. Increasing fl ow rates on HFNC leads to a reduction in respiratory rate and effort in children with bronchiolitis. 6 The amount of airway distending pressure supplied by HFNC is also linearly correlated to increasing fl ows, with fl ows of 2 L/kg/min capable of providing a pressure up to 4 cm H2O. 6,15 The provision of pressure nearer to auto-positive end expiratory pressure reduces inspiratory burden, and the higher distending airway pressure has additional bene fi ts of ' stenting ' the lower airways open during exhalation. 14

While PICU admission rates did not decrease, there was a reduction in the proportion of patients requiring intubation, and a non-signi fi cant trend towards shorter PICU length of stay. Total hospital length of stay was signi fi cantly reduced. Ganu et al . 2 had previously reported our experience of NIV usage in children with bronchiolitis admitted to our PICU from January 2000 to December 2009. The implementation of HFNC in our institution has been associated with a decline in the need for NIV in this group of patients. We also found that intubation rates were further reduced following HFNC introduction when compared to Ganu ' s data. These fi ndings mirror that of work by Schibler 10 and McKiernan. 7 The unique feature of humidi fi ed air fl ow generated by HFNC may provide additional bene fi ts that may not be evident in NIV. Its ease of setup and use may also have contributed to earlier initiation of some form of respiratory support, thus minimising or preventing the deleterious onset of air trapping and/or atelectasis.

About one quarter of patients admitted to the PICU on HFNC required escalation in their respiratory support. This was more likely in children younger than 6 months of age or those with RSV bronchiolitis. Recent literature has suggested that younger postnatal age and RSV bronchiolitis are factors associated with more severe disease. 16 -19 Our data supports this observation and it may be that this particular group of patients might bene fi t from earlier PICU support and intervention.

As the majority of patients did not require escalation of support beyond HFNC at 2 L/kg/min, allowing such fl ows to be used in the general ward could potentially reduce PICU admissions for this patient population. Pilot studies 11,13 have shown that use of HFNC in the paediatric wards to be safe. There are both fi scal and logistic implications for reducing or shortening PICU admissions. A paediatric acute respiratory intervention study, 20 which is a multicentre randomised control trial is currently underway which should hopefully answer the question of applicability of HFNC in the paediatric wards and its health cost implications.

Our study is limited by its inherent nature of being retrospective in design. We risk confounding our results by aberrant patterns in viral virulence during the years selected. This was also a single centre study, hence our experience may not mirror those of other units employing HFNC in their patient population.

Our study was also uncontrolled for other interventions. The use of nebulised hypertonic saline was introduced at a similar time period to HFNC and could have confounded the bene fi t we observed with HFNC implementation. However, recent studies have shown con fl icting results with nebulised hypertonic saline, 21 -23 and there is no evidence that nebulised hypertonic saline reduces the rate of intubation in patients with bronchiolitis. The introduction of Standardised Paediatric Observation Charts and Between the Flags calling criteria as well as the development of the PICU outreach service during the time frame of the study could have in fl uenced ICU referral and potentially affected our fi ndings.

Conclusion

We have found HFNC for children with bronchiolitis to be safe and effective and associated with a reduction in hospital length of stay for patients admitted to PICU during their stay. Utilisation of HFNC with fl ow rates ≤ 1 L/kg/min outside the PICU did not reduce admission rates of bronchiolitis patients to the PICU in our institution. The introduction of HFNC has been associated with a decrease in the need for intubation in this subset of patients, with a trend towards shorter PICU stay. Infants less than 6 months of age or those with RSV-bronchiolitis were more likely to fail HFNC therapy, but most managed with NIV support.
