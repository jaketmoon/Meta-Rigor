TRACHEAL INTUBATION IN CRITICAL CARE TRANSPORT: GLOBAL CONSENSUS QUALITY METRIC PERFORMANCE

Michael Bigham, Hamilton Schwartz, Michael Gothard, Megan Gothard, Patrick Parrish

Learning Objectives: Tracheal intubation (TI) is a life-saving critical care skill and failed TI attempts can harm patients. Critical care transport (CCT) teams function as the 1st point of critical care contact for patients being transported to tertiary medical centers for specialized surgical, medical or trauma care. The Ground and Air Medical qUality in T ransport (GAMUT) Quality Improvement Collaborative uses a quality metric database to track CCT quality metric performance, including TI. We sought to describe TI amongst GAMUT participants. Methods: GAMUT database is a global, voluntary database for tracking consensus quality metric performance amongst CCT programs performing neonatal(neo), pediatric(ped), and adult(adlt) transports. The TI-specific quality metrics are 1st attempt TI success and definitive airway sans hypoxia/hypotension on 1st attempt (DASH1A). The 2015 GAMUT database was queried and analysis included patient age, program type, and intubation success. Analysis included simple statistics and Pearson chi-square with Bonferroni adjusted post-hoc z tests (significance=p<0.05). Results: 85,704 patient contacts were included [neo n(%)=12,664(14.8%), ped n(%)=28,992(33.8%), adlt n(%)=44,048(51.4%)] with 4,036(4.7%) TI attempts. 1st attempt TI success was lowest in neos [59.3%, 617 attempts], better in peds [81.7%, 519 attempts], and best in adlts [87%, 2900 attempts], p<0.001. Adult-focused CCT teams had higher overall 1st attempt TI success vs. non-adult teams (86.9% vs 63.5%, p<0.001) and higher ped 1st attempt TI success (86.5% vs. 75.3%, p<0.001). DASH1A rates were lower across all patient types [neo rate in %=51.9%, ped=74.3%, adlt=79.8%]. Stratification of performers (z±1.645) identified a single high-performing and 2 lowperforming CCT teams using weighted average for expected TI success vs. observed TI success based on patient population. Conclusions: TI is common in CCT, with higher rates of TI and DASH1A success in adult patients and adult-focused CCT teams. Identifying factors influencing TI success amongst high performers should influence best practice strategies for CCT TI.

USE OF EXTERNAL NASAL DILATOR AS AN ADJUVANT TO HIGH-FLOW NASAL CANNULA OXYGEN THERAPY IN CHILDREN

Anil Swayampakula, Jim Eguchi, David Juma, Michele Wilson, Janeth Ejike

Learning Objectives: Acute bronchiolitis is one of the most common diagnoses in children admitted to PICU. Although use of High Flow Nasal Cannula (HFNC) has been previously assessed among children with acute bronchiolitis, additional effects of External Nasal Dilators (ENDs) have never been evaluated. The primary outcome of this study was to compare Modified Bronchiolitis Severity Scores (MBSS) among children treated with ENDs in addition to HFNC versus HFNC oxygen therapy alone in the management of acute bronchiolitis. The secondary outcomes included the need for intubation, length of PICU stay and length of hospital stay comparisons between the two groups. Methods: All children age < 2 years admitted to Loma Linda University Children's Hospital ICU between August 2015 to July 2016 requiring HFNC therapy for acute bronchiolitis were randomly assigned to HFNC + ENDs or HFNC alone. Patients needing immediate intubation, with diseases affecting respiration other than bronchiolitis, chronic lung disease with oxygen dependence, chronic respiratory failure and post cardiac surgery patients were excluded. A spline linear mixed effect model was used to compare the change in MBSS scores over time between the two treatment arms. All tests were two-sided and conducted at significance level α = 0.05. Results: A total of 55 participants (28 in HFNC + END group, 27 in HFNC group) were enrolled to meet adequate (80%) power. The decrease in MBSS scores over time was significantly faster for HFNC + END compared to HFNC alone (p value <0.001), with the strongest decline observed within the first 8 hours, -10.85 (95% CI: -13.82 to -8.28) versus -2.66 (95% CI: -5.23 to -0.09). Number of intubations in HFNC + END and HFNC groups were 0 and 5 respectively. In contrast, there was no significant difference between the two groups in regards to PICU length of stay and hospital length of stay. Conclusions: ENDs significantly accelerated the decline in MBSS among children with acute bronchiolitis, and should be considered at the time of HFNC initiation. Additionally, use of ENDS was also associated with decreased rate of intubations.

PREVALENCE AND OUTCOMES OF HYPEROXIA IN THE INTENSIVE CARE UNIT

Shahzad Shaefi, Ariel Mueller, Valerie Banner-Goodspeed, Sajid Shahul, Todd Sarge, Jonathan Sevransky, Daniel Talmor

Learning Objectives: Oxygen is typically titrated to avoid potentially injurious periods of hypoxemia. The same attention has not been afforded for levels of relative hyperoxia with the assumption that excess oxygen is relatively harmless. Emerging clinical data suggest that this may not be true. We examined the prevalence of hyperoxia in a large ICU database and its relationship to mortality, hypothesizing that mortality would be highest in hypoxic and hyperoxic patient populations. Methods: 6,179 patients across 59 ICUs were prospectively observed in the United States, on which center characteristics and patient-level data was collected on newly admitted patients one day each week, as part of the Critical Illness and Injury T rials Group Critical Illness Outcomes Study (USCIITG CIOS). For this secondary analysis, patients were stratified according to PaO2: hypoxia (<60mmHg), normoxia (60-99mmHg), mild hyperoxia (100-149mmHg), moderate hyperoxia (150-249mmHg) and severe hyperoxia (>250mmHg). Results: Overall, the mortality rate in this cohort was 24% with a prevalence of hyperoxia of 52%. There was a significant difference in mortality across groups, persisting after accounting for in-hospital clustering (p=0.01). Using normoxia as the reference group, the ORs (95% CI) for mortality were as follows: hypoxemia 1.34 (1.08-1.66), mild hyperoxia 0.96 (0.77-1.20), moderate hyperoxia 1.00 (0.73-1.37), and severe hyperoxia 0.82 (0.59-1.14). Of note, when controlling for administration of oxygen among patients with moderate and severe hyperoxia (PaO2>150mmHg), those with a higher FiO2 ( ≥ 0.6) had a higher odds of death (OR 1.57, 95% CI: 1.03-2.39; p-value 0.03) than those with lower FiO2. Conclusions: We found a high prevalence of hyperoxia (52%) in this dataset of ICU patients. There was an association between hypoxemic PaO2 levels and mortality but no association was seen with increasing levels of hyperoxia. However, there may be trend toward increased mortality in those who are hyperoxic but exposed to a higher FiO2 level. Further work is needed in order to elucidate the true relationship between hyperoxia and mortality.
