High-fl ow oxygen therapy in acute bronchiolitis

High-fl ow nasal cannula oxygen (HFNCO) is a relatively novel medical device rapidly becoming the standard of care in hospital management of acute viral bronchiolitis. HFNCO has perceived benefi ts-so far undemonstrated 1 -for patient comfort, lung mechanics, and gas exchange, 2 but higher associated unit costs than does standard oxygen therapy. An undefi ned clinical role for HFNCO in bronchiolitis has resulted in variable clinical management-mirroring that seen following the ubiquitous introduction of oxygen-saturation monitoring in the 1980s, which is only now being unraveled. 3,4

Elizabeth Kepreotes and colleagues are to be congratulated for bringing the fi rst known randomised controlled trial 5 among children of HFNCO compared with standard low-fl ow nasal cannula oxygen (standard therapy) in acute bronchiolitis-a common condition with no eff ective treatment and very limited evidence for best practice in standard care. 6 Almost unavoidably the trial was open label, and although baseline triage oxygen saturation suggests the study population had relatively mild disease, the subsequent hospital clinical course appears consistent with that seen in many countries: oxygen saturation at baseline frequently falls once children are settled in hospital. 7

The trial did not achieve its primary outcome of time to weaning off oxygen-ie, the time from randomisation to the fi rst sustained room-air observation after oxygen. Kepreotes and colleagues used historical data to derive study size estimates for this outcome, and introduced a tightly defi ned oxygen-weaning procedure (defi ned prior to the trial commencing) to reduce variance in clinical management. Within the trial, this procedure would also minimise performance bias-ie, the risk of diff erential treatment decisions and assessments infl uencing trial outcomes-given that treatments were unmasked. However, the independent eff ect of the weaning procedure appears signifi cant across both arms, as median time to weaning off oxygen reduced from a historical 38 h to just 24 h for the standard therapy group (greater than the estimated eff ect of the intervention). The oxygen weaning procedure appears to be a low-cost, clinically effi cient method of reducing health-care burden in acute bronchiolitis and deserving of assessment in a future multicentre study.

The trial also used treatment failure as an outcome, with defi nitions incorporating physiological parameter thresholds. Similar physiological parameters are found in paediatric early warning scores, and are commonly used across hospital paediatrics. These parameters are able to predict adverse outcomes when applied retrospectively, 8 although their ability to infl uence morbidity and mortality has been diffi cult to demonstrate in practice. 9 In bronchiolitis, with no agreed standardisation, appropriate defi nitions linked to clinical outcomes are yet to be determined.

Although not powered for key secondary outcomes, the trial results help to hone the debate as to where in care pathways HFNCO might best be placed and provide insight as to the potential physiological eff ects of HFNCO. Admissions to the intensive care unit were similar in both groups, although again the study had insuffi cient power to detect diff erences and these fi ndings need further testing. HFNCO was provided (per protocol) to 32 children who were deteriorating in the standard therapy group, 20 of whom did not subsequently require critical care. This model of care seems clinically the most effi cient, as standard therapy appears to have been eff ective in 67% of children in the standard therapy group, and time to discharge was the same in both groups. The study therefore supports the perspective that HFNCO could be an eff ective intermediary therapy, capable of bridging standard and critical care. An important point of discussion for HFNCO is which part of the therapy delivers potential benefi t: the warmth, higher humidity, potential for higher oxygen concentration delivery, or a de-facto continuous positive pressure benefi t? Although previous studies have addressed physiological outcomes, 2,11 Kepreotes and colleagues' trial provides some further evidence. In the standard therapy group, 33 children experienced treatment failure compared with 14 in the HFNCO group. However, an inability to maintain oxygen saturation greater than 90%-an important threshold 3 -was the reason for treatmen t failure in just one of the children receiving standard therapy. This result suggests that the capability of HFNCO to provide a higher concentration of oxygen than does standard therapy was not the principal benefi t of transfer to HFNCO in the 20 children who subsequently improved without the need for critical care.

Kepreotes and colleagues' study 5 contributes helpful evidence as to where HFNCO might be placed in the care pathway for acute bronchiolitis. However, questions remain about the generalisability of the technology across health-care systems and to alternative manufacturers' devices with diff erent fl ow characteristics; how to predict and identify non-responders; and whether patients with diff erent phenotypes of bronchiolitis will have diff erential benefi t. 12
