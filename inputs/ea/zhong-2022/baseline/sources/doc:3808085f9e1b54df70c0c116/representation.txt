High fl ow in children with respiratory failure: A randomised controlled pilot trial -A paediatric acute respiratory intervention study

Donna Franklin, BN MBA PhD , 1,2,3 Deborah Shellshear, BSc, MBBS, FRACP, 2,4,5 Franz E Babl, MD MPH FAAP FRACP FACEP , 2,6,7,8 Rikki Hendrickson, BN, 4,5 Amanda Williams, BN, 2,6,7,8 Kristen Gibbons, BMaths(Hons), BInfoTech, PhD, 1,9 Kate McEnery, BN, 1 Melanie Kennedy, BN, 1,4 Trang MT Pham, BEng MEng, 1 Jason Acworth, MBBS FRACP, 2,4,5 David Levitt, MBChB, DRCOG, FRACP, 4,10 Ed Oakley, MBBS FACEM, 2,6,7,8 Andreas Schibler, MD FCICM, 1,2,4 on behalf of PARIS and PREDICT

1 The University of Queensland, Paediatric Critical Care Research Group, Child Health Research Centre, School of Medicine, South Brisbane, 3 Gold Coast University Hospital, Emergency Department Collaborative Research Group, Southport, 4 Queensland Children ' s Hospital, Brisbane, 2 PREDICT (Paediatric Research in Emergency Departments International Collaborative), Parkville, 6 Faculty of Medicine, Dentistry and Health Sciences, University of Melbourne, 7 Emergency Department, Royal Children ' s Hospital, 8 Emergency Research, Murdoch Children ' s Research Institute, Melbourne, Victoria, 5 Paediatric Emergency Department, Children ' s Health Queensland and Health Services, 9 Mater Research Institute, The University of Queensland, Brisbane and 10 Department of Paediatrics, Children ' s Health Queensland and Health Services, South Brisbane, Queensland, Australia

Aims: High- fl ow is increasingly used in children with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure (AHRF), despite limited evidence. The primary feasibility endpoint for this pilot-study was the proportion of treatment failure, secondary outcomes being intensive care unit (ICU) admissions and proportion of patients requiring escalation of care. We measured duration of hospital stay, duration of oxygen therapy and rates of ICU admission.

Methods: An open-labelled randomised controlled trial feasibility design was used in two tertiary children ' s hospitals in the emergency department and general wards. Children aged 0 -16 years with AHRF were randomised (1:1) to either high- fl ow or standard-oxygen. Children on standard-oxygen received rescue high- fl ow in general wards if failure criteria were met.

Results: Of 563 randomised, 283 received high- fl ow and 280 standard-oxygen with no adverse events. The proportion of children who failed treatment and receiving escalation of care was 11.7% (32/283 children) on high- fl ow and 18.1% (50/280 infants) on standard-oxygen (odds ratio 0.68, 95% con fi dence interval 0.38 -1.00). In children with obstructive airway disease, 9.7% on high- fl ow and 17.4% on standard-oxygen required escalation (risk-difference -7.7% percentage points; 95% con fi dence interval -14.3, -1.1); in children with non-obstructive disease no difference was observed. Neither difference in ICU admissions nor any difference in length of hospital stay was observed. Sixty percent of children who failed standard-oxygen responded to rescue high- fl ow.

Conclusion: High- fl ow outside ICU appears to be feasible in children with AHRF and the required proportion of escalation was lower compared to standard-oxygen. The trial design can be applied in a future large randomised controlled trial.

Key words: acute respiratory failure; oxygen saturation; paediatrics; reactive airways disease; respiratory distress.

What is already known on this topic

1 Nasal high- fl ow therapy has been evaluated in infants with bronchiolitis less than 12 months of age and has shown if used in hypoxaemic infants this requirement will reduce the need to escalate respiratory support.

What this paper adds

1 High- fl ow therapy is safe to use in all age groups presenting to hospital with acute respiratory illness and can be safely used in emergency care settings and on general paediatric wards.

Acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure (AHRF) as a consequence of a chest infection remains the most common reason of death in children under 5 years of age world-wide. 1 -3 While the childhood mortality due to respiratory infections has decreased in highincome countries, AHRF is the most frequent cause of hospital admission resulting in major consumption of health-care resources. 4 -7 Currently, standard-oxygen is used via nasal cannula or face mask. Excessive use of oxygen creates toxicity related to the concentration and length of exposure. 8 -10 Other than for emergency usage of 100% oxygen, it is recommended that inspired oxygen concentration (FiO2) should be carefully titrated against oxygen saturation (SpO2).

Nasal high- fl ow generates some degree of positive airway pressure, decreases the work of breathing 11 -14 and is well tolerated by sick children. 5,15 Because of its easy application and minimal cooperation of the child required, high- fl ow has become popular in paediatric emergency departments. 16 -22 High- fl ow has reduced the requirement to escalate therapy in infants (aged <12 months) with bronchiolitis. 18 The role of high- fl ow in older children with AHRF remains unclear. In view of the lack of high-grade evidence in AHRF, we performed the PARIS 2 pilot-study, in which we assessed the feasibility of a randomised controlled trial (RCT) and the safety/quality of high fl ow in general wards in AHRF (ef fi ciency endpoint). We measured as the primary feasibility endpoint, the proportion of children who failed treatment.

Methods

Study design and oversight

A randomised open labelled and controlled pilot-trial in two children ' s hospitals in Australia investigating the feasibility of highfl ow with AHRF.

Patients

Children with respiratory symptoms presenting to the emergency department were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: aged 0 -16 years, respiratory failure with oxygen requirement to maintain SpO2 ≥ 92%, and admission to hospital. The syndrome of AHRF in these patients was characterised into either obstructive (ongoing bronchodilator and/or steroid therapy) clinical diagnoses such as reactive airways (asthma) and bronchiolitis, or non-obstructive clinical diagnoses such as pneumonia, pneumonitis, acute lower respiratory tract

obstructive clinical feature, with a reduction for the need to escalate respiratory support.

Excluded were infants with bronchiolitis and aged <12 months as they were studied in our previous study. 18 Excluded were children with previous high- fl ow therapy during the current illness (> 7 days), upper airway obstruction, craniofacial malformation, critically ill requiring immediate higher level of care with invasive or non-invasive ventilation, basal skull fracture, trauma, cyanotic heart disease, home oxygen therapy, cystic fi brosis, palliative care and oncology. Deferred written consent by parents was used.

Randomisation and masking

A computer-generated randomisation sequence with a block size of 10 was used. Three strata were used, the fi rst for age: (i) 0 -1 years; (ii) >1 -<5 years; (iii) 5 -16 years; the second for obstructive and non-obstructive strata and third for participating centre. Clinicians allocated children to the obstructive strata if they considered ongoing bronchodilator and/or steroid treatment. Eligibility was assessed by the emergency physician and the intervention allocation completed by on-line randomisation treatment assignment (1:1). Masking of the allocation was not possible due to the visual differences between the two modes of oxygen delivery. While the primary outcome could not be masked, the data and outcome fi ndings remained locked in the database until all data was unlocked at the end of recruitment in December 2017.

Study interventions

Children were treated initially with the relevant management for the cause of their AHRF which could include burst/inhalation therapy for reactive airways, fl uid bolus and other medications. If AHRF (SpO2 < 92% in room air) symptoms persisted and the clinician decided to admit to hospital, then the eligible children were included and randomised. The study only prescribed the oxygen delivery methods (standard-oxygen or high- fl ow). The remaining medical treatment was according to standard protocols.

On the standard-oxygen, children received subnasal oxygen to a maximum of 4 L/min or via Hudson mask 4 -8 L/min to maintain SpO2 between 92 and 98%. Reducing or weaning of oxygen was permitted at any time to maintain the lowest oxygen delivered possible to maintain SpO2 ≥ 92%.

Study outcomes

The primary feasibility endpoint was de fi ned by the proportion of children when treatment failure of the allocated oxygen therapy occurred. Secondary feasibility endpoints included proportion of children requiring intensive care admission and escalation of care and adverse events.

Further feasibility outcomes were recruitment rate, safety and ef fi cacy of early use of high- fl ow in children presenting with AHRF in comparison to standard-oxygen. Safety was assessed by incidence of serious adverse events (pneumothorax, unexpected emergency intubations, respiratory or cardiac arrest and death before hospital discharge). Oxygen delivery method related complications were recorded which included one nose bleed and one pressure area both in the high- fl ow arm.

The length of oxygen therapy, length of hospital stay and intubation for mechanical ventilation were measured. Pre-planned subgroup analyses for primary and secondary outcomes were age, prematurity, neonatal respiratory support, previous hospital admission for respiratory disease and clinical presentation (obstructive or non-obstructive).

Escalation of care

In the standard-oxygen group, escalation to high- fl ow was offered but not mandated by the study protocol. For children in the high- fl ow group missing an equivalent general ward-based escalation therapy, all children failing high- fl ow required intensive care unit admission.

De fi nitions

Treatment failure with the requirement of escalation of care was considered if the child required escalation of respiratory support. At the point of care, the treating clinicians determined the presence of treatment failure if at least three of four the clinical criteria were met and escalation of care was required: (i) heart rate remained unchanged or increased compared to admission/enrolment observations; (ii) respiratory rate remained unchanged or increased compared to admission/enrolment observations; (iii) oxygen requirement on high- fl ow exceeded FiO2 ≥ 40% to maintain SpO2 ≥ 92% or oxygen requirement in standard-oxygen arm exceeded >2 L/min (subnasal) or > 8 L/min (Hudson mask) to maintain SpO2 ≥ 92%; (iv) hospital early warning tool necessitates a medical review and the clinician decided on an escalation of care. Both participating hospitals used comparable early warning tools to trigger escalation of care. Clinicians were allowed to escalate therapy if they were concerned for other clinical reasons. For children in the standard-oxygen group who received escalation of care, it was suggested to change to high- fl ow in the inpatient environment at the discretion of the clinician. Data on non-study treatment and medications were recorded. Enrolled patients were strati fi ed with a pragmatic and point-of-care de fi nition, which included clinically diagnosed: (i) wheeze (obstructive) and reactive airway disease; and (ii) absent wheeze (non-obstructive) and parenchymal lung disease.

Statistical analysis

Sample size

The focus of this pilot-study was to test if a difference in the proportion of children requiring escalation of care can be detected to inform a future full RCT, a statistically powered sample size calculation was not undertaken. Instead we ensured that the sample size was adequate to estimate the variability in our feasibility outcome measures (treatment failure), as well as demonstrate recruitment rate over a oneyear period (i.e. all four seasons) to capture all respiratory conditions causing AHRF.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented to report the baseline characteristics of the total study cohort strati fi ed by treatment group. The primary outcome of treatment failure, as well as categorical secondary outcomes, are analysed using logistic regression adjusting for strata variables where possible (age group, obstructive/non-obstructive, centre) to generate odds ratio with 95% con fi dence interval (CI); risk difference and 95% CI is also presented. Continuous outcome measures (hospital length of stay, intensive care unit length of stay and length of oxygen therapy) were all non-normally distributed; median and interquartile range were presented along with quantile regression. A pre-planned sub-group analysis for the primary feasibility outcome was performed for age, preexisting medical conditions such as previous respiratory support and obstructive and non-obstructive admission diagnosis. The primary analysis was by intention-to-treat. Statistical signi fi cance is set at the 0.05 level. Analyses were undertaken in StataSE version 15.1 (StataCorp Pty Ltd., College Station, TX, USA).

Ethics

The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (HREC/15/QRCH/159) and we used a consent to continue process. The study protocol was prospectively registered (ACTRN12615001305516). The trial was overseen by a steering committee and a Data Safety Monitoring Committee.

Patient and public involvement

Recruitment of patients commenced on 20 June 2016, with parents of children consenting to their child ' s involvement. This consent was performed both prospectively and as consent to continue. Parent and/or consumer involvement occurred prior to the start date with inclusion of their comments and feedback in the design of the study and how it would be performed. The consumers feedback was considered and integrated when fi nalising the protocol. Information on study outcome is offered on initiation by the parent/consumer.

Primary feasibility outcomes

Treatment failure occurred in 18.1% of patients allocated to standard-oxygen and in 11.7% in the patients allocated to highfl ow (odds ratio 0.62; 95% CI 0.38 -1.00) (Table 3).

Secondary feasibility outcomes

After escalation of care with high- fl ow, 39% of these children required admission to intensive care (Table 3). All children in the high- fl ow arm were transferred to intensive care as no alternative rescue therapy could be offered on the wards.

No serious adverse event was observed; neither pneumothoraces nor emergency intubation outside intensive care (Table 3). In the high- fl ow arm, there was one nose bleed observed, which the clinicians deemed unrelated to high- fl ow. The most common reason to escalate care was directed by clinicians based on the early warning tool.

Feasibility of intervention allocation

The vast majority of patients (94.1%) randomised to the study were started with the allocated treatment (Table 4). In the highfl ow arm, the main reason for not starting the allocated treatment was non-tolerance (5.3%). The time from presentation to enrolment was (median (IQR)): high fl ow 0.15 h (0.071, 0.25 h) and standard-oxygen 0.18 h (0.079, 0.35 h), overall 0.16 h (0.073, 0.30 h). All patients on high- fl ow received the prescribed fl ows.

Additional outcome measures

The treatment effect analysed within the three age groups was smaller (Table 5). Prematurity showed a greater treatment effect in the high- fl ow group; similarly, infants having received respiratory support in the neonatal period also had a lower rate of treatment failure in the high- fl ow group. Children who had a previous hospital admission for respiratory disease showed a signi fi cant higher ef fi cacy of high- fl ow. Children presenting with an obstructive airway disease showed a greater treatment bene fi t of high- fl ow.

The hospital length of stay was similar in both groups as was the length of oxygen therapy. All children in the standardoxygen arm (100%) were escalated to high- fl ow if required. Data analysed per protocol are shown in Tables S1 -S3 (Supporting Information).

Discussion

This large pilot RCT performed in two tertiary children ' s hospital investigated treatment feasibility of high- fl ow in AHRF. The primary feasibility data showed that overall high- fl ow reduced the proportion of children requiring escalation of care with a bene fi t in children who received respiratory support in the neonatal period and in children with history of a previous respiratory hospital admission. Ex-premature infants have an increased susceptibility to have more severe respiratory disease later in childhood, likely to impaired lung growth and maturation. 23 Children with an obstructive airway disease were more likely to bene fi t from high- fl ow therapy.

The study showed a recruitment rate of 69% of eligible patients, a high protocol adherence and a high safety pro fi le (no adverse events reported). The suggested escalation criteria appear to create a safe boundary to avoid adverse events. An increasing oxygen requirement triggered an escalation in only half of the children. There were no safety concerns for high- fl ow in emergency departments and general paediatric wards.

The trial intervention was applied for the age range between 0 and 16 years but we excluded bronchiolitis less than 12 months of age as this disease group was part of our previous RCT. 18 The age group <12 months contributed minimally to recruitment. The majority of patients (81%) were in the age group 1 -<5 years with predominately reactive airway disease.

The length of stay in hospital and length of oxygen therapy was similar in both groups. We only included children in the emergency department after they had received their initial medical treatment and the decision was made to admit. This avoided unnecessary inclusion of children with an oxygen requirement that improved rapidly after initial treatment.

Our data are re fl ective of similar observations in infants with bronchiolitis with the use of high- fl ow. 18,24 In bronchiolitis, highfl ow reduces the proportion of infants requiring escalation of care.

A large proportion of children with AHRF (60%) on standardoxygen meeting the escalation criteria were rescued with highfl ow and could be cared and managed in the paediatric wards. Any cross-over to the other study intervention remains problematic and considering the relative high ' rescue ' proportion observed in our study, this suggests that a future large RCT design may compare early versus rescue high- fl ow.

Limitations

Our data suggest that there are some potentially important outcomes to be considered for the design of a larger, de fi nitive multicentre trial. Intensive care admission rate would re fl ect an important primary outcome. From a pragmatic standpoint, clinicians would not have agreed to participate in the trial if they would not have had the option of rescue high- fl ow before intensive care admission. Therefore, we chose for this pilot-study the time point when failure of the fi rst treatment allocation occurred as the primary feasibility outcome.

Rescue high- fl ow was successful in 60% of the children. No comparable rescue therapy was offered in the high- fl ow group and hence the number of intensive care admissions in the highfl ow arm was higher than on the standard-oxygen.

In a non-blinded trial, there is the potential of bias by triggering early escalation particularly on the standard-oxygen. The reason for escalation based on the selected criteria was not different between standard-oxygen and high- fl ow.

Conclusion

This pilot-study showed the feasibility of the trial design and demonstrated that high- fl ow for AHRF can be safely used in emergency departments and general wards. With the high within trial acceptance of rescue high- fl ow for treatment failure, other important primary outcome measures than intensive care admission need to be considered for a full trial.
