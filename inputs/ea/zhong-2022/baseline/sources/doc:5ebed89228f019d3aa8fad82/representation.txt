Pilot study of vapotherm oxygen delivery in moderately severe bronchiolitis

Heated humidifi ed high fl ow nasal cannula (HHHFNC) therapy is becoming increasingly used in preterm infants and there is evidence that it can provide some degree of respiratory support. 1 2 We designed a prospective, randomised, open pilot study to assess the safety and feasibility of using HHHFNC therapy in infants with bronchiolitis.

We recruited infants with a clinical diagnosis of moderately severe bronchiolitis. Following parental informed consent, infants were randomised to head-box oxygen or the Vapotherm 2000i (Vapotherm Inc, Stevensville, Maryland, USA). Initial vapotherm fl ow was 4 l/min with 100% oxygen and was increased up to 8 l/min if tolerated. Oxygen concentration was adjusted in both groups to achieve target pulse oximeter oxygen saturation (SpO 2 ) of 92-96%. Vapotherm was continued at high fl ow for at least 24 h; the fl ow rate was then decreased sequentially and switched to dry oxygen once 2 l/min. The primary outcome measure was SpO 2 at 8 h post-randomisation; other clinical parameters were recorded at intervals up to 48 h.

Nineteen infants were randomised (median age 3.0 months, range 0.3-11.3 months), of which eleven received vapotherm and eight continued in head-box oxygen. All infants on vapotherm tolerated it well and no infant needed further respiratory support. Median SpO 2 was higher in the vapotherm group at 8h (100% vs 96%, p=0.04) and 12 h (99% vs 96%, p=0.04), but similar at 24 h. The fractional inspired oxygen concentration was higher in the vapotherm group at all three time points. Parameters at other time points, including the time to switch to dry nasal cannula oxygen, the total time in oxygen, time to feed, time to discharge and total length of stay, were similar between groups.

This study is obviously small, the intervention unblinded and we had no formal measure of pulmonary mechanics or gas exchange other than SpO 2 . To show even a large reduction in the need for further respiratory support would need a study with over 100 infants in each arm. However, a retrospective review has described a 68% reduction in the rate of intubation of infants with bronchiolitis following introduction of HHHFNC therapy. 3 There are a number of mechanisms through which HHHFNC could provide respiratory support, including washout of the nasopharyngeal deadspace, matching inspiratory fl ow rates and, potentially, a distending pressure comparable with continuous positive airway pressure. 4

Since this study, the vapotherm has become popular with our nursing staff as a method of delivering oxygen to infants with respiratory infection, not least because it allows easier nursing care and is easy to use. There may also be a perception that it offers respiratory support. It is, however, a relatively expensive medical device with an ongoing cost for consumables. We are also concerned that HHHFNC therapy with high concentrations of oxygen might provide falsely reassuring oxygenation in sick infants who are in fact tiring. While HHHFNC therapy appears a safe and feasible therapy in infants with moderately severe bronchiolitis, we would urge some caution before its widespread use. Whether it offers respiratory support should undergo further investigation.
