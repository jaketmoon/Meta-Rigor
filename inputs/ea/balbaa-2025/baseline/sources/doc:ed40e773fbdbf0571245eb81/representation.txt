The TONIC Trial

Madjid Boukantar, MD, a, * Paul-Matthieu Chiaroni, MD, a, * Romain Gallet, MD, PHD, a Patrick Zamora, MD, a Tony Truong, MD, a Andrea Mangiameli, MD, a Laura Rostain, MD, a Anne-Sophie Tuffreau-Martin, MD, a Pierre-André Natella, MD, b Nadia Oubaya, MD, PHD, c,d Emmanuel Teiger, MD, PHD a

ABSTRACT

BACKGROUND Fasting before coronary procedures is currently recommended to reduce complications despite the lack of scienti fi c evidence.

OBJECTIVES The TONIC (Comparison Between Fasting and No Fasting Before Interventional Coronary Intervention on the Occurrence of Adverse Events) noninferiority trial investigated the safety and comfort of a nonfasting strategy (ad libitum food and drinks) vs traditional fasting ( > 6 hours for solid food and liquids) before coronary procedures.

METHODS In this monocentric, prospective, single-blind randomized controlled trial, 739 patients undergoing coronary procedures were included and randomized to a fasting or a nonfasting strategy. Emergency procedures were excluded. The primary endpoint was a composite of vasovagal reaction, hypoglycemia (de fi ned by blood sugar # 0.7 g/L), and isolated nausea and/or vomiting. Noninferiority margin was 4%. Secondary endpoints were contrast-induced nephropathy and patients ' satisfaction.

RESULTS Among the 739 procedures (697 elective and 42 semiurgent), 517 angiographies, and 222 angioplasties (including complex and high-risk procedures) were performed. The primary endpoint occurred in 30 of 365 nonfasting patients (8.2%) vs 37 of 374 fasting patients (9.9%), demonstrating noninferiority (absolute between-group difference, [UNMAPPED_GLYPH U+0000] 1.7%; 1-sided 95% CI upper limit: 1.8%). No food-related adverse event occurred, and contrast-related acute kidney injuries were similar between groups. Overall, procedure satisfaction and perceived pain were similar in both groups, but nonfasting patients reported less hunger and thirst ( P < 0.01). In case of redo coronary procedures, most patients (79%) would choose a nonfasting strategy.

CONCLUSIONS The TONIC randomized trial demonstrates the noninferiority of a nonfasting strategy to the usual fasting strategy for coronary procedures regarding safety, while improving patients ' comfort. F asting has been a cornerstone of general anesthesia since Mendelson fi rst published in 1940 an increased risk of vomiting and aspiration pneumonia, although low, 1 in nonfasting patients. In current practice, this precaution often applies also to exams performed under local anesthesia. In the setting of coronary procedures under local anesthesia with moderate sedation, patients are usually kept fasting for a minimum of 6 hours for solid food and 2 hours for clear liquids, as this is thought to reduce those risks. Preprocedural fasting is recommended by the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) 2021 guidelines, although they acknowledge that data supporting this practice are missing. 2 Major complications of percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) requiring intubation are very rare, with an incidence of emergency cardiac surgery between 0.15% and 0.4%, 3,4 and under 1% for cardiac arrest. 5 On the other side, fasting could be associated with adverse outcomes such as hypoglycemia, insulin resistance, acute renal injury caused by dehydration, and patient discomfort, while increasing periprocedural pain. 6,7

In the fi eld of interventional neuroradiology procedures, also performed under local anesthesia, no differences in safety were found between fasting and nonfasting patients in a large prospective nonrandomized study. 8 In another retrospective study, longer fasting time was associated with higher incidence of vasovagal reaction. 9 However, coronary intervention differs from those procedures. In the setting of coronary procedures, retrospective studies 7,10,11 found no differences regarding adverse outcomes between fasting and nonfasting patients. The randomized CHOW NOW (Can we Safely Have Our Patients Eat With Cardiac Catheterization - Nix Or alloW) trial was designed to address that question but has not been published to date. More recently, a small, randomized trial showed that a ' heart-healthy diet ' before a coronary procedure improved patient satisfaction. 12 However, the lack of a well-designed published randomized study regarding coronary procedures safety is still hindering changes in daily practice. 2 Besides improving patients ' comfort and satisfaction, such changes in practice would also prevent procedures cancelation caused by nonfasting patients, and thus improve catheterization laboratories ef fi ciency.

The TONIC (Comparison Between Fasting and No Fasting Before Interventional Coronary Intervention on the Occurrence of Adverse Events) randomized trial aimed to demonstrate the noninferiority of a nonfasting strategy to the current fasting strategy for patients undergoing coronary procedures regarding safety outcomes.

METHODS

STUDY DESIGN AND SETTINGS. This is a prospective, 2-arm parallel-group, random-

Patients were informed about the study after consenting to the coronary angiography procedure, and written informed consent was obtained after detailing all processes of the study before inclusion. This study was approved by an ethics committee (CPP -Ile-de-France VII), and was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki, good clinical practice, and French legislation on clinical research. This trial was registered under ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT04116346.

STUDY POPULATION. All patients 18 years of age or older who were planned to undergo an elective or semiurgent coronary procedure (angiography and/or PCI) were screened. On-site cardiac surgery allowed complex and high-risk procedures to be performed, which were also included. Patients presenting hemodynamic instability, coronary emergency, pregnancy or breastfeeding, those protected by law (under guardianship or curatorship), those with artifi cial nutrition or requiring fasting for a medical reason and those undergoing a noncoronary procedure at the same time (for example percutaneous aortic valvular replacement or right-heart catheterization) were excluded. Also, patients couldn ' t be included twice in the trial.

PROCEDURE. The coronary procedure (angiography and/or PCI) was performed in both groups as usual, according to routine practice and current guidelines. Local anesthesia using subcutaneous lidocaine 1% (1 to 20 mL) was administered around the arterial access site. After sheath introduction, intra-arterial vasodilators (2.5-mg verapamil and 1-mg isosorbide dinitrate) and unfractionated heparin (3,000 IU) were administered (radial access only). Additional heparin (total of 50-100 IU/kg intravenous [IV]) was administered if PCI or fractional fl ow reserve measurements were performed. The contrast agent used was iodixanol (Visipaque 320, GE Healthcare), an iso-osmolar contrast agent.

RANDOMIZATION AND BLINDING. Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio, to either a fasting group (control group) or a nonfasting group (experimental group). The randomization lists were computergenerated by an independent statistician using a variable block size and with strati fi cation on insulindependent diabetes. The randomization was carried out at baseline on the electronic Case Report Form software, Cleanweb (Telemedicine Technologies).

The ward staff (doctors and nurses) were unblinded to group allocation of patients and informed by the investigator to ensure availability of food and drinks for nonfasting patients. The interventional staff (cardiologists and nurses performing the procedure) was blinded to the group allocation. The investigator was unblinded but uninvolved in treatment, conducted the postprocedural questionnaire and monitored the adherence to blinding.

INTERVENTIONS. All patients undergoing nonurgent coronary procedures were told to fast for at least 6 hours for both solid food and liquids, which is routine practice. Patients allocated to the nonfasting group were allowed to eat and drink at their convenience (usually with standard meals and drinks provided by the hospital), whereas patients in the fasting group were not. All patients were allowed to eat and drink after the procedure.

DATA COLLECTION AND FOLLOW-UP. Baseline characteristics (sociodemographic data, medical background, treatments and procedure indication) were collected by ward physicians. Biological and echographic data reported were the most recent available ( < 7 days). Last solid and liquid food intake were reported by the ward staff before the patient left for the coronary procedure. Fasting times were calculated from the last food intake to the start of the procedure. All periprocedural data (including medications and complications) were collected by the interventional staff. Heart rate and invasive blood pressure were measured after introduction of the arterial sheath and before removal. Arterial blood samples were taken at the beginning and at the end of the coronary procedure to assess blood glucose.

Patients were monitored for at least 4 hours after the procedure to assess complications and primary criteria endpoints. Satisfaction questionnaires were given to patients, self-completed after the procedure, and collected before hospital discharge. They were asked simple questions regarding overall satisfaction, hunger, thirst, periprocedural pain, and feeding preference (see the Supplemental Appendix for full questionnaires). At day 3, blood tests were systematically prescribed to assess serum creatinine level. At day 7, patients were called to search for adverse events, including acute kidney injury and pneumonia (fever, dyspnea, and/or cough).

STATISTICS AND OUTCOMES. As this trial aimed to determine whether allowing food and liquid intake before elective or semiurgent coronary angiography was noninferior to fasting in terms of safety and well-being of patients, the primary endpoint was a composite of vasovagal reaction, hypoglycemia (de fi ned by blood sugar # 0.7 g/L), and isolated nausea and/or vomiting occurring up to the end of the 4 hours postprocedure monitoring. Vasovagal reaction was de fi ned by 2 of the 3 following criteria, in absence of another cause: 1) nausea, vomiting, paleness, or sweating; 2) low systolic blood pressure < 100 mm Hg (or drop of > 15% if already lower than 100 mm Hg); 3) low heart rate < 60 beats/min (or drop > 15% if already lower than 60 beats/min).

Safety and well-being of patients were also assessed, with secondary endpoints: contrastinduced acute kidney injury (increase in serum creatinine > 25% or > 44 m mol/L), aspiration pneumonia (de fi ned by respiratory symptoms and fever within 7-day follow-up), and patient satisfaction (according to the questionnaire). The procedure complications were also reported: local hematoma, coronary dissection or thrombosis, perforation, cardiac tamponade, and death.

Based on the literature data, assuming a percentage of the primary outcome (vasovagal reaction, hypoglycemia, or isolated nausea and/or vomiting) of 5% in the fasting and nonfasting groups, the analysis of 736 patients would yield 80% power, with a 1-tailed alpha risk of 5%, to show the noninferiority of the nonfasting strategy to the usual fasting strategy before the procedure, with regard to the occurrence of the primary outcome, considering a noninferiority margin of 4%. A margin of 4% was considered based on expert consensus within our steering committee, who deemed it acceptable considering the primary outcome, which did not encompass mortality or other serious adverse events. Thus, noninferiority was de fi ned as the upper bound of the 1-tailed 95% CI of the absolute between-group difference in the percentage of patients reaching the primary outcome lying below the threshold of 4%.

Baseline characteristics in the 2 arms were reported descriptively using mean  SD or median (Q1-Q3) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Data of the intervention and the procedure were described in the same fashion. The analysis of the primary ef fi cacy outcome and of the criteria of this primary composite outcome were performed in the per-protocol and modifi ed intention-to-treat (mITT) populations. The perprotocol population was de fi ned as the population who complied with the intervention, that is excluding patients from the fasting group who did not fast for at least 6 hours. The mITT population is de fi ned as all the randomized patients who underwent the coronary procedure. The 1-sided 95% CIs of the absolute between-group differences were calculated. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for insulin-dependent diabetes (ie, the criterion on which randomization was strati fi ed) were performed. RESULTS

PATIENTS AND PROCEDURES DATA. Between May 2020 and July 2021, 2,252 patients underwent a nonurgent coronary procedure in our center, and 755 patients (outpatients, n ¼ 507; inpatients, n ¼ 248) were included in the study and randomized: 379 to the fasting group and 376 to the nonfasting group. Baseline characteristics of the patients were similar in the 2 groups ( Table 1 , Supplemental Table S1);

569 (75%) were men, mean age was 68 years, mean body mass index 27 kg/m 2 , 376 (50%) were smokers or past smokers, 219 (29%) diabetic, 543 (72%) had hypertension, and 441 (58%) dyslipidemia. Of the 755 patients included, 16 had their procedure cancelled (no indication according to the interventional cardiologist) and were excluded from the analyses ( Figure 1 ).

Characteristics of the 739 procedures are presented in Table 2 (and Supplemental Table S2). Overall, 697 (94%) were elective, whereas 42 (6%) were non -STsegment elevation myocardial infarction, and 517 (70%) were only coronary angiography (  fractional fl ow reserve measurements), whereas 222 (30%) included a PCI (planned PCI, n ¼ 68, ad hoc PCI, n ¼ 154). Of the 222 PCI performed, 24 (11%) were chronic total occlusions, 7 (3%) had high calcium burden requiring rotational atherectomy, and 21 (9%) included treatment of the left main coronary artery. Also, 58 procedures (8%) had a femoral access, and 212 PCI (95%) were successful. Periprocedural conscious sedation was performed for 155 patients (21%) (midazolam IV, n ¼ 151 or droperidol IV, n ¼ 4), with no unbalance between groups. Starting blood glucose was lower in the fasting group (0.97 g/L vs 1.16 g/L, respectively).

FASTING TIMES. Mean fasting times were longer in the fasting group compared with the nonfasting group (15 hours vs 3 hours, respectively), as shown in Table 3 . In the fasting group, 14 patients (4%) misunderstood the instructions and did not fast ( Figure 1 ). In the nonfasting group, 82 (22%) and 68 (19%) patients had fasting times $ 6 hours for solid food and liquids, respectively, mostly because of delay between the procedure and the last opportunity to eat, although 66 patients (18%) fasted for personal convenience.

PRIMARY OUTCOME. As shown in Figure 2 (and Supplemental Table S3), in the modi fi ed intention-totreat population, a primary outcome event occurred in 30 of 365 patients (8.2%) in the nonfasting group and in 37 of 374 patients (9.9%) in the fasting group (absolute difference: [UNMAPPED_GLYPH U+0000] 1.7%, upper limit of the 1-sided 95% CI: 1.8%). This upper bound of the 1-sided 95% CI of the absolute difference in the primary outcome was below the prespeci fi ed 4% noninferiority margin. Results were consistent in the per-protocol population (absolute difference: [UNMAPPED_GLYPH U+0000] 2.1%, upper limit of the 1-sided 95% CI: 1.5%) (Supplemental Table S3). We also showed noninferiority of the nonfasting group to the fasting group with regard to the individual components of the primary composite outcome, that is, hypoglycemia, isolated nausea and/or vomiting, and vasovagal reaction taken separately, in both mITT and per-protocol populations. Vasovagal reaction occurred in 5.4% in the fasting group vs 2.7% in the nonfasting group. A total of 41 patients (6%) presented hypoglycemia (22 in the fasting group), but only 4 ( < 1%) had symptoms (3 in the fasting group) and required management. In subgroup analyses according to diabetes, sex, age, hospitalization, use of moderate sedation, and the type of procedure ( Figure 3 , Supplemental Table S4), the effect of the nonfasting intervention was consistent regardless of subgroup, with no signi fi cant interaction (all P values for interaction $ 0.05). SECONDARY OUTCOMES. During follow-up, blood works at day 3 were retrieved for 484 (65%) patients, and 668 (90%) patients answered the phone call at day 7. No differences between groups for contrastinduced acute kidney injury and pneumonia were found in safety analyses ( Table 4 ). No adverse events related to food intake occurred.

Regarding patients ' well-being, satisfaction questionnaires were completed by 720 patients (97%). As shown in Table 5 (and Supplemental Table S6), no differences were found for overall procedure satisfaction nor procedure perceived pain. However, patients in the nonfasting group were satis fi ed for having free access to food and drinks and felt less hungry and less thirsty ( P < 0.0001) than in the fasting group. Also, most patients would choose a nonfasting strategy if they had to undergo the same procedure in the future ( P < 0.0001).

DISCUSSION

The TONIC trial assessed the impact of a nonfasting strategy compared with the usual fasting strategy regarding safety and well-being for patients undergoing elective or semiurgent coronary procedures. We demonstrated, in both modi fi ed intention-to-treat and per-protocol populations, that a nonfasting strategy was noninferior to the usual fasting strategy before coronary procedures with regard to the occurrence of a composite primary outcome comprising vasovagal reaction, hypoglycemia, and isolated nausea and/or vomiting, and of the occurrence of each of the individual components of the primary composite outcome. Regarding safety, no adverse events related to food and liquid intake occurred, and no differences were observed regarding aspiration pneumonia and contrast-induced acute kidney injuries. Overall, patients were satis fi ed in both groups, and the majority of them would prefer free access to food and drinks if they had to undergo another procedure in the future.

OVERALL SAFETY. Procedure safety has always been 1 of the main concerns of the operators. Highosmolality contrast agents used in the past induced nausea and vomiting in almost 25% of patients. 13 Moreover, complications requiring general anesthesia and intubation were more likely to occur at the initial development of interventional cardiology. For this reason and despite little evidence supporting it, fasting has always been recommended to reduce the risk of pulmonary aspiration in such scenario. Nowadays, those risks are very low for nonemergency procedures. 3,4,5,7 Moreover, newer nonionic contrast agents induce mild nausea in only 3% of patients. 13 Our results con fi rm the very low incidence of pulmonary aspiration (n ¼ 2, 0.3%) and of nausea/ vomiting (n ¼ 12, 1.6%) but also of major adverse events requiring intubation: only 1 (0.3%) patient suffered a coronary perforation complicated by tamponade and cardiac arrest. This very low frequency of adverse events is likely to be the main reason for the absence of difference between nonfasting and fasting strategies observed in previously published retrospective and nonrandomized studies. 7,10,11

FASTING SAFETY. In the fi eld of interventional electrophysiology, the study of Bode et al 14 randomized 201 patients planned for cardiac implantable electronic device procedures under local anesthesia and moderate sedation to a fasting vs nonfasting strategy. The results reported better patients ' wellbeing with no adverse safety outcomes in the nonfasting group. In the fi eld of coronary interventions, the results of the CHOW NOW trial presented as an abstract were promising but never published in a peer-reviewed journal, preventing any practice change, as stated in the SCAI 2021 guidelines. 2 More recently, a smaller randomized trial compared a ' heart-healthy ' diet (speci fi ed diet low in fats and acidity) vs fasting and demonstrated improved patient satisfaction in the nonfasting group, 12 but was underpowered to address safety. Moreover, patients in the nonfasting group were assigned to a speci fi c diet, which could be too restrictive to implement in daily practice.

The TONIC trial was, however, designed to assess safety outcomes. Its major strength is that its population truly re fl ects real-life patients and procedures, only excluding emergency procedures (which are not fasting per se): outand inpatients, elective, semiurgent, complex and high-risk procedures (left main artery lesions, chronic total occlusions, high calcium burden requiring rotational atherectomy and bifurcation lesions) were all included. Therefore, our results highlight that a nonfasting strategy is safe for virtually every patient undergoing coronary procedures (even the ones deemed at-risk).

PATIENTS ' WELL-BEING. As expected, nonfasting patients were signi fi cantly less hungry and less thirsty than fasting patients. For future procedures, most patients (79%) would prefer free access to food and drinks over fasting, with higher values (87% vs 71%; P < 0.0001) in the nonfasting group, probably because they just experienced improved comfort without added risks. However, fasting did not in fl uence global patient satisfaction and perceived pain of the procedure, as reported in previous studies. 14,16 This reinforces the idea that what probably matters is not the food intake in itself but patients ' beliefs and perception of the waiting time and the procedure. If a patient is convinced fasting is better, forcing him to eat would probably be detrimental to his well-being and likely to induce a nocebo effect. Thus, patients should be informed that eating is safe, and then be free to decide.

STUDY LIMITATIONS. First, this was a monocentric study and results may need to be con fi rmed in larger multicentric randomized studies with variety of local practices. Second, among the 41 biological hypoglycemia cases observed, only 4 (10%) were associated with symptoms and required speci fi c glucose treatment. However, whether we consider hypoglycemia or symptomatic hypoglycemia, noninferiority of the nonfasting intervention was present. Third, a very low (but still present, 4%) number of patients in the fasting group did not comply with the instructions and ate food. Although these crossovers may have changed the results of the study, a per-protocol analysis was planned and found similar results. Also, fasting times were longer than expected in both groups, with mean fasting times of 3 hours vs 15 hours in the nonfasting and in the fasting groups, respectively. Even in the nonfasting group, few patients had food or liquid intake very close to the procedure. It is, however, dif fi cult to give a precise intra-day schedule for procedures, and patients sometimes wait longer than expected. We did not ask patients for their perception of the waiting time, which might have felt longer in the fasting group. Last, a minor limitation of our study was missing data for some of the secondary outcomes: 19 satisfaction questionnaires (3%) were not retrieved, 246 blood tests (33%) at day 3 were not performed, and 71 patients (10%) were lost during follow-up at day 7. Nonetheless, there was no missing data for the primary outcome.

CONCLUSIONS

The TONIC trial demonstrates noninferiority in regard to safety of a nonfasting strategy to the routine fasting strategy for all patients undergoing elective or semi-urgent coronary procedures, with no foodrelated adverse event. A nonfasting strategy could improve patients ' comfort as well as catheterization laboratories ef fi ciency.

PERSPECTIVES

WHAT IS KNOWN? Fasting before coronary angiographies has been routine practice since the fi rst procedures in the 1960s, with severe complications requiring intubation frequently occurring. Such complications are now exceptional, but fasting is still recommended in the guidelines despite the absence of scienti fi c evidence.

WHAT IS NEW? A nonfasting strategy was noninferior to the routine fasting strategy, with similar incidence of vasovagal reaction, hypoglycemia and nausea. No food-related adverse events were reported.

WHAT IS NEXT? When implemented in routine practice, a nonfasting strategy will improve comfort and catheterization laboratories ef fi ciency with no added risks for patients.
