Aims Data on safety and ef fi cacy of a non-fasting strategy in minimal invasive cardiac procedures are lacking. We assessed a non-fasting strategy compared with a fasting strategy regarding patient ' s well-being and safety in elective cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) procedures.

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . Methods and results In this randomized, single-blinded clinical trial, 201 patients (non-fasting = 100, fasting = 101) with a mean age of 72.0 + 11.6 years (66.7% male) were assigned to a non-fasting strategy (solids/ fl uids allowed up to 1 h) or a fasting strategy (at least 6 h no solids and 2 h no fl uids) before the procedure and analysed on an intention-to-treat basis. The co-primary outcomes were patients ' well-being scores (based on numeric rating scale, 0 -10) and incidence of intra-procedural food-related adverse events, including vomiting, perioperative pulmonary aspiration, and emergency intubation. Renal, haematological, and metabolic blood parameters and 30-day follow-up data were gathered. The summed pre-procedural patients ' well-being score was signi fi cantly lower in the non-fasting group [non-fasting: 13.1 + 9.6 vs. fasting: 16.5 + 11.4, 95% con fi dence interval (CI) of mean difference (MD) -6.35 to -0.46, P = 0.029], which was mainly driven by signi fi cantly lower scores for hunger and tiredness in the non-fasting group (non-fasting vs. fasting; hunger: 0.9 + 1.9 vs. 3.1 + 3.2, 95% CI of MD -2.86 to -1.42, P , 0.001; tiredness: 1.6 + 2.3 vs. 2.6 + 2.7, 95% CI of MD -1.68 to -0.29, P = 0.023). No intra-procedural food-related adverse events were observed. Relevant blood parameters and 30-day follow-up did not show signi fi cant differences.

Conclusion

These results showed that a non-fasting strategy is bene fi cial to a fasting one regarding patient ' s well-being and comparable in terms of safety for CIED procedures (NCT04389697).

Keywords Pre-procedural fasting · Implantable cardiac de fi brillator · Pacemaker · Aspiration · Patient-reported outcome measures

What ' s new?

The Fast-CIED Study is the fi rst randomized clinical trial to assess the bene fi ts of a non-fasting compared with a fasting strategy before elective CIED procedures.

Nointra-procedural food-intake-related incidents concerning vomiting, aspiration, or emergency intubation were observed in both groups.

A non-fasting strategy is advantageous compared with a fasting strategy regarding patient ' s well-being and comparable in terms of safety.

Non-fasting before CIED procedures did not in fl uence renal, metabolic, and haematological blood parameters and duration of hospital stay.

Results of the Fast-CIED study can help to facilitate scheduling of CIED procedures and therefore allow ef fi cient patient and work fl ows.

Introduction

Pre-procedural fasting has often been practiced for about 4 -6 h before interventional cardiac procedures, such as percutaneous coronary interventions and implantation of cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs). However, extended fasting may occur when these procedures are delayed or cancelled. 1 Requiring patients to have a strict 6-h fasting window for solid food also hinders scheduling patients for earlier appointments if schedule changes.

Prolonged fasting may exacerbate the organic response to trauma by increasing insulin resistance, gluconeogenesis, and acute-phase response; it leaves patients often dissatis fi ed and adds to the discomfort and anxiety of waiting for a procedure. 2 Moreover, patients under fasting are potentially at risk of dehydration, contrast-induced nephropathy, hypoglycaemia, and poorly controlled hypertension, particularly in the older age group. 3

The primary concept of pre-operative fasting was instituted between 1855 and 1945 when anaesthetic techniques were rudimentary, and chloroform was used. Nausea, vomiting, and aspiration were feared. 4,5 However, current guidelines reveal the absence of a strong association between fasting, gastric volume, or risk of nausea, vomiting, and aspiration. 6 Pre-procedural fasting practice varies substantially between interventional cardiologists, and the available evidence to guide the correct practice is also limited. 7

Scattered evidence from observational studies shows the feasibility of pre-procedural non-fasting in patients undergoing cardiac angiography and angioplasty. 3,8 However, the feasibility and safety of fasting strategies in patients undergoing CIED procedures remain unreported. These procedures are mainly performed under local anaesthesia without or with only slight anxiolytic sedation and analgesia.

This Fast-CIED trial aims to assess a non-fasting strategy compared with a fasting strategy regarding patient ' s well-being and safety in CIED implantation procedures.

Methods

Study design and setting

This clinical trial was conducted at a high volume centre, Department of Electrophysiology of Leipzig Heart Center, Germany, between January and November 2020. It was designed as investigator-driven, prospective, parallel-group, randomized, and single-arm blinded.

Research ethics

Patients were informed about the study after consenting to the CIED procedure. This study was approved by the local ethics committee, and written consent was obtained from the patients after detailing all processes of the study before randomization. This study was performed and reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. This trial was registered with ClinicalTrial.gov, NCT04389697.

Participants

Inpatients above 18 years old who underwent elective CIED procedures (new implantation, battery exchange, and lead revision) were included. Patients with increased intra-abdominal pressure (body mass index . 40, severe intra-abdominal tumours, severe ascites), those who presented with an acute unstable condition, patients with bradycardia , 30 b.p.m. or temporary pacing wire, intubated patients, and those scheduled for deep sedation (unresponsive to vocal stimuli, tolerating an oropharyngeal airway, and breathing spontaneously) were excluded from the study.

Randomization and allocation

Patients were randomly allocated into two parallel groups (1:1 ratio), either the fasting group (control group) or the non-fasting group (intervention group), using completely opaque envelopes. The contents of the envelope were previously randomized using a randomization programme. Envelopes were organized into blocks with 20 envelopes per block. After accepting and signing the study consent form, the envelope was opened, and the patient informed about group allocation.

Blinding

The ward staff members (doctors/nurses) were unblinded to group allocation of patients and informed by the instructor to ensure availability of food and drinks for patients. The instructor assigning the groups and conducting the pre- and post-procedural standardized questionnaires was also not blinded to the group allocation but uninvolved in treatment. Surgeons and nursing staff performing the CIED procedure were blinded to the group allocation. The instructor monitored adherence to blinding. Unblinding was pre-speci fi ed in the study protocol including the need for deep sedation/intubation during the procedure and/or acute cardiorespiratory emergency per the discretion of the surgeon.

Interventions

Patients in the fasting group were asked to fast for at least 6 h for solid food and 2 h for liquids before the scheduled start of the CIED procedure. Patients in the non-fasting group were allowed to eat and drink up to 1 h before the CIED procedure, mostly until transfer to the operating site. Patients were thoroughly instructed on the details of their study protocol to keep up the blinding. Food and drinks provided were regular meals and drinks that were available at the centre. Medication intake was not in fl uenced by group allocation.

Cardiac implantable electronic device procedure

The CIED procedure was performed in both groups according to current standards, and every patient received antibiotic prophylaxis. 9,10 Local anaesthesia with 20 -40 mL Mepivacaine 1% was administered to the operation site. Depending on the patient ' s needs, additional administration of intravenous analgesics (fentanyl) and sedatives (midazolam, rarely Propofol) was possible to achieve minimal to moderate sedation with at least adequate spontaneous ventilation, maintained cardiovascular function, and purposeful response to verbal or tactile stimulation, 11 meaning that protective re fl exes were maintained. After infraclavicular incision and mostly subcutaneous pocket preparation (subpectoral preparation in cachexia or existing subpectoral device-pocket), cephalic vein or subclavian vein was used for lead access. Subcutaneous and intracutaneous absorbable sutures were used for wound closure. No de fi brillation tests in implantable cardioverter de fi brillator recipients were made. The duration of the CIED procedure was de fi ned as the time between the fi rst incision and the last suture.

Data collection

Procedural-related parameters

Baseline characteristics were collected from the patient charts. Blood samples were drawn within 24 h before (baseline) and after the CIED procedure and analysed for sodium, potassium, glucose, urea, and creatinine with calculation of glomerular fi ltration rate (GFR) according to the Modi fi cation-of-Diet-in-Renal-Disease-Study (MDRD) formula. A venous blood gas analysis was taken and analysed 30 min before incision. During the CIED procedure, patients ' blood pressure, pulse, and O2 saturation were assessed. Medications given during the CIED procedure by the nursing staff following the surgeon ' s guidelines were collected and categorized into sedatives, analgetics, and antiemetics. Any intra-procedural adverse events, including emergency intubation, perioperative pulmonary aspiration, and vomiting, were recorded.

Patient-reported outcomes

The patients were asked to quantify several score items on a numeric rating scale (NRS) with values between 0 (item not existent) and 10 (most severe expression of the item). These score items, which were about the patients ' well-being, were collected 30 min before the start of the CIED procedure and included tiredness, weakness, dizziness, anxiety, hunger, thirst, mouth dryness, headache, stomach ache, other pains, and nausea. The NRS levels were used to classify three pain categories low/moderate/severe (NRS 0 -3, 4 -5, 6 -10), respectively. These categories were also used to classify patients ' well-being scores. The presence of preprocedural vomiting was evaluated as well.

On the morning after CIED procedure, patients were asked about nausea during the CIED procedure. Post-procedural surgical-site pain at rest and during exertion, their sleep quality and overall satisfaction with the group allocation were recorded on an NRS 0 -10. Patients were also asked to report their preference for future procedures based on their current experience.

Times

Fasting time was calculated as the time from last solid food intake to the time of pre-procedural questioning, which occurred in the preparation room 30 min before fi rst incision. Time spent on the intensive care unit (ICU) and total hospital stay were also collected.

Follow-up

Thirty days after the procedure, patients were questioned by telephone or in person during check-up appointments for infections, especially respiratory ones, such as colds or pneumonia. Due to the emergence of COVID-19 during the time of patient acquisition in this study, patients whoreported any respiratory illnesses were asked for possible infections regarding COVID-19, but no patients were reported to have tested positive.

Outcomes

The fi rst co-primary endpoint was pre-procedural patients ' well-being scores. The summed patients ' well-being score was calculated as the sum score of all pre-procedural well-being scores, except for vomiting. A lower score shows a higher level of patients ' well-being.

The second co-primary endpoint was the incidence of intraprocedural adverse events, pre-speci fi ed as food-intake related like vomiting, perioperative pulmonary aspiration, and emergency intubation.

Secondary outcomes were intra-procedural medication usage, postprocedure changes in serum creatinine ( Δ creatinine) and GFR compared with pre-procedure, patients ' vital signs and blood parameters, overall satisfaction with procedure, status of sleep quality and surgical-site pain, length of ICU and hospital stay, 30-day chest infection rate, and incidence of procedural-related death.

Sample size

The sample size of was determined on the fi ndings of Itou et al ., 12 who used ' Dry mouth ' and ' Hunger ' as primary endpoints. These two endpoints were components of our well-being score. To achieve a power of 80% for detecting a difference in proportions of 0.53 and 0.20 between the two groups (test-reference group) at a two-sided P of 0.05 considering the endpoints ' Dry mouth ' and ' Hunger ' , respectively, the required sample size was estimated to be between 13 and 103 for each group.

Statistical analysis

For the co-primary endpoint of the summed well-being score, we calculated means and standard deviations, and we compared the means between the two groups with a t -test using 95% con fi dence interval (CI) for the difference in means. We also calculated P -values based on t -tests and Mann -Whitney test. The means for the components of the wellbeing score were analysed in the same manner. For the presence of complaints (well-being score NRS . 0), we calculated the percentage of patients with complaints, the rate ratio, and its 95% CI, and P -values based on χ 2 test.

For the second co-primary endpoint, we calculated event rate as percentages of patients with an adverse event. For other binary outcomes, we calculated percentage of patients with the outcome and uses rate ratio and χ 2 test for the comparison between groups. Other quantitative outcome parameters we analysed as the primary endpoint. All statistical comparisons were performed according to an intention-to-treat approach. The signi fi cance for P was set at , 0.05 without adjustment for multiple testing. The SPSS was used for data analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Two hundred and one patients (non-fasting, n = 100; fasting group, n = 101) completed this study ( Figure 1 ). Table 1 summarizes and compares the patients ' baseline characteristics. The average fasting time was 5.20 + 4.88 and 12.63 + 6.36 h for the non-fasting and fasting groups, respectively ( P , 0.001).

Primary outcomes

Patients ' well-being

The summed patients ' well-being score was signi fi cantly lower for the non-fasting group compared with the fasting group [13.1 + 9.6 vs. 16.5 + 11.4, mean difference (MD) -3.40, 95% CI of MD ( -6.35 to -0.46), P = 0.029]. Among the patients ' well-being scores, the levels of hunger and tiredness were signi fi cantly lower in the nonfasting group [non-fasting vs. fasting; hunger: 0.9 + 1.9 vs. 3.1 + 3.2, MD -2.14, 95% CI ( -2.86 to -1.42), P , 0.001; tiredness: 1.6 + 2.3 vs. 2.6 + 2.7, MD -0.99, 95% CI ( -1.68 to -0.29), P = 0.023; Table 2 ]. Incidences of hunger (26.0 vs. 62.4%) and tiredness (43.0 vs. 61.4%) were signi fi cantly lower in the non-fasting group ( Table 3 ). Figure 2 shows the distribution of the well-being scores classi fi ed according their severity. No signi fi cant difference was observed between the two groups regarding post-procedural patientreported outcomes ( Table 4 ).

Intra- and post-procedural vomiting, aspiration, and intubation

No intra-procedural food-related adverse events, such as vomiting, emergency intubation, or aspiration, occurred in both groups. One patient (non-fasting group) requested brief chest compression due to complete AV-block with cardiac arrest during two-chamber-pacemaker implantation. He did not develop any sequelae. No signi fi cant difference was observed between the groups regarding intra-procedural nausea [non-fasting vs. fasting: 7 (7.4%) vs. 6 (6.2%), relative risk 1.22, 95% CI 0.39 -3.77, P = 0.299].

Secondary outcomes

Usage of intra-procedural medications

During CIED procedures, 104 patients (51.7%) received a mean of 3.1 + 1.4 mg of Midazolam, and 67 patients (33.3%) received a mean of 0.05 + 0.03 mg of Fentanyl ( Table 5 ). Intra-procedural medication for Midazolam, Propofol, and Fentanyl did not show statistically signi fi cant differences between the two groups ( Table 5 ). No patients asked for or needed antiemetic medication during the procedure.

Serum creatinine ( Δ creatinine) and glomerular fi ltration rate changes

No signi fi cant difference was observed between the two groups regarding postvs. pre-procedural serum creatinine changes [nonfasting vs. fasting: -6.2 + 19.6 vs. -5.8 + 16.3, MD -0.36, 95% CI ( -5.67 to 4.95)] and GFR changes [non-fasting vs. fasting: 4.2 + 9.7 vs. 3.5 + 12.4, MD 0.73, 95% CI ( -2.59 to 4.06)].

Patients ' vital signs and blood parameters

Among all the pre-procedural blood tests, signi fi cant differences were found only in the venous blood gas analysis for levels of lactate ( P = 0.027), calcium ( P = 0.018), and glucose ( P , 0.001; see Supplementary material online, Table S1 ), while among the postprocedural blood tests, the thrombocyte level ( P = 0.043) was the only parameter with a signi fi cant difference (see Supplementary material online, Table S2 ). Heart rate, mean blood pressure, and SpO2 were analysed and showed no statistically relevant differences ( Table 5 ).

Overall satisfaction with the procedure, status of sleep quality, and surgical-site pain

Overall satisfaction with the CIED procedure was not signi fi cantly different between the groups. Moreover, there were no signi fi cant differences between the two groups regarding the status of sleep quality and surgical-site pain ( Table 4 ). Also, 177 patients stated postprocedurally their preferred group allocation for future procedures, while 64.4% ( n = 114) of the patients preferred a non-fasting strategy, and 35.6% ( n = 63) favoured the fasting strategy ( P , 0.001; Figure 3 ).

Length of intensive care unit and hospital stay

Post-operative stay (total and ICU) was comparable in both groups, with no statistically signi fi cant difference (total: non-fasting 1.9 + 2.6 days vs. fasting 1.6 + 1.3 days, P = 0.36; ICU: non-fasting 0.06 + 0.42 days vs. fasting 0.01 + 0.10 days, P = 0.55).

Thirty-day chest infection rate and incidence of procedural-related deaths

Symptoms of respiratory infections were reported in three patients within 30 days post-procedure; one patient in each group had a common cold, and one patient in the fasting group was treated for pneumonia without sequelae ( P = 0.999). No procedural-related death occurred.

Discussion

This study showed that a non-fasting strategy is advantageous compared with a fasting strategy regarding patient ' s well-being and comparable in terms of safety for patients undergoing elective CIED procedures. To our knowledge, this is the fi rst randomized clinical study to assess the bene fi ts of a non-fasting strategy for an interventional cardiac procedure. The summed pre-procedural well-being score was signi fi cantly lower for the non-fasting group, which re fl ects more well-being in this group which was mainly driven by signi fi cantly lower scores for hunger and tiredness in the non-fasting group. Non-fasting before CIED procedures did not in fl uence renal, metabolic, and haematological blood parameters and duration of hospital stay. No intra-procedural foodintake-related incidents concerning vomiting, aspiration, or emergency intubation were observed in both groups. The study also showed that, in daily practice, fasting times for patients were longer than intended.

The fi rst attempt to assess the clinical application of non-fasting before cardiac interventions was conducted by Hamid et al . 3 in 2014. In this retrospective analysis of 2000 percutaneous coronary interventions, reports of bronchial aspiration, aspiration pneumonia, or intra-procedural intubation over a 3-year period were lacking. 3 Studies about non-cardiac procedures, such as abdominal surgery, 8 -15 thyroidectomy, 16 or elective ophthalmologic surgery, 17 showed that shortened pre-operative fasting or supplying patients with a carbohydrate-loaded drink 2 h before the procedures is safe and decreased parameters, such as post-operative nausea and vomiting, hospital stay, insulin resistance, and increased well-being. Although most of these procedures required at least deep sedation and in fl icted more physical trauma on the body than CIED procedures, the bene fi cial effects of a non-fasting strategy can be assumed to be comparable with other cardiac interventional procedures, such as those in our trial. Even if many patients are overweight, fasting before surgery is not an adequate time to start with weight loss, as it increases insulin resistance, disrupts intra-cellular calcium homeostasis, 18 and provokes mental and physical stress, which might be detrimental to the recovery process. The assessed levels for hunger and tiredness differed signi fi cantly, with lower scores for the non-fasting group. Other well-being factors for patients, such as pain, nausea, dizziness, dry mouth, and thirst before the procedure, were not signi fi cantly different. This is not surprising, as even patients scheduled for fasting were allowed to drink for up to 2 h until the procedure. Hunger being higher in fasting patients is also expected. However, although this difference is statistically signi fi cant, the actual values for both groups are not very high. It is possible that during questioning, which occurred before the start of the CIED procedure, patients were too nervous or excited, which decreased their feelings of hunger. There are a few studies investigating well-being parameters in the fi eld of non-cardiac interventions, which showed, depending on the investigated items, more hunger, thirst, mouth dryness, headache, tiredness, weakness, and inability to concentrate in overnight fasted patients compared with patients prepared with carbohydrate drinks 2 h before surgery. 16,17 In contrast to these studies, non-fasting patients in our study were allowed to eat and drink up to an hour before surgery and were not provided with mandatory carbohydrate drinks.

Pre-procedural venous blood gas analysis showed statistically relevant differences for glucose, calcium, and lactate, but values were still within normal ranges and would not in fl uence treatment decisions. The clinical relevance of these differences seems to be low and needs further research.

Incidents of vomiting/aspiration during the procedures in this trial were lacking. Post-procedural respiratory infections might be a sequel of (silent) aspirations and were therefore collected in this study, but were unremarkable. Overall, the rate of intra-operative aspiration has been reported to be between 1:7000 19 and 3:10 000, 20 which is mainly in procedures with at least deep sedation. Additionally, the actual mortality of aspiration due to the therapeutic advantages of antibiotics has considerably decreased during the last decades. Therefore, it is questionable whether the minor bene fi t of less gastric content in the rare case of aspiration is relevant enough to keep a patient fasting for a long time when long periods of fasting also incur other risks and increase feelings of discomfort, such as hunger and tiredness. This is especially true for procedures where patients are in control of their re fl exes and are not intubated, such as the patients collected for this study. A study by Manchikanti et al . 13 showed that, for 18 472 nonfasting interventional procedures in 3179 patients with mostly conscious or intravenous sedation, only very few patients (189 of 11 856 encounters, 1.6%) complained of nausea after the procedure. Vomiting occurred in only three patients. No aspiration effects were described within all procedures. 13

Some studies have demonstrated that prolonged perioperative fasting induces a longer duration of hospital or ICU stay. 15 These studies were related to procedures with longer recovery periods than CIED procedures. This was not observed in the current study probably due to the overall short length of stay after the CIED operations.

Notably, real fasting times for both groups were longer than the minimum demands. Fasting times were , 2 h for 32 and , 3 h for 42 patients in the non-fasting group. Meal serving times in hospitals are mainly standardized, regardless of the procedure starting time. Last meals are mainly served in the evening. Patients who are scheduled for the fi rst procedure in the morning, before/during breakfast is served, have therefore fasted much longer. Studies on fasting time in real practice are rare. 1,13 Our data agree with a study by Chon et al ., 14 demonstrating that delays, schedule changes, and errors often occur and induce increased fasting time, with 12.8 + 2.2 h of average fasting before a procedure, similar to our fasting group, and an additional 2.5 + 1.7 h, if delayed. This means that even with a non-fasting policy, some patients will appear during the CIED procedure with longer fasting hours. This might reassure doctors who are still afraid of non-fasting policies.

Interestingly, 64.4% of all patients would prefer to be in the nonfasting group, with even higher values (81.6%) for patients having experience with the non-fasting strategy compared with 47.8% of the fasting group. Thus, it is likely that assignment to a group can in fl uence preferences. We wanted to know why patients preferred a certain approach: 63.9% of patients preferring fasting feared complications and 14.7% mentioned ' tradition ' or ' it has always been like this. ' Based on the aforementioned results, it seems that proper patient and staff education is required if a non-fasting strategy will be implemented.

Limitations

This was a single-centre study. We developed the study to compare a ' fasting ' vs. a ' non-fasting ' strategy in daily practice in our tertiary centre. The requested fasting times (1 and 6 h) served as a minimal demand, and it was impossible to force the patients to keep eating until the considered times. Therefore, the study could not be powered to compare exact 1 vs. 6 h fasting because this would not re fl ect the reality in our hospital.

Our literature review revealed very small event rates (0 -0.03%) for the second co-primary endpoint, the incidence of food-related intra-procedural adverse events. 4,13,19,20 Because the required sample size would be beyond the scope of this fi rst study in this fi eld, we decided to build the sample size calculation solely on the well-being score. Overall serious intra-procedural complications including pericardial tamponade, acute respiratory insuf fi ciency, acute heart failure, and cardiac arrest are expected to be rare in experienced CIED surgery. 9 As we have observed only one serious non-food-related intra-operative event, we could not evaluate if a non-fasting strategy would impair the outcome in such a context.

Seven patients were unblinded before or during the procedure (four non-fasting, three fasting). No CIED procedures were delayed after unblinding, but one patient was re-scheduled for cardioversion the next day due to non-fasting. Two of the unblinded patients (one from each group, 2.5 and 15 h fasting) received an intra-operative cardioversion to treat persistent atrial fi brillation.

The well-being scores were not normally distributed. Nevertheless, weusedmeanscoresbecausethese best typify the patients ' well-being status at the group level. We performed non-parametric analysis which showed almost similar P -values.

Conclusion

This study showed that a non-fasting strategy is bene fi cial to a fasting strategy regarding patient ' s well-being and comparable in terms of safety for the CIED procedures, allowing optimized procedure scheduling with high patient satisfaction. Fast-CIED trial made an important step to show that a non-fasting strategy is a viable alternative to a fasting strategy in patients undergoing elective CIED surgery. However, further large-scale trials are still required to ensure maximal patients ' comfort and safety and to facilitate and maximize patient fl ow.
