Effects of Dexmedetomidine-Iso fl urane versus Iso fl urane Anesthesia on Brain Injury After Cardiac Valve Replacement Surgery

Fang Kang, PhD, ChaoLiang Tang, MD, MingMing Han, MD, XiaoQing Chai, MD, Xiang Huang, BA, Juan Li, PhD 1

Department of Anesthesiology, Anhui Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Anhui Medical University, Anhui Province, China

Objectives: To compare dexmedetomidine combined with iso fl urane versus iso fl urane anesthesia on brain injury after cardiac surgery.

Design:

Setting:

A prospective, randomized, single-blind study.

University hospital.

Participants: Adult patients undergoing elective valve replacement surgery.

Interventions: Ninety-seven patients scheduled for valve replacement surgery were randomly divided into 2 groups: dexmedetomidine and iso fl urane (Dex-Iso, n ¼ 50) and iso fl urane alone (Iso, n ¼ 47). Dexemedetomidine was infused at 0.6 μ g/kg as a bolus, followed with 0.2 μ g/kg/h until the end of surgery.

Measurements and Main Results: Jugular blood samples were drawn for analysis of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) and glial fi brillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels on time points of: T1 (before induction); T2 (5 minutes after cardiopulmonary bypass [CPB] onset); T3 (after CPB off); T4 (the fi rst day after operation); T5 (the second day after operation). Plasma lactate levels in arterial and jugular venous blood also were quanti fi ed. The difference between arterial and jugular bulb venous blood lactate levels (AVDL) was calculated. An antisaccadic eye movement (ASEM) test was carried out on the day before the operation and the seventh day postoperatively. In both groups, serum MMP-9 and GFAP concentrations increased after CPB, with the peak values occurring after CPB. At time point T5, MMP-9 and GFAP levels were close to those at T1. MMP-9 concentrations in the Dex-Iso group were lower than the Iso group at T3 and T4. GFAP concentrations in the Dex-Iso group were lower at T3 but were higher than the Iso group at T2. No signi fi cant differences were found in AVDL between the 2 groups perioperatively except at T2. The ASEM scores decreased signi fi cantly postoperatively. There was no signi fi cant difference in the ASEM scores between the 2 treatment groups before and after the operation.

Conclusions: The use of dexmedetomidine decreased the biochemical markers of brain injury but did not improve the neuropsychological test result after cardiac surgery.

Dexmedetomidine is a potent and highly selective, shorteracting intravenous alpha-2 adrenergic agonist with sympatholytic, sedative, amnestic, and analgesic properties. 8 It has been described as a useful and safe adjuvant in many clinical applications. In addition to diverse clinically useful effects, there is substantial in vivo and in vitro evidence that dexmedetomidine has neuroprotective properties. 9 -12

Volatile anesthetic agents, especially iso fl urane and sevofl urane, also have been shown to have neuroprotective effects over the last 2 decades. Iso fl urane preconditioning has been shown to induce tolerance against cerebral ischemia insults in animal models. 13 -16 Moreover, an observational study by Wojciech et al showed that the use of volatile anesthetics (0.5 minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) iso fl urane and sevo fl urane) before cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) signi fi cantly reduced biochemical markers of brain injury in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass graft surgery. 17

On the basis of these preliminary data, 9 -17 the authors hypothesized that a combination of iso fl urane and dexmedetomidine, compared with iso fl urane anesthesia alone, would be accompanied by a marked neuroprotective effect. However, patients undergoing open-chamber procedures, such as mitral valve or aortic surgery, are believed to be at higher risk for adverse cerebral outcomes than patients whose hearts are not opened during surgery. 18,19 Accordingly, the primary object of this study was to compare the effect of iso fl urane-dexmedetomidine-based anesthesia with that of iso fl urane anesthesia alone on the release of brain injury biomarkers and early postoperative cognition function after cardiac valve replacement surgery.

Materials and Methods

Subsequent to the approval of this study and informed consent by the Research Ethics Committee of Anhui Provincial Hospital (2012 No.5 of Ethic Review), 107 patients scheduled to undergo valve replacement with CPB were enrolled into this prospective, randomized, clinical trial. Patients were 45 to 65 years of age. The exclusion criteria encompassed the following: a history of brain surgery or severe head trauma, any current neurologic disease or a history of neurologic disorders (eg, stroke with a residual de fi cit), diagnosed psychiatric illness, signi fi cant carotid artery stenosis, unstable angina pectoris or left ventricular ejection fraction o 40% before operation, a serious disturbance in the cardiac conduction system, chronic respiratory disease, serious endocrine disease, renal failure or liver insuf fi ciency, and those on a methyldopa or clonidine treatment were excluded from this study. This study was controlled and randomized using a randomization function in SPSS version 13.0 and stored in consecutively numbered sealed envelopes until allocation.

Intramuscular morphine doses were administered at 0.2 mg/kg of body weight 30 minutes prior to surgery. Upon arrival of the patients at the operating room, standard monitoring (including noninvasive blood pressure, 5 lead electrocardiogram, pulse oximetry and bispectral index) was performed using IntelliVue M50 (Philips Electronics Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Under sterile conditions and with local anesthesia, the left radial artery was cannulated using a 20-gauge catheter (Becton Dickinson, New York) and left jugular vein retrograde cannulation was performed with a 5.5-F, 15-cm, single lumen catheter (Beijing Target Medical Technologies, Beijing, China) using an ultrasound guided (Sonosite FujiFilm, Bothell, WA) technique. The tip of the jugular venous catheter was positioned to correspond with the jugular bulb, and placement was veri fi ed by a lateral cervical radiograph. After blood samples had been acquired from the radial artery (1 mL) and jugular bulb (6 mL) as the baseline, anesthesia was induced with midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), etomidate (0.2-0.3 mg/kg), and sufentanil (1 μ g/kg). Cisatracurium was administered at a dose of 0.3 mg/kg to facilitate intubation. After tracheal intubation, all patients were ventilated using intermittent positive-pressure ventilation with an air/ oxygen mixture (2 L/min with an inspired oxygen concentration of 65%) and the PaCO2 was maintained at 35 to 40 mmHg. Then, the right jugular vein was cannulated with a 7-F, 16-cm, 3-lumen central venous catheter (Beijing Target Medical Technologies, Beijing, China) under sterile conditions. Sufentanil was administered at a dose of 0.5 μ g/kg before the skin incision was made and was infused throughout the procedure at doses of 0.6 μ g/kg/h. Patients received dexmedetomidine and iso fl urane (Dex-Iso) or iso fl urane (Iso) anesthesia determined by the sealed envelope method. Dexmedetomidine was infused at 0.6 μ g/kg in 15 minutes as a bolus, continued at 0.2 μ g/kg/h, and was discontinued at the end of surgery. The inhaled concentration of iso fl urane was adjusted to maintain the bispectral index (BIS) 40-50. During CPB, iso fl urane was administered through the oxygenator of the CPB circuit and the inhaled concentration was adjusted to keep the BIS value between 40 and 50. Additional cisatracurium was administered as needed during the procedure. The utilization of iso fl urane was calculated by weighing the iso fl urane evaporator before and after the surgery.

Hypotension (lower than 70% of baseline) accompanied with a heart rate (HR) less than 50 bpm was treated with 5 mg ephedrine intravenously (IV). If hypotension with a heart rate of 50 bpm or more occurred, 50 μ g phenylephrine was administered IV. Bradycardia (HR o 40 bpm) was treated with 0.5 mg atropine. Hypertension (higher than 130% of baseline) was treated with 0.2 mg nicardipine, and tachycardia (HR 4 100 bpm) was treated by 5 to 10 mg esmolol.

Heparin was administered at a dose of 300 Units/kg before CPB, and the activated coagulation time was kept over 400 seconds. Mild hypothermic (30 1 C-32 1 C) CPB were adopted with a membrane oxygenator and an arterial line fi lter. The pump was primed with Lactated Ringer ' s Solution and gelatin, and serial hematocrit levels were kept at 21%. Perfusion was maintained at a pump fl ow rate of 2 to 2.4 L/min/m 2

throughout CPB to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 60 to 80 mmHg. Arterial blood gases were measured every 15 to 30 minutes to maintain arterial carbon dioxide partial pressures of 35 to 40 mmHg, and oxygen partial pressures of 150 to 250 mmHg. After the ascending aorta was cross-clamped, a cold crystalloid cardioplegia solution was administered. The rewarming speed was 2 1 C/min. After removal of the aortic cross-clamp, dopamine and dobutamine were given if inotropic support was needed. Infusion doses of dopamine and dobutamine were titrated according to the hemodynamics (systolic arterial pressure 4 90 mmHg). Protamine was administered to reverse the effect of heparin. After CPB, patients received an infusion of potassium chloride and magnesium sulfate, which was dose-controlled by their plasma concentrations.

Patients were sent to the cardiac and vascular intensive care unit. Standard postoperative care was implemented for all patients by the same surgery and intensive care unit teams.

Jugular venous blood (6 mL) and radial artery blood (1 mL) samples were drawn at: before induction (T1), 5 minutes after CPB was initiated (T2), after CPB was off (T3), 8:00 am of the fi rst day after operation (T4), and 8:00 am of the second day after operation (T5). Jugular venous blood samples were drawn at a rate no greater than 1.5 mL/min. This approach should limit extracerebral blood contamination to an insignificant amount. Jugular blood samples were analyzed with matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), glial fi brillary acidic protein (GFAP) levels and a blood gas analysis. Arterial samples were used for blood gas analysis only. Blood samples were centrifuged (3,000 r/min) for 10 minutes. Serum samples were aspirated and frozen at -70 1 C for subsequent assessment. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was used to determine serum MMP-9 and GFAP concentrations. Plasma lactate concentrations were measured automatically using the blood gas analysis equipment (Abbott, i-STAT 300, Massachusetts, Princeton, USA)

The neuropsychological state was evaluated using a simplifi ed antisaccadic eye movement test (ASEM) on the day before surgery and the seventh day postoperatively by the same investigator who was blinded to the study. During the ASEM test, 20 the patient was seated facing the examiner, who positioned his hands equidistantly from the midline in the patient ' s left and right visual fi elds, with each index fi nger extended in the manner commonly used to test visual fi elds by confrontation. The stimulus for each trial employed 2 to 5 rapid, repeated fl exions of one or the other of the examiner ' s index fi ngers. The correct antisaccadic response was an initial eye movement to the side opposite to that of the moving fi nger, and any initial eye movement across the midline toward the moving fi nger was recorded as an error, even if a subsequent correction toward the nonmoving fi nger was made. All patients received an explanation of the ASEM test and were given practice trials before being subjected to the actual test. Twenty trials were done for each patient. The total number of correct responses was used as the score and a change in percentage of the before and after surgery score was calculated as follows: ([postoperative score -preoperative score] / preoperative scores) -100%. Postoperative ASEM impairment was de fi ned as a decrease of scores more than 30% from the preoperative values. 21

Statistical Analysis

The measurement data was analyzed for normal distribution with the Shapiro -Wilk test using Excel 6.0 and SPSS 19.0 statistical software. The results were reported as the mean 7 standard deviation if a normal distribution was met. Mauchly ' s test of sphericity was used to judge whether there were relations among the repeatedly measured data. If any (p r 0.05), multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was taken, and data in different groups of each measurement time could be compared pairwise. If any (p 4 0.05), the one-way ANOVA was taken. Enumeration data were analyzed using the Chi-square test. The data were statistically signi fi cant with a p value of less than 0.05.

Results

From March 2012 to November 2015, a total of 107 patients consented to participate in the study and were randomized. Ten patients who enrolled were not subsequently treated (6 patients refused neurological testing, 4 patients were excluded because of serious postoperative hemodynamic disorders or postoperative bleeding requiring operation). Ninety-seven subjects completed the protocol. Fifty patients received dexmedetomidine and iso fl urane anesthesia, and 47 received only iso fl urane anesthesia. Discussion

This prospective randomized study showed that dexmedetomidine-iso fl urane anesthesia during cardiac valve surgery with CPB decreased the plasma MMP-9 and GFAP concentrations compared with iso fl urane anesthesia alone. The results also proved that dexmedetomidine can spare inhalational anesthetics. However, the combination of dexmedetomidine did not result in better ASEM scores than iso fl urane anesthesia after cardiac valve surgery.

Early diagnosis of brain damage is very dif fi cult. Some authors proposed using biochemical markers in the diagnosis of postoperative neurologic outcomes. 17,22 In this study, brain damage was evaluated through analysis of biochemical markers of brain injury and neurological function tests. GFAP is a brain-speci fi c intermediate fi lament protein found only in glial cells of the central nervous system. Serum concentrations of GFAP higher than 0.49 ng/mL are considered pathological. It has been documented in several studies that GFAP measurement can act as a speci fi c indicator of ischemic or hemorrhagic brain injury. 17,22,23 Several studies also con fi rm the high speci fi city of GFAP to brain injuries in comparison to other biomarkers such as S100 β and neuron-speci fi c enolase. 24,25 MMP-9, one type of gelatinase, is produced by endothelial cells, microglia, and astrocytes. The active form of MMP-9 is the major component of the basal lamina in the blood-brain barrier. An increase in MMP-9 has been observed after various types of brain injury. 26,27 Moreover, the plasma MMP-9 concentration strongly correlates with free radical production after brain ischemia. 26,28 Wojciech et al reported that patients who received a 0.5 MAC of iso fl urane or sevo fl urane before CPB showed lower plasma GFAP and MMP-9 levels compared with patients in the control group. 17 For these reasons, the authors took these 2 biochemical makers as indexes of brain injury. The authors ' results showed that the serum concentration of MMP-9 and GFAP increased following the start of CPB, which indicated that the brain might be damaged to some degree. The reason might be disorders within the brain circulation due to multiple factors including cerebral microemboli, hypoperfusion, and systemic in fl ammatory response. Furthermore, patients of the Dex-Iso group had lower levels of GFAP and MMP-9 after CPB was off compared with the Iso group. Therefore, the authors deduced that the Dex-Iso anesthesia alleviated brain injury when compared with isofl urane anesthesia in cardiac surgery. However, as the inhaled concentration of iso fl urane was lower with dexmedetomidine, it was unclear which factor played a role, the dexmedetomidine or the lower concentration of iso fl urane. Conversely, the authors are unable to explain the reason for the inconsistent tendencies of these 2 biochemical markers. Their different production might be the reason.

In this study, ASEM tests were used for the evaluation of cognitive function. The test was costless and can be performed as a bedside test even in the intensive care unit. Furthermore, ASEM was used to assess neurocognitive function in several studies of cardiac surgery. 21,29,30 ASEMs are eye movements deliberately made in the direction opposite to that of a sudden presented peripheral stimulus. This requires the generation of both excitatory and inhibitory oculomotor signals, as well as using representational memory of what the target means rather than using the direct stimulation that is immediately present in the target. The ASEM is thought to use corticotectal pathways that originate from several areas of the prefrontal cortex, including frontal eye fi elds. 28 The authors ' results show that the ASEM scores after surgery were similar in the 2 groups postoperatively. This indicates that the priority of neuroprotection in the Dex-Iso group was not conclusive.

Perioperative disorders in oxygen delivery resulting from ischemia are important factors of brain injury after an operation. 31 Increases in lactate levels are considered an imbalance of oxygen delivery and consumption. Differences between arterial and jugular venous lactate levels (AVDL) have been found to be associated with the cerebral ischemia intervals during CPB. 32 The present study showed that the AVDL of the Dex-Iso group was lower than the iso fl urane group 5 minutes after CPB was on. This might indicate that dexmedetomidine and iso fl urane anesthesia were better at maintaining the cerebral perfusion in case of hemodynamic fl uctuations. This study also showed similar AVDL at most time points, which implies that metabolism of the brain was similar in both groups. The authors ascribe these data to similar hypothermia levels, stable hemodynamics, similar hematocrit levels, and the relationship between depths of anesthesia (similar BIS value variation). Although dexmedetomidine has been reported to decrease central sympathetic activity and signi fi cantly reduce circulating catecholamine levels, 33,34 it did not appear to decrease cerebral metabolic rate. 35 The present study also showed the same result even combined with iso fl urane.

In the present study, patients in the Dex-Iso group and the Iso group received iso fl urane in varied concentrations to maintain the BIS value within 40 to 50 throughout the procedure. This might have affected the neuroprotective effect of iso fl urane. The serum concentration of biochemical markers increased after the CPB initiated and reached the highest value as the CPB was off, which showed that the brain injury was associated with CPB. A similar tendency between the 2 treatment groups also veri fi ed that dexmedetomidine combined with iso fl urane anesthesia could not avoid brain injury. Therefore, although the neuroprotective effects of dexmedetomidine and iso fl urane have been veri fi ed in animals, 9 -16 it is hard to bridge the gap between clinical and pre-clinical perspectives. The present study showed similar ASEM scores in the Dex-Iso group and the Iso group. Other studies have reported similar results. 36 Interestingly, a recent report about dexmedetomidine has been documented to be associated with a decrease in postoperative mortality and decreased incidence of postoperative complications and delirium in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. 37 Con fl icting results between reports might be due to different protocols and populations studied.

There were limitations in this study. First, although the study was random, it was not strictly double blinded. The laboratory assessments and neurological function tests were blinded to all the research fellows; however, the anesthesiologist in charge of each case could detect if dexmedetomidine was used or not by the hemodynamics or BIS. Second, there was no control group of patients without iso fl urane or dexmedetomidine in this study. The authors assumed that these 2 medicines have neuroprotective effects according to previous studies. 9 -17 The bene fi t or detriment of these 2 medicines should be evaluated in patients undergoing cardiac surgery in the future. Third, the transesophageal echo was not applied to con fi rm the de-airing of the left heart in this study. Therefore, aeroembolism of the cerebral vascular and its effect on brain jury could not be avoided completely. Another important limitation of the present study was that the inhaled concentration of iso fl urane was not always kept at a fi xed value, which would alter the effect of iso fl urane. During the study, fi xed amounts of dexmedetomidine were given; however, it was dif fi cult to keep a fi xed inhaled concentration to obtain an ideal BIS value in all of the patients. Finally, the dosage of dexmedetomidine that was used (0.6 μ g/kg bolus, 0.2 μ g/kg/h infusion) was lower than the recommended maximum sedation dosage. However, in fact, hemodynamic stability is dif fi cult to obtain with high doses of dexmedetomidine in cardiac anesthesia, so a lower dosage was used for sedation based on experience. Additionally, different doses may result in different effects. The lack of analysis of longterm outcomes in patients with postoperative neuropsychological complications was another limitation of this study. Moreover, only 1 test was taken to evaluate the neuropsychological state in this study. The sensitivity might not be suf fi cient. Increasing the number of tests used to classify postoperative cognitive dysfunction appears to increase sensitivity. 38 All of these problems need to be investigated in the future.

In conclusion, dexmedetomidine combined with iso fl urane anesthesia may decrease the biochemical markers of brain injury, but does not improve the neuropsychological test results in the early period after cardiac valve replacement surgery compared with iso fl urane alone.
