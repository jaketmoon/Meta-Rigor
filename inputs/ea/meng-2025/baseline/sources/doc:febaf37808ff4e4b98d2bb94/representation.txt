Purpose: To summarize select critical care pharmacotherapy guidelines and studies published in 2016. Summary: The Critical Care Pharmacotherapy Literature Update (CCPLU) Group screened 31 journals monthly for relevant pharmacotherapy articles and selected 107 articles for review over the course of 2016. Of those included in the monthly CCPLU, three guidelines and seven primary literature studies are reviewed here. The guideline updates included are as follows: hospital-acquired pneumonia and ventilator-associated pneumonia management, sustained neuromuscular blocking agent use, and reversal of antithrombotics in intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). The primary literature summaries evaluate the following: dexmedetomidine for delirium prevention in post-cardiac surgery, dexmedetomidine for delirium management in mechanically ventilated patients, high-dose epoetin alfa after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, ideal blood pressure targets in ICH, hydrocortisone in severe sepsis, procalcitonin-guided antibiotic de-escalation, and empiric micafungin therapy.

Conclusion: The review provides a synopsis of select pharmacotherapy publications in 2016 applicable to clinical practice.

1. Introduction

Critical care clinicians are challenged with the task of staying abreast of the medical literature in order to optimize clinical outcomes for their patients. The question of how to achieve this while attending to a busy practice is crucial with the constantly expanding body of literature. PubMed searches filtered by year using the keywords critical care and intensive care show a dramatic increase in identified publications from 5446 and 8170 in 2009 to 14,316 and 16,453 in 2016, respectively.

The Clinical Pharmacy and Pharmacology Section's Research Committee within the Society of Critical Care Medicine tasked a working group with creating a monthly publication in 2009 to keep its members abreast of additions to the medical literature in regards to critical care pharmacotherapy. Thus, the Critical Care Pharmacotherapy Literature Update (CCPLU), comprised of critical care pharmacists, commenced with monthly reviews of publications selected from 31 journals based on the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system to assess quality of evidence and strength of recommendation in addition to relevance to clinical practice [1]. The monthly CCPLU is now nationally distributed electronically to a mailing group as well as via social media networks. One hundred seven articles were reviewed in 2016 with three guidelines and seven primary literature studies chosen based on their GRADE criteria (highly-rated [1A] for primary literature), their potential to change or reinforce current best practices, and an emphasis on pharmacotherapy in critical care practice.

1.1. Kalil et al. Infectious Diseases Society of America/American Thoracic Society Management of adults with hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated pneumonia [2]

This guideline for the diagnosis and treatment of hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) updated the 2005 iteration [3]. The concept of healthcare-associated pneumonia (HCAP) was removed from the discussion in the 2016 guideline, a major change from the 2005 version. The decision to remove HCAP came from increasing evidence that suggests patients with HCAP are not at as high of a risk for multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) as previously considered. These guidelines suggest HCAP may be included in the upcoming community-acquired pneumonia guidelines that are anticipated to bereleased in 2017 and may define new risk factors for MDROs in HCAP.

GRADE methodology was applied to relevant articles published up to November 2015 for guideline inclusion [1]. This is the first version of the HAP and VAP guidelines to use the GRADE methodology.

Notable changes in this update are found in the newly defined risk factors for MDROs for both HAP and VAP (Table 1). These risk factors are to provide guidance in selecting empiric therapy. Other major changes for this update focused on the initial treatment of HAP and VAP. All suspected cases of HAP and VAP should be empirically covered for S. aureus , P. aeruginosa , and Gram-negative bacilli (HAP, strong recommendation, very low-quality evidence) and VAP (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence). The panel determined these organisms make up the majority of HAP and VAP cases, and inadequate coverage of these organisms can lead to increased mortality. When choosing empiric treatment, a local and preferably intensive care unit (ICU)-specific antibiogram is suggested. To determine the need for empiric coverage of MDROs including methicillin resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and P. aeruginosa , it is recommended to use the defined risk factors for MDROs, severity of illness, and the local prevalence of drug-resistant organisms (Table 2).

The use of aminoglycosides as a secondary agent for the empiric treatment of VAP is not suggested if other agents with sufficient Gram-negative coverage are available for use (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence). This recommendation is supported by evidence from a meta-analysis demonstrating patients treated with or without an aminoglycoside had similar mortality rates, but those treated with an aminoglycoside had a lower clinical response rate [2,4-6]. The panel made this suggestion due to the poor lung penetration of aminoglycosides as well as the increased risk for drug-induced nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity.

Patients with HAP or VAP caused by P. aeruginosa should be treated based on microbiological testing. No preferred agent was recommended due to the paucity of data pointing to a desired agent. Due to this lack of data, the use of a local antibiogram may be helpful in determining the best agent for P. aeruginosa coverage. Monotherapy is recommended for most patients with HAP or VAP caused by P. aeruginosa (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence); however, in patients with P. aeruginosa who continue to be in septic shock or at a high risk of death (i.e., mortality risk N 25%) after susceptibility results are available, continuation of two antipseudomonal antibiotics is suggested (weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence) [7-11]. This recommendation is controversial as data shows no difference in mortality, treatment failure, ICU and hospital length-of-stay (LOS), duration of mechanical ventilation or the development of resistance in patients receiving monotherapy compared to combination therapy for a HAP or VAP caused by P. aeruginosa [7,8]. The panel looked at evidence for patients in septic shock caused by P. aeruginosa from sources other than just HAP and VAP. A meta-analysis demonstrated no difference in mortality, but a propensity-matched analysis determined combination therapy was associated with a decreased mortality [9,10]. The panel felt the potential for decreased mortality outweighed the risks and burden of combination therapy.

In patients with HAP or VAP due to extended-spectrum betalactamase-producing species, antibiotic therapy should be based on antimicrobial susceptibility as well as patient-specific factors. The panel decided not to recommend an antibiotic regimen due to a lack of evidence identifying a preferable agent or combination of agents.

A preferred antibiotic regimen was suggested for the treatment of HAP or VAP due to susceptible Acinetobacter spp. with a carbapenem or ampicillin/sulbactam (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence). Antibiotic therapy de-escalation is suggested in both HAP and VAP once susceptibilities are known (weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence).

Perhaps the most notable change to the guidelines was the recommendation for a shorter, 7-day course of antibiotic therapy regardless of organism for both HAP (strong recommendation, very low-quality evidence) and VAP (strong recommendation, moderate -quality evidence) [18-20]. No evidence exists to determine the appropriate duration of HAP therapy, which has been extrapolated from VAP data. Although there were no differences in mortality, recurrent pneumonia, treatment failure, hospital LOS, and duration of mechanical ventilation, VAP treated with shorter courses of antibiotics (i.e., 7 -8 days) was shown to increase antibiotic-free days compared with longer courses of antibiotics (i.e., 10 -15 days) [18,19]. In a subgroup analysis of patients with VAP caused by a non-glucose-fermenting Gram-negative bacillus (e.g., P. aeruginosa and Acinetobacter ), patients were more likely to experience recurrence if they received a shorter course of antibiotics [20]. No difference in mortality was found between the different duration groups. In addition to the two systematic reviews, the panel's own meta-analysis of the included trials demonstrated no difference in mortality between short and long-course regimens. The panel determined even if shorter courses of antibiotics for VAP caused by non-glucose fermenting bacilli may slightly increase risk of recurrence, the patient does not seem to be at risk for increased mortality or inability to achieve a clinical cure.

Overall, the 2016 guidelines for the management of HAP and VAP introduced several major changes from the 2005 guidelines in regards to MDROriskfactors, selection of empiric therapy, and treatment duration. These suggestions were made to limit patient harm and safeguard against the superfluous use of antibiotics. To aid in this objective, a local antibiogram is suggested to optimize therapeutic regimens and de-escalate therapy. Many of the recommendations in this guideline were based on low- or very low-quality evidence, pointing to the lack of data and the pressing need for more to assist in decision-making as it relates to the management of HAP and VAP.

1.2. Murray et al. clinical practice guidelines for sustained neuromuscular blockade in the adult critically ill patient [21]

This guideline provided an update to the clinical practice guidelines regarding neuromuscular blocking agent (NMBA) use from 2002 [22]. The update broadened topics further to include information on specific indications and recommendations, clinician management of critically ill patients receiving NMBAs, mechanical ventilation management during therapy, techniques and therapies to decrease potential complications of therapy, and use in specific patient populations. A table summarizing the recommendations and the quality of evidence supporting them is provided in Table 3.

NMBA use in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was suggested to be administered by continuous IV infusion early in its clinical course in patients with a PaO2:FiO2 b 150(weakrecommendation,moderate quality of evidence). This recommendation was supported by studies that evaluated early use of 48-hour cisatracurium infusions in adult critically ill ARDS patients [23-25]. Significant improvement in oxygenation compared to control groups was appreciated. Pooled results indicated that a 48-hour cisatracurium infusion consistently reduced risk of death at 28 days and at hospital discharge, reduced barotrauma risk, and did not increase the risk of ICU weakness [25]. The mechanism of benefit of the NMBA class in ARDS remains unknown. Regarding lung mechanics, better synchrony may lead to improved lung recruitment, ventilator compliance, systemic oxygenation, and gas exchange. These data included in the updated guidelines adjust to an evolution of findings since 2004 suggesting a reduction in inflammatory mediators, improvement in gas exchange, and improvement in 90-day mortality with use of short-term NMBAs in early ARDS. They could be considered a reasonable construct for protocolization with study methodology including ARDS criteria and ventilator settings to define patients that may benefit from early incorporation of cisatracurium, in particular.

Routine administration of NMBAs in mechanically ventilated adult patients presenting with status asthmaticus is not recommended (weak recommendation, very low quality of evidence). A trial of an NMBA is suggested in life-threatening presentations with profound hypoxemia, respiratory acidosis, or hemodynamic compromise, but the frequency of death in these patients has been retrospectively shown to be low [26-28].

The use for NMBAs in therapeutic hypothermia to manage overt shivering is suggested (weak recommendation, very low quality of evidence). Data have shown the ability for NMBAs to eliminate shivering in 100% of patients administered a therapeutic dose without a decrease in systemic blood pressure but with a decrease in oxygen consumption [29]. Potential harm of NMBAs in critically ill patients include increased risk of secondary infections including pneumonia due to increased accumulation of bronchial secretions, perpetuation of ICU-acquired weakness, decreases in blood pressure with reflexive bradycardia from autonomic ganglia blockade, and cardiovascular manifestations from histamine release from certain agents [30]. The authors do specify a good practice statement supporting a protocol-driven approach to guide NMBA administration in patients undergoing therapeutic hypothermia.

The only strong recommendation in the guidelines suggested scheduling eye care consisting of lubricating drops or gel as well as eyelid closure for patients receiving continuous infusions of NMBAs. NMBAs will result in incomplete eyelid closure and absence of corneal reflexes, which may increase the risk of developing infection, scarring, or ulcerations. These recommended therapies have been shown to reduce the prevalence of ocular surface disorders that can occur in over half of patients that are heavily sedated or receiving NMBAs, although no consensus exists on the most effective eye-care protocol [31-35].

A target blood glucose level of b 180 mg/dL in patients receiving NMBAs is recommended. Strict glycemic control has been shown to be an independent risk factor for development of critical illness polyneuromyopathy [36]. Additional data are needed to confirm this relationship in a broader population, but this remains inadequate to make specific recommendations for or against lower glucose targets [37].

It is suggested to use a consistent weight, either ideal or adjusted, when calculating NMBA doses for obese patients with a BMI exceeding 30kg/m 2 . No data are available to determine if size descriptor influences outcomes, such as LOS, when used on a sustained basis, but a common theme is recognizing the ease of ideal body weight calculation and uniformity of day-to-day endpoints.

The strength of these guidelines is in providing the most up-to-date and best evidence available regarding NMBA use, monitoring, and safety. While specific patient factors, clinical experience, and site resources should still be considered when applying these recommendations to practice or the care of individual patients, the direction provided within this document is the most comprehensive to-date. The primary weakness is that it includes many areas with no recommendation due to insufficient evidence. The authors do provide a nice review of available data within these areas to provide some guidance, but decision-making will ultimately be left up to the clinician.

1.3. Frontera et al. Neurocritical Care Society/Society of Critical Care Medicine Guideline for reversal of antithrombotics in intracranial hemorrhage [38]

These guidelines were developed through an international, multidisciplinary committee. Recommendations were established based on available clinical trials, meta-analyses, pre-clinical studies, review articles, and practice guidelines through November 2015. The quality of evidence and strength of recommendations were presented using the GRADE system, which allows for ' strong ' or ' conditional ' recommendations with supportive evidence described as high, moderate, low or very low. ' Good practice statements ' are used to imply high confidence in recommendations taken from indirect evidence where a formal GRADEassessmentwould bechallenging to complete [39]. A table summarizing the recommendations can be found in Table 4.

Urgent vitamin K antagonist (VKA) reversal with vitamin K and prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) is recommended in patients with intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) (strong recommendation, moderate quality evidence). Vitamin K administration results in sustained reversal of anticoagulant activity; however, reduction of INR to b 1.4 has been shown to take up to 24 h [40-42]. A retrospective study found intra-parenchymal hemorrhage growth in 50% of patients receiving vitamin K alone compared to 33% and 19% in patients receiving fresh frozen plasma(FFP) and PCC, respectively [43]. Delay in VKA reversal in the setting of ICH may worsen outcomes; therefore, vitamin K therapy is only recommended in combination with other reversal agents.

Three- or four-factor PCCs are recommended for VKA reversal in ICH over FFP (strong recommendation, moderate quality evidence). VKA reversal with FFP has been shown to be effective but delayed when compared to PCC [44]. In one study, FFP was retrospectively associated with a higher risk of major hemorrhage and increased risk of death or severe disability at three months compared to PCC [45]. Administration of PCC for VKA-associated ICH results in a more rapid INR reversal compared to FFP alone; however, data evaluating functional outcomes are conflicting [46,47]. Administration of FFP is recommended if PCC are unavailable (strong recommendation, moderate quality evidence). Four-factor PCC is recommended over three-factor PCC based on evidence from a systematic review indicating a more rapid INR reversal and the theoretical benefit of inclusion of Factor VII (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence) [48].

Direct factor Xa inhibitors have relatively short elimination half-lives and cessation of drug therapy may be sufficient management for minor bleeding [49]. Guidance of pharmacologic strategy for reversal should be based on timing of last drug exposure (Good Practice Statement) and presence of bleeding over laboratory data (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence). Activated charcoal may be considered for reversal of oral DTI agents if the last known drug exposure occurred within 2 h of presentation and risk for aspiration is low (conditional recommendation, very low quality evidence). Idarucizumab is a monoclonal antibody fragment that neutralizes dabigatran and is recommended first line for dabigatran reversal if the last known dose occurred within 3 -5 half-lives (strong recommendation, moderate quality evidence) or if renal insufficiency is present resulting in prolonged drug exposure beyond 3 -5 half-lives (strong recommendation, moderate quality evidence). Idarucizumab has been shown to completely reverse dabigatran-induced coagulopathy within 4 h of administration [53]. Re-anticoagulation secondary to redistribution of dabigatran from the extravascular to the intravascular space may occur; therefore, re-dosing of idarucizumab is recommended if clinically significant bleeding persists after initial treatment (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence). Four-factor PCC or aPCC are recommended for DTI reversal in the setting of ICH if idarucizumab is not available or if the ICH is associated with a DTI other than dabigatran (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence). Animal and healthy volunteer studies have suggested PCC may correct dabigatraninduced coagulopathy; however, a prospective cross-over in vivo study found four-factor PCC did not correct dabigatran-induced changes in thrombin time, endogenous thrombin potential, ecarin clotting time, or activated partial thromboplastin time [54,55]. Conversely, a small case series of patients with dabigatran-associated life-threatening hemorrhage described adequate hemostasis after administration of aPCC [56,57]. Additional dosing of reversal agents should be based on evidence for clinically significant bleeding over laboratory parameters (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence).

Hemodialysis is recommended for patients with dabigatran-related ICH and renal insufficiency when idarucizumab is not available or clinically significant bleeding is still present after reversal (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence). Dabigatran is removed by hemodialysis and studies have shown effective drug clearance in both intermittent and continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration [58-60]. There is insufficient evidence to support rFVIIa and FFP for DTI reversal; therefore, these agents are not recommended (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence). Recommendations for the reversal of direct oral anticoagulants aligned with the guidance document from the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis [61].

Antithrombotic effects of pentasaccharide agents are not reversed by protamine sulfate [74]. PCC and aPCC have been shown in vitro and in animal studies to correct fondaparinux-induced coagulopathy [75,76]. One retrospective study evaluating various reversal treatments in acute coronary or venous thromboembolism patients treated with fondaparinux who experienced a 10% reduction in hematocrit described hemostasis attributed to rFVIIa in 50% of patients [74]. Multiple studies identified superior normalization of laboratory values and reduced bleeding time for aPCC compared to rFVIIa; therefore, aPCC is recommended for reversal of pentasaccharide agents in the setting of ICH (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence) [76,77]. rFVIIa is recommended if aPCC is contraindicated or unavailable (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence).

Cryoprecipitate is recommended for reversal of symptomatic ICH if a thrombolytic agent was administered in the last 24 h (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence). Outcome-related data to support this recommendation are sparse, and multiple studies have found no difference in functional outcome or mortality [78,79]. Cryoprecipitate is recommended over FFP due to lower required transfusion volumes (no GRADE assessment provided). Limited data are available that evaluate the utilization of antifibrinolytic agents for thrombolytic reversal; however, a single case report of tranexamic acid administration in alteplase-associated ICH described no hematoma expansion [79]. Despite the lack of available data, antifibrinolytic agents are recommended as an alternative to cryoprecipitate (conditional recommendation, very lowquality evidence). The role of platelet transfusions for the reversal of thrombolytic agents in the setting of ICH is unclear based on the results of a multi-center, retrospective study describing an association with increased hematoma expansion [78].

Reversal of antiplatelet agents in the setting of ICH is controversial as the impact of such agents on hematoma expansion and other clinical outcomes is largely unknown [80 -83]. One randomized controlled trial evaluated platelet transfusion compared to no transfusion in patients with acute hypertensive basal ganglia hemorrhage undergoing neurosurgical intervention. The authors found less hemorrhage recurrence, lower post-operative hematoma volume, and lower mortality in the platelet transfusion group [84]. The external validity of this study has been questioned given the single-center design, and the results may be difficult to interpret as various sub-groups may increase the risk for type-1 error. A prospective observational study found platelet transfusions led to smaller follow-up hemorrhage and increased the likelihood of a modified Rankin score less than four in intraparenchymal hemorrhage patients with abnormal platelet function [85]. The PATCH trial was a randomized controlled trial comparing platelet transfusion compared to standard of care in patients on antiplatelet therapy with spontaneous ICH [86]. Patients in the platelet transfusion group had significantly higher odds of death or dependence at 90 days; therefore, the authors concluded platelet transfusion was inferior to standard care in patients taking antiplatelet therapy who experience ICH. Based on available prospective evidence, multiple retrospective studies showing no clinical impact of platelet transfusion, and the increased risk for adverse events associated with transfusion, platelet transfusion is only recommended in aspirin- or adenosine diphosphate (ADP) receptor inhibitor-associated ICH patients who will undergo neurosurgical intervention (conditional recommendation, moderate quality evidence).

Desmopressin (DDAVP) administration has a low risk for adverse events and has been found to improve platelet function in ICH patients [87,88]. As a result, DDAVP is recommended in ICH associated with aspirin/COX-1 inhibitors, ADP receptor inhibitors, and in addition to platelet transfusion in patients undergoing neurosurgical intervention (conditional recommendation, low quality evidence).

Antithrombotic use is increasing as the prevalence of pro-thromboembolic disease states such as atrial fibrillation rise [89 -91]. The reviewed guideline recommendations address an increasing patient population at risk for devastating ICH. Concise, evidenced-based recommendations for the reversal of antithrombotic agents in the setting of ICH may assist practitioners to expedite emergent treatment. This guideline provides more specific guidance for reversal agent dosing compared to the 2015 American Heart Association (AHA)/American Stroke Association (ASA) recommendations for the management of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage [92]. The scope of this guideline is limited to adult patients and does not address considerations for special populations including pediatrics, geriatrics, or those presenting with complex clinical scenarios. Unfortunately, this guideline failed to produce strong recommendations for reversal of all reviewed antithrombotic agents. This limitation is secondary to a lack of high quality data or available targeted reversal agent.

1.4. Reade et al. effect of dexmedetomidine added to standard care on ventilator-free time in patients with agitated delirium [93]

The Dexmedetomidine to Lessen ICU Agitation (DahLIA) study was performed in ICUs in Australia and New Zealand between May 2011 and December 2013. The study was a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group study in an intubated, adult mixed ICU population with agitated delirium who were close to extubation except for their agitation. Patients were randomized to receive dexmedetomidine compared to placebo in a 1:1 fashion. Patients were enrolled if, within 4 h of randomization, they required sedation, antipsychotic medication, or mechanical restraints, were Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) positive for delirium, and had a Motor Activity Assessment Scale (MAAS) of at least 5. Dexmedetomidine or placebo were initiated at 0.5 mcg/kg/h and could be titrated from 0 to 1.5 mcg/kg/h to a Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS) of 0 or as specified by the treating physician. After 48 h of study drug infusion, open-label dexmedetomidine could be prescribed, and the study drug was discontinued. Treatment failure was defined as N 7 days duration of study drug. The primary outcome was ventilator-free hours at 7 days with an intention-to-treat analysis. Patients with tracheostomies were considered to be free of sedation after a 4-h period in which they did not receive opiate analgesics or sedatives. Secondary outcomes included ICU and hospital LOS, and time to achieve appropriate RASS ( -2 to +1) and MAAS (2 to 4) scores.

Overall, 74 patients were randomized, with data from 71 patients included in the analysis (dexmedetomidine = 39; placebo = 32). Most patients were sedated with propofol and 20% received antipsychotic agents. Overall, patients in the placebo group were administered more antipsychotics but lower amounts of opioids and sedatives when compared to the dexmedetomidine group. Patients receiving dexmedetomidine demonstrated more ventilator-free hours at 7 days compared to patients receiving placebo (144.8 vs. 127.5 h; p = 0.01) with dexmedetomidine associated with earlier extubation (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.36 -0.95, p = 0.03). There was no significant difference in adverse events of bradycardia, hypotension, or agitation in the two groups. This trial was terminated early before the planned 96 patients due to lack of enrollment.

1.5. Cariou et al. early high-dose erythropoietin therapy after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [96]

The High Dose of Erythropoietin Analogue After Cardiac Arrest (EPOACR-02) trial was a multicenter, randomized, single-blind, controlled trial that evaluated the safety and efficacy of high dose epoetin alfa in improving neurological outcomes. Post-cardiac arrest patients who were still comatose (i.e., Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] b 7) after out-ofhospital resuscitation from October 2009 to July 2013 in France were included in the study. Patients were between the ages of 18 and 80 years, with a witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest from presumed cardiac origin and subsequent return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) within 60min.Patients were excluded if the origin of arrest was extracardiac in nature, if they had received previous or current epoetin treatment, were pregnant, had a fatal underlying comorbid conditions, or did not have insurance. Eligible patients were randomized to receive epoetin alfa 40,000 units as soon as possible after ROSC and every 12 h thereafter for a total of four doses within 48 h (n = 234) or standardized care (n = 242). All patients were treated according to standard post-ROSC care guidelines, including targeted temperature management (goal temperature between 32° and 34 °C) and coronary revascularization, as appropriate. The primary outcome was proportion of Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) Scale of 1 (good cerebral performance or minor disability) at 60 days. Secondary outcomes included distribution of each CPC level at post-arrest days 30 and 60 in addition to all-cause mortality during ICU stay, hospital stay, at day 30, and at day 60. Additionally, adverse events were monitored through day 60.

Baseline characteristics were similar between groups, including time from collapse to ROSC (median 25 min in both groups, p = 0.57) and incidence of targeted temperature management (93.5% of all cases). Median time to first dose of epoetin alfa was 1.43 h (IQR 0.8 -2.7). Due to the high incidence of early mortality, only 75.6% of patients randomized received the entire 48-hour treatment course.

There were no differences between groups in the primary outcome at 60 days (epoetin alfa: 32.4% vs. control: 32.1%; OR 1.01; 95% CI 0.68 -1.48; p = 0.96). Furthermore, distribution of CPC at every level wassimilar between groups at all time points. Mortality rate was similar at all time points, with 57.7% and 56.4% of patients being alive at 60 days in the epoetin alfa and control groups, respectively (p = 0.85). Anoxic brain injury was the primary cause of death in both groups, accounting for 76.9% and 78.2% of patients in the epoetin alfa and control cohorts, respectively (p = 0.74). Serious adverse events were more frequently observed in the epoetin alfa group compared to the control group (22.6% vs. 14.9%; p = 0.03), including an increased risk of thromboembolic complications (12.4% vs. 5.8%; p = 0.01).

Anoxo-ischemic injury remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality following cardiac arrest, with targeted temperature management as the only intervention that has demonstrated improved outcomes [97,98]. Despite preliminary data suggesting that erythropoietin analogs may provide neurocognitive protection, this present study failed to demonstrate benefit and found a risk of increased complications. Animal models demonstrated erythropoietin- induced neuronal and vascular protection in the nervous system, thus it has been investigated in a variety of clinical scenarios, including acute myocardial infarction, middle cerebral aneurysm stroke, and traumatic brain injury [99 -105]. In a single-center feasibility study, epoetin alfa did not impact survival rates in 18 patients compared to 40 patients in a matched cohort; however, neurological recovery was higher (55% vs. 37.5%; p N 0.05) [106]. Another single-center study suggested that erythropoietin-beta administration within 2 min of cardiopulmonary resuscitation results in a higher rate of ROSC, ICU admission, and survival to discharge [107]. Contrary to these previously documented positive studies, the current study failed to replicate these single center results in a multicenter format.

The authors provide several potential reasons for a lack of benefit: differences in animal nervous systems, missing the therapeutic time window, and subtherapeutic dosage. In experimental models, the therapeutic window varied, but was observed up until 3 h. Although the median time to administration was 1.5 h, it is possible that the time window was missed. The dosage chosen of 200,000 units over a 48hour period was the highest studied dose and twice the dose used in stroke patients, thus it likely achieved adequate concentrations [101, 102]. Due to the lack of efficacy and increased complications, epoetin alfa cannot be recommended to improve neurologic outcomes in comatose survivors of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.

The Stop Antibiotics on Procalcitonin guidance Study was a multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial that evaluated the efficacy and safety of PCT-guided antibiotic treatment in critically ill patients in a Netherlands health-system with relatively low reported use of antibiotics. 1.7. Qureshi et al. intensive blood-pressure lowering in patients with acute cerebral hemorrhage [112]

The Antihypertensive Treatment of Acute Cerebral Hemorrhage II (ATACH-2) study was a multicenter, randomized, open-label trial comparing the efficacy of intensive to standard antihypertensive treatment in patients with spontaneous supratentorial intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) from May 2011 to September 2015. Patients were included if they had a GCS score of 5 or more, intraparenchymal hematoma volume of b 60 cm 3 on initial computerized tomographic scan, and had at least onereadingof a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 180 mm Hg or more between symptom onset and initiation of IV antihypertensive treatment. Nicardipine, administered by IV infusion, was the first-line antihypertensive agent. If the SBP was higher than the target despite the maximum dose of nicardipine (15 mg per hour) for 30 min, the prespecified second-line agent, IV labetalol, was used. In countries where labetalol was not available, IV diltiazem or urapidil was used. Initiation of IV antihypertensive treatment had to occur within 4.5 h of symptom onset. The trial initially recruited patients within 3 h of symptom onset, but this was extended to 4.5 h based on published data that suggested that the occurrence of hematoma expansion is equally prevalent in patients presenting between 0 and 3 h and those presenting between 3 and 4.5 h after symptom onset [113].

The goal of treatment was to reduce the SBP to 110 to 139 mm Hg in the intensive-treatment group and 140 to 170 mm Hg in the standardtreatment group for 24 h after randomization. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients who had moderately severe or severe disability (defined as a score of 4 to 6 on the modified Rankin scale) or who had died at 3 months. Primary treatment failure was defined as not reaching the target SBP within 2 h after randomization. Secondary outcomes were the scores on the European Quality of Life-5 Dimensions (EQ-5D) utility index (patients self-reported perception of health in 5 dimensions; scores range from -0.109 [least favorable health state] to 1 [most favorable health state]) and visual-analog scale (patients self-reported perception of their own health state on a scale of 0, worst imaginable health to 100, best imaginable health; a score of 0 was assigned to those who died) at 3 months and the proportion of patients with hematoma volume expansion of 33% or more. The authors estimated they would need a total of 1042 participants to see at least a 10% difference; however, the study was discontinued for futility after randomization of 1000 patients (500 patients in each group).

The mean age of enrolled patients was 61.9 years, 38% of patients were women and 56.2% of patients were Asian. Other baseline characteristics were similar between groups. Death or disability was observed in 186 patients (38.7%) in the intensive-treatment group and 181 patients (37.7%) in the standard-treatment group (p = 0.72). Primary treatment failure was seen in 12.2% of patients in the intensive-treatment group and 0.8% in the standard-treatment group (p b 0.001). The mean minimum SBP in the first 2 h after randomization was 128.9 mm Hg in the intensive-treatment group and 141.1 mm Hg in the standard-treatment group. There were no significant betweengroup differences in the EQ-5D measures, the percentage of patients with hematoma expansion, the rate of death at 3 months, or neurologic deterioration at 24 h after randomization. The only significant difference in adverse events was an increase in renal adverse events (e.g. acute renal failure) within 7 days after randomization in the intensive-treatment group compared to the standard-treatment group (9.0% vs. 4.0%; p = 0.002).

Previous studies have shown that elevated SBP is associated with hematoma expansion after ICH thereby increasing the risk of poor functional outcome and death [114 -120]. Two pilot studies, ATACH and INTERACT, evaluated the safety of rapidly reducing a patient ' s SBP to b 140 mm Hg and found it to be safe [121,122]. INTERACT2, the phase 3 trial published in 2013, evaluated patients presenting with ICH and SBP between 150 and 220 mm Hg [123]. Although there was no difference in the primary outcome of death or major disability between the intensive-treatment group (target SBP b 140 mm Hg within 1 h) and the standard-treatment group (target SBP b 180 mm Hg within 1 h), they did find a significant benefit in regard to quality of life in the intensive-treatment group in a secondary analysis.

When compared to INTERACT2, ATACH-2 included patients with higher initial SBPs and more effectively lowered the SBP in both the intensive- and standard-treatment groups. They did not show a difference in death or major disability. This may be because there was only a 12 mm Hg difference in the SBPs in both the intensive-and standardtreatment groups and the mean minimum SBP in the standard-treatment group was 141.1 mm Hg, making it difficult to truly assess the standard-treatment goal range of 140 -170 mm Hg. ATACH-2 also showed a non-significant trend toward less hematoma expansion but more renal adverse events in the intensive-treatment group. Based on the results of INTERACT2, the current AHA/ASA ICH guidelines state that acutely lowering a patient's SBP to b 140 mm Hg, if they present with an SBP between 150 and 220 mm Hg, is safe and can be effective for improving functional outcomes [92]. When incorporating the results of INTERACT2 and ATACH-2, it appears that intensively lowering the SBP may provide a benefit in regard to functional outcomes and it appears to have minimal adverse effects. Based on this, it would be reasonable to intensively lower the SBP to b 140 mm Hg. Considerations could be made for patients not included in these studies, such as those with pre-existing conditions that would be negatively affected by a drastic drop in blood pressure or those who have an initial SBP greater that 220 mm Hg.

1.9. Su et al. dexmedetomidine for prevention of delirium in elderly patients after non-cardiac surgery: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial [132]

This was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial conducted in China in two tertiary-care hospitals designed to determine whether prophylactic, low-dose dexmedetomidine can reduce the incidenceof delirium in patients older than 65 years following elective noncardiac surgery under general anesthesia. Eligible patients were randomized to either IV dexmedetomidine (0.1 mcg/kg/h on the day of ICU admission after surgery, starting at 2000 and ending at 0800 the next day, n = 350) or placebo (normal saline, n = 350). The study drug infusion was started in mechanically ventilated patients only after propofol or midazolam was titrated to a RASS of -2 or higher. Patients received patient-controlled or epidural analgesia postoperatively. For patients who did not receive patient -controlled analgesia or those who needed additional analgesia, morphine or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were administered. Propofol or midazolam was used as sedation in patients that were mechanically ventilated via bolus or IV infusion and titrated to a goal RASS between -2 and +1. Daily sedation interruption occurred in patients who were not extubated by the morning of postoperative day 1. Standard operating procedures including reorientation, cognitive stimulation, early mobilization, sleep-promotion, hearing or visual aids, and timely correction of visual aids to decrease the occurrence of delirium in the ICU were implemented. If patients developed postoperative delirium, non-pharmacological strategies were used first. If non-pharmacological therapy was ineffective, haloperidol was administered to those with severe agitation. The primary endpoint was the incidence of delirium in the first seven days following surgery. Adverse events such as bradycardia and hypotension were monitored for 24 h following surgery or until resolution of the event.

The incidence of postoperative delirium was lower in the dexmedetomidine group compared to the placebo group in the 7-day follow-up period (9.1% vs. 22.6%; OR 0.35; 95% CI 0.22 -0.54; p b 0.0001). The reduction in the incidence of delirium was still significant when patients were stratified according to intubation status on ICU admission. The incidence of delirium was reduced within the three motoric subtypes of delirium (hypoactive, hyperactive, and mixed) for dexmedetomidine-treated patients (p b 0.0001). The prevalence of non-delirium complications (medical events that required therapeutic intervention and occurred within 30 days following surgery) was lower in the dexmedetomidine group compared to the placebo group (15% vs. 21%; OR 0.67; 95% CI 0.45 -0.98; p = 0.039). Additionally, the mean length of ICU stay was shorter in the dexmedetomidine group compared to the placebo group (20.9 h, SD 0.5 vs. 21.5 h, SD 0.8; HR 1.18; 95% CI 1.02 -1.37; p = 0.027). The incidence of hypertension and tachycardia were lower in the dexmedetomidine group compared to the placebo group (9.7% vs. 17.7%; OR 0.50; 95% CI 0.32 -0.78; p = 0.002), (6.6% vs. 13.7%; OR 0.44; 95% CI 0.26 -0.75; p = 0.002), respectively.

Based on the available literature, low-dose dexmedetomidine may decrease the incidence of delirium in elderly patients during the first 7 days postoperatively following non-cardiac surgery regardless of intubation status or motoric type of delirium. Given the methodology of the study, propofol or midazolam would have to be utilized until goal RASS -2 to +1 before starting the dexmedetomidine in mechanically ventilated patients. Additionally, non-pharmacologic strategies would need to be utilized first in patients to prevent and treat delirium while receiving dexmedetomidine in addition to haloperidol if needed. Further research needs to be conducted to determine the long-term outcomes of this intervention.

1.10. Keh et al. effect of hydrocortisone on development of shock among patients with severe sepsis (HYPRESS) [133]

The Hydrocortisone for Prevention of Septic Shock (HYPRESS) study was a multicenter, placebo-controlled, double-blind, randomized clinical trial evaluating the addition of hydrocortisone on progression to septic shock among patients with severe sepsis. This study was initiated prior to the revision of the consensus definitions of ' sepsis ' and ' septic shock ' and also prior to the publication of the 2016 Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) guidelines. Because of this, the terms ' severe sepsis ' and ' septic shock ' in this study were based upon the definitions from the 2012 SSC guidelines [134 -136]. In addition, the 2016 SSC guidelines utilized the term ' sepsis ' for the patients in the HYPRESS study that were in the ' severe sepsis ' group [137]. Patients in the treatment group received a hydrocortisone taper that started with a 50 mg IV bolus followed by a 24-hour continuous infusion consisting of 200 mg on days 1 to 5, 100 mg on days 6 and 7, 50 mg on days 8 and 9, and 25 mg on days 10 and 11. Hydrocortisone was given as a continuous infusion following the recommendations in the 2012 SSC guidelines and tapered according to the recommendations in the CORTICUS trial [134, 135]. The primary outcome was the progression from severe sepsis to septic shock within 14 days. Patients were evaluated daily for the developmentof septic shock for up to 14 days or until discharge from the ICU. Secondary outcomes included time until septic shock, ICU or hospital mortality, survival up to 180 days, assessment of secondary infections, weaning failure, muscle weakness and hyperglycemia.

A total of 380 patients were randomized (190 per group), with 353 analyzed in the intention-to-treat analysis. The baseline characteristics including organ dysfunction, source of infection, and glucocorticoid use prior to ICU admission were similar between both groups.

Regarding the primary infectious etiology, there was less pneumonia and more thoracic, intra-abdominal, and catheter-related infections in the hydrocortisone group, compared to the placebo group (p N 0.05). There was no significant difference in the treatment groups regarding progression to the primary endpoint of septic shock. In the intentionto-treat population, septic shock occurred in 36 of 170 patients (21.2%) in the hydrocortisone group compared to 39 of 170 patients (22.9%) in the placebo group (difference -1.8%; 95% CI -10.7 -+7.2%; p = 0.70). In the per-protocol population, septic shock occurred in 29 of 155 patients (18.7%) in the hydrocortisone group compared to 33 of 156 patients (21.2%) in the placebo group (difference -2.4%; 95% CI -11.5 -+6.6; p = 0.59). Subgroup analyses evaluating medical, surgical, pneumonia patients, or treatment with study medication for 48 h showed no benefit for prevention of septic shock.

The HYPRESS study is the first to investigate whether the use of hydrocortisone in patients with severe sepsis will prevent progression to septic shock. There have been many studies conducted that have investigated the use of steroids in various stages of sepsis and septic shock and several reviews of the medical literature evaluating those studies andtheir outcomes. The quality of evidence of these studies and the outcomes has been mixed. The recommendations from the recent 2016 SSC guidelines are to treat septic shock with hydrocortisone only in adult patients who have not had an adequate response to fluid resuscitation and vasopressor therapy [137]. The 2016 SSC guideline specifically references the HYPRESS study and states that steroids should not be used in septic patients to prevent septic shock. Use of hydrocortisone as investigated in this study is not recommended in severe sepsis and only in specific clinical situations in septic shock (e.g. history of steroid therapy or adrenal dysfunction). Furthermore, this study concluded that in adult patients with severe sepsis, the use of hydrocortisone showed no difference in incidence of patients progressing to septic shock within 14 days. Based upon the HYPRESS study results and the recommendations of the most recent SSC guidelines, hydrocortisone for severe sepsis to prevent the progression to septic shock is not clinically indicated.
