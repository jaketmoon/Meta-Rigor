Postoperative delirium is a common complication affecting 10 -50% of patients undergoing major surgery [1]. Delirium is a risk-factor for increased morbidity, mortality, loss of functional independence and persistent neurocognitive decline beyond 30 postoperative days [2]. The economic burden is estimated to be € 182 billion annually in Europe and $164 billion in the USA due to prolonged hospitalisation, higher nursing and medical interventions, post-discharge institutionalisation and loss of productivity; or £ 37,000/ € 40,500/USD$50,800 per person diagnosed with delirium [1,3].

This topic has thus deservedly received much research and clinical attention, with 932 individual citations in PubMed for ' postoperative delirium ' in the last 10 years. The literature describes two broad categories of preventive therapies: multimodal, pragmatic care bundles; and single therapy (usually pharmacological) interventions. Examples of peri-operative care bundles have included: the Hospital Elder Life Program (orientation, nutritional support, early mobilisation) after abdominal surgery [4]; and a multidisciplinary approach to hip fracture surgery (fascia iliaca blocks, rationalising prescribing practices and avoidance of triggers) [5]. Investigations of single interventions have included: benzodiazepine restriction [6];

intra-operative use of the inhalational anaesthetic agent, xenon [7]; depth of anaesthesia monitoring [8]; and dexmedetomidine given at various time-points in the perioperative period [9].

In this issue of Anaesthesia , van Norden et al. [10] report on the results of a small randomised controlled trial (60 patients analysed) comparing dexmedetomidine vs. saline placebo. However, to appreciate how dexmedetomidine may bene fi t, it is useful to review the mechanistic pathways implicated in delirium.

Pathophysiology of postoperative delirium

The pathophysiology of delirium is complex and poorly understood. While several theories have been proposed over the last half century, none have precipitated signi fi cant changes to clinical practice. The primary barrier has been a lack of clinical translational studies and mechanistic animal models. Fortunately, multiple research teams are addressing this de fi cit with many advances in delirium science in 2020 alone providing new evidence supporting the two primary hypotheses for delirium pathogenesis. The fi rst is the ' cerebral metabolic insuf fi ciency hypothesis ' , which suggests that metabolic failure of the brain is the critical pathogenic step in delirium [11]. Recent research showing that delirium is associated with neuronal (and cardiac) injury [12] and covert stroke [13] supports this as well as further research into the changes in brain activity. The other major theory is the ' neuro-in fl ammation hypothesis of delirium ' [12], which may explain more generally how systemic in fl ammatory insults can induce metabolic failure of the brain. Ultimately, it appears likely that neuro-in fl ammation and metabolic impairment are intrinsically related in their roles in inducing delirium (Fig. 1).

Further stumbling blocks to our understanding are: the heterogeneous causes of delirium; different blends of predisposing factors (such as cognitive decline or dementia); and that precipitating factors such as surgery or infection may combine to produce different symptoms that can be grouped together as delirium. Of course, this means that therapies targeting precipitating factors, such as surgery, may have different effects dependent on the prevalence of different predisposing factors in a trial. For example, one would anticipate that alcohol withdrawal and surgery would have very different treatments. One can also imagine that the impact of anti-in fl ammatory therapies may vary based on the extent to which pre-surgery cognition is impaired in an individual. For this reason, it is commendable that van Norden et al. [10] reported pre-operative cognitive data as part of their study.

If we consider that dementia and cognitive decline are on a neurodegenerative disease spectrum of metabolic insuf fi ciency, pre-operative impairments in cerebral metabolism could predispose to delirium. In the setting of pre-operative neurodegeneration, small degrees of in fl ammation may still induce delirium. In the peri-operative period, accumulating evidence suggests that systemic in fl ammation associated with surgery and bleeding leads to delirium, potentially leading to disruption of the blood -brain barrier, in fl ux of immune mediators with subsequent changes in neuronal metabolism and excitability. Hence, while van Norden et al. [10] hypothesise that dexmedetomidine may act to reduce in fl ammation to prevent delirium, it is a shame they did not validate their hypothesis by measurement of in fl ammatory changes. This sort of mechanistic approach would be valuable in the context of a small trial and would enhance the plausibility of the results.

More recent work has shown that systemic in fl ammation correlates with electroencephalogram (EEG) slowing, and with neuronal injury biomarkers, neuro fi lament light and tau [14]. The underlying synaptic and energetic changes for EEG slowing remain obscure, though it appears likely that there is a general shift towards suppression of excitability, perhaps mediated through prostaglandins and adenosine signalling [15]. In parallel, in fl ammation may directly hinder cerebral bioenergetics (which is also plausibly linked to adenosine pathways). Understanding these potential pathways may stimulate novel therapies for delirium, and perhaps, subsequent cognitive decline and dementia.

Dexmedetomidine

Interest in dexmedetomidine in reducing delirium stems from its neuroprotective effect via anti-in fl ammatory and anti-apoptotic extracellular protein kinases [16]; improving sleep quality by inducing a sedative state most closely resembling natural sleep [17]; and reducing the consumption of other sedatives and opioids from a 2adrenoreceptor mediated effects. Recent meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials conclude a bene fi cial effect of dexmedetomidine to reduce delirium in intensive care patients [18] and after major surgery [9].

The results reported by van Norden et al. are in keeping with these conclusions, fi nding a reduction in delirium in patients receiving dexmedetomidine. This was administered as a 48-h infusion (0.2 -1.4 µ g.kg -1 .h -1 ) which was started at the same time as induction of anaesthesia, continuously titrated against haemodynamic variables intraoperatively and sedation scores postoperatively. Patients with known risk factors for postoperative delirium were screened and were not included (pre-existing cognitive impairment, alcohol misuse and audio-visual impairment), and delirium was diagnosed as a dichotomous outcome using the Confusion Assessment Method tool administered twice daily up to postoperative day fi ve. Data veracity was checked by independent assessors. The question is, does this all provide further evidence for peri-operative intravenous dexmedetomidine as a magic bullet for the prevention of postoperative delirium?

A more guarded viewpoint would highlight several limitations. Firstly, recruited patients were a mix of 77% major abdominal and 23% on-pump cardiac coronary revascularisation surgery; but the power calculation was made using an estimation from a cardiac surgery-speci fi c trial. Secondly, 40% of patients were recorded as having protocol violations, including serious violations of wrong treatment assignment, variations in the dexmedetomidine infusion duration, and incorrect recruitment criteria being applied. Thirdly, the sample size for the primary outcome was not achieved. Finally, the small trial size exhibits statistical fragility; when calculated (https://clincalc.com/Sta ts/FragilityIndex.aspx) from the reported primary outcome results, the fragility score is zero -suggesting that a much larger sample size is necessary to provide con fi dence that the results are truly due to the dexmedetomidine intervention. These limitations have been appropriately noted by the authors. Other methodological issues that make comparisons between studies more dif fi cult include dissimilar dosing schedules and uncontrolled patient management outside of the intervention: any bene fi t from dexmedetomidine may be lost when patients are not kept to delirium minimisation best practices when they leave the operating theatre [5]. These may have contributed to the outcome of the DECADE study (dexmedetomidine for reduction of atrial fi brillation and delirium after cardiac surgery) which was terminated early at 798 patients due to futility [19]. Thus, while a single therapeutic intervention is highly appealing to prevent delirium, given the complexity of the mechanistic pathways, it is perhaps too optimistic to wish for a magic bullet.

Nonetheless, the study by van Norden et al. is a valuable contribution to ongoing efforts to determine the effectiveness of dexmedetomidine. The authors titrated the infusion to endpoints rather than a fi xed dosing regimen, used a suf fi cient duration of exposure for biological plausibility and employed robust follow up. The health, social and economic costs will be a continual driver for research that reduces the impact of delirium on our patients.
