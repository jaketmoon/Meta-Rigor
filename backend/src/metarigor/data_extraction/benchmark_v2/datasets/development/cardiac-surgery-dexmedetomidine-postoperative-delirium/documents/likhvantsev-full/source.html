Objective

Conflicting data exist on the effect of dexmedetomidine on delirium. For the present study, a randomized trial was performed to investigate the effect of perioperative dexmedetomidine on the rate of postoperative delirium after cardiac surgery.

Design

A randomized controlled trial.

Setting

University hospital.

Participants

Patients (n = 169) undergoing elective cardiac surgery (coronary artery bypass graft surgery, valve surgery, or combined surgery) with cardiopulmonary bypass.

Interventions

Patients received a sevoflurane-based general anesthesia and were randomly assigned 1:1 to receive a dexmedetomidine infusion that started in the operating room (0.7 μg/kg/h) and continued into the intensive care unit (0.4 μg/kg/h) or an equivolume infusion of placebo.

Measurements and Main Results

A decrease in the rate of delirium in the dexmedetomidine group compared with the placebo group was demonstrated (6 of 84 [7.1%] v 16 of 85 [18.8%]; p = 0.02; odds ratio [OR] 0.33 [95% confidence interval {CI} 0.12-0.90]). Reduced intensive care unit and hospital lengths of stay also were observed (18 [18-22] hours v 22 [18-39] hours; p = 0.002 and 17 [7-20] days v 19 [8-21] days; p = 0.04, respectively). Mortality at 30 days was 2 (2.4%) in both groups. On multivariate analysis, only dexmedetomidine administration (OR 0.24 [95% CI 0.08-0.74]) and cardiopulmonary bypass time (OR 1.02 [95% CI 1.01-1.03] for increases of 1 min) were independent predictors of delirium development.

Conclusions

Dexmedetomidine administered during and after general anesthesia for cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass decreased the rate of postoperative delirium and intensive care unit and hospital lengths of stay.

POSTOPERATIVE delirium (POD) is an acute and fluctuating alteration of mental state with reduced awareness and disturbance of attention.1 Worldwide, more than 230 million people undergo major surgery every year, including more than 80 million in Europe.2, 3, 4 The incidence of POD varies because of a multitude of perioperative risk factors and settings5, 6, 7 and ranges between 9% and 52% after cardiac surgery.8,9
Despite the fact that POD often is considered to be only a temporary attenuation of brain function followed by full remission, it is in fact a severe complication that reduces cognitive function of patients at short-10 and long-term follow-up,11,12 and is associated with prolonged hospitalization.13 According to randomized evidence, there are no indications supporting any drug as a preventive measure for POD,14 with the possible exception of dexmedetomidine.15
Dexmedetomidine is an alpha-agonist that can provide light to moderate sedation in intensive care unit (ICU) settings.16,17 Because dexmedetomidine has analgesic and organ protection properties,18, 19, 20 it also has been used in surgical settings.21 The evidence on the preventive effect of dexmedetomidine on POD is accumulating,22 but few high-quality studies exist on the administration of dexmedetomidine during cardiac surgery to prevent POD, and not all of the studies support the hypothesis that this drug improves the outcome.22, 23, 24
Therefore, the authors performed a randomized, double blind-trial to study the effect of dexmedetomidine started at anesthesia induction and continued in the ICU on POD after cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A single-center, double-blind, parallel-group, randomized clinical trial was performed to test the effects of perioperative dexmedetomidine treatment on POD in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. The study started after obtaining local ethical committee approval of the Moscow Regional Research and Clinical Institute and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03091166). The study was conceived according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki,25 and the present report follows the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for reporting parallel group randomized trials.26

Participants

Patients older than 45 years undergoing elective coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or valve surgery or a combination of the 2 with CPB who signed an informed consent were eligible for the trial. Exclusion criteria were evidence of preoperative mental impairment (all patients were visited by a psychiatrist before surgery) and known allergy to the study drug. Patients who underwent a second surgery (surgical revision for bleeding or to fix a surgical problem) before ICU discharge also were excluded from the trial.

Intervention

Patients randomly assigned to the intervention group received an infusion of dexmedetomidine (Dexdor; Orion Pharma, Espoo Finland) 100 mg/mL (200 mg were diluted in 50 mL of 0.9% normal saline), which was started at anesthesia induction without bolus or loading dose and lasted throughout the procedure, including during CPB. The infusion rate was fixed at 0.7 μg/kg/h in the operating room starting at anesthesia induction. In the ICU the starting dose was 0.4 μg/kg/h, with a possible range of infusion of 0.4 to 1.4 μg/kg/h at the discretion of the attending physician. Patients randomly assigned to the control group received placebo (normal saline) with identical volumes, rates of infusion, and possibility to change the dose in the ICU (Fig 1). Infusion of the study drug lasted until the beginning of ventilation weaning in the ICU, which started after patient awareness, restoration of muscle tone, and the emergence of attempts to self-breathe.
No crossover and no open label use of dexmedetomidine was allowed either in the operating room or in the ICU, except if delirium was diagnosed in the ICU. In these patients who had already reached the primary endpoint of the study, the caring physician became unblinded. Management of delirium in patients in the treatment group was with dexmedetomidine open label (starting dose 0.7 μg/kg/h with a possible range of 0.2-1.4 μg/kg/h), whereas patients in the control group had delirium managed with low-dose propofol (0.5-3.0 mg/kg/h). Haloperidol and benzodiazepines were used for delirium management if required.

Outcomes

All outcomes measures were classified following the European Society of Anesthesiology – European Society of Intensive Care Medicine joint taskforce on perioperative outcome measures.27
The primary outcome was the rate of POD. The Confusion Assessment Method for Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) method was used for the diagnosis of delirium twice a day during the first 5 days postoperatively according to European Society of Anesthesiology guidelines.14 The POD rate was defined as the number of patients who scored at least 3 of 4 points on the CAM-ICU test on at least 1 occasion. Patients first were evaluated for an altered level of consciousness in the ICU after waking up and twice a day for 5 days thereafter (either in the ICU or in the main ward). The CAM-ICU checklist includes 4 paragraphs, each of them elucidating 1 of the following essential features of delirium: alteration/fluctuation in mental status, inattention, altered level of consciousness, and disorganized thinking (details in Supplemental Material).
In patients with an established delirium diagnosis on the CAM-ICU scale, delirium severity also was assessed with the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist. Patients received 1 point for each of the following criteria experienced: inattention, disorientation, hallucination-delusion-psychosis, psychomotor agitation or retardation, inappropriate speech or mood, sleep/wake cycle disturbance, or symptom fluctuation.28
All these tests were performed by trained senior anesthesiologists, who were members of the investigator team and blinded to the group assignment. In addition, all ICU staff were trained on the diagnosis of POD, and they informed the senior anesthesiologist if they suspected delirium at any time. The members of the investigator team were the only ones allowed to diagnose delirium for study purposes. The diagnosis of POD was irreversible (once the diagnosis was made, it was not possible to reject it).
Secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality at 30 days, hospital and ICU lengths of stay, the number of patients receiving renal replacement therapy, major adverse cardiac and cerebral events (MACCE), and the vasopressor-inotrope score during the ICU stay. A phone follow-up was performed 30 days after the surgery to assess vital status. If loss to follow-up by telephone occurred, different attempts were made to contact study participants, including contacting the patient's general practitioner, contacting the city municipality, and sending a letter to the patient's home address. Hospital and ICU stay were calculated from the surgical day and are expressed as median and interquartile range. The indication for renal replacement therapy initiation followed the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes classification of acute kidney injury stage 3, which is defined as oliguria (urine output <0.3 mL/kg/h or <500 mL/d) for >24 hours; anuria (urine output <100 mL/d) for >12 hours; or serum creatinine >4 mg/dL (354 µmol/L) or >3 times the baseline.27 MACCE was a composite endpoint of nonfatal cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, heart block or ventricular arrhythmias, and stroke (each definition is expanded later). A simplified vasopressor-inotrope score, defined as dobutamine dose (μg/kg/min) + 100 × epinephrine dose (μg/kg/min) + 100 × norepinephrine dose (μg/kg/min), was used.29
Tertiary outcomes included time on mechanical ventilation (hours from ICU arrival to extubation); a composite outcome of delirium + MACCE + renal replacement therapy + 30-day mortality; and the single components of the composite outcome MACCE.27 Stroke was defined as an embolic, thrombotic, or hemorrhagic cerebral event with persistent residual motor, sensory, or cognitive dysfunction (eg, hemiplegia, hemiparesis, aphasia, sensory deficit, impaired memory). Acute myocardial infarction was defined as elevation of Cardiac Troponin values >10 times the 99th percentile together with at least 1 of the following: new pathologic Q waves, angiographic-documented new graft occlusion or new native coronary artery occlusion, imaging evidence of new loss of viable myocardium, or new regional wall motion abnormality in a pattern consistent with an ischemic etiology.30 Nonfatal cardiac arrest was defined as the absence of cardiac rhythm or presence of chaotic rhythm necessitating any component of basic or advanced cardiac life support.
In the electronic case reporting form, demographics, past medical history, preoperative risk factors, preoperative medications, and procedural characteristics of each patient were collected.

Sample Size

The sample size was calculated on the primary endpoint delirium and was based on a previously published rate of POD of 25% versus 9% in patients who received propofol or dexmedetomidine after cardiac surgery, which the authors considered to be clinically relevant.7 The calculated sample size was 86 patients in each group with an 80% power (β = 0.2) and a significance level (α) of 0.05. The present study comprised 175 patients to account for possible postoperative reinterventions and protocol deviations.

Randomization and Blinding

A randomization sequence was created by permuted-block randomization with a block size of 20 and a 1:1 allocation generated by computer. The allocation sequence was prepared by an independent operator not otherwise involved in the trial and was concealed by opaque sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes. Patients were screened for eligibility and were asked to sign an informed consent to participate in the trial on the day before surgery. Patients were randomized into the trial after they entered the operating room by dedicated study personnel. Patients were randomly allocated to the intervention or placebo group by assigning them the sequentially numbered envelope with the lowest numeration. Trial participants, care providers, and data collectors were blinded to group assignment. The treatment was prepared by a nurse who was not involved in the study or care of the patient; that nurse opened the sealed envelope and prepared the treatment. The syringe for the continuous intravenous infusion was labeled only with the acronym of the trial and the number of randomization of the patient; syringes containing dexmedetomidine and those containing placebo were indistinguishable (dexmedetomidine is a colorless substance indistinguishable from saline). After that, the envelope was resealed.

Statistical Analysis

Normality of variables distribution was estimated with the D'Agostino-Pearson test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range] according to the distribution. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the independent Student t or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as percentages and were compared using the chi-square or Fisher exact test (2-tailed). Univariate and multivariate logistic regressions were performed to evaluate the associations of demographic, therapeutic, and clinical outcome variables. The primary outcome (delirium) also was expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data analysis was performed with Statistica 10.0 (StatSoft, Inc, Tulsa, OK) and MedCalc 12.5.0.0 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium) by an independent statistician. For the primary outcome, the Fragility Index (how many patients in the dexmedetomidine group would have to change from not having delirium to having delirium to reverse statistical significance) also was conducted. An analysis to assess whether dexmedetomidine was an independent predictor of POD was performed through a multivariable regression analysis with a stepwise method. All preoperative data with a p value <0.1 at the univariable analysis were entered into the model.

Routine Preoperative, Intraoperative, and Postoperative Management in Both Groups

Patients were admitted to the hospital the day before surgery. Aspirin and beta-blockers were continued until the morning of surgery, and other cardioactive and vasoactive drugs were not administered starting from the day before the intervention.
Premedication with morphine was administered 30 minutes before surgery only in selected anxious patients. Fentanyl, 4 μg/kg, propofol, and rocuronium, 0.6 mg/kg, were used to induce anesthesia. After intubation, continuous mandatory volume ventilation mode was applied throughout the procedure with a tidal volume of 6 to 8 mL/kg, a respiratory rate of 12 to 14 per minute (target pCO240 mmHg), and a FiO2 of 0.4 (target pO2 <200 mmHg) with fresh gas flow of 4 L/min. Anesthesia maintenance consisted of sevoflurane (target of 1.0 minimal alveolar concentration) and fentanyl before CPB and propofol and fentanyl during CPB. Perioperative continuous monitoring consisted of heart rate, 5-lead electrocardiography with ST analysis, invasive blood pressure through a radial artery catheter, pulse oximetry, central venous pressure through internal jugular vein cannulation, tidal volume, end-tidal carbon dioxide, inspiratory oxygen, end-tidal sevoflurane concentration, and body temperature.
In patients with an ejection fraction <50%, cardiac output was measured with thermodilution, and pulmonary artery and wedge pressures also were measured. In other patients, cardiac function was monitored through echocardiography.
Patients underwent CPB with normothermia or mild hypothermia with Stroker HLM Cobe (Sorin Group, Milan, Italy) and Jostra HL20 (Maquet, Rastatt, Germany) heart-lung machines and cardioplegia with Custodiol (Dr. Franz Köhler Chemie GmbH, Bensheim, Germany) or Console (Samson-Med Ltd, St Petersburg, Russia) solutions. Three surgeons were the first operators, and 2 anesthesiologists were responsible for providing anesthesia throughout the study period.
Hypotensive (mean arterial pressure <65 mmHg) patients were tested for hypovolemia (volume loading or passive leg raise test) and treated with fluids if indicated. Normovolemic hypotensive patients received norepinephrine (starting dose 0.02 µg/kg/min with increments of 0.02 µg/kg/min) if cardiac output >3 L/min/m2 or dobutamine (starting dose 5 µg/kg/min). Diagnosis of low cardiac output syndrome was performed after excluding or correcting hypothermia; hypovolemia; cardiac rhythm abnormalities (eg, bradycardia); cardiac tamponade; or myocardial ischemia. Low cardiac output syndrome was treated with dobutamine at an initial dose of 5 μg/kg/min. Epinephrine infusion was added after the dobutamine dose reached 15 μg/kg/min.
No sedation was performed in the ICU on top of the study drug infusion (dexmedetomidine 0.4 μg/kg/h in the treatment group and an equivolume of normal saline in the placebo group). No propofol was administered in the ICU except for patients in whom delirium was diagnosed after ICU admission. No prophylactic antipsychotics were allowed either in the operating room or the ICU until the diagnosis of delirium was established. Morphine and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were used for analgesia in the ICU. Pain always was assessed and treated before assessing for delirium (Visual Analog Scale target ≤3), and the dose of any administered morphine was collected.
Patients were switched to pressure support ventilation mode and weaned after recovering awareness, having restoration of muscle tone, attempting to self-breathe, and having the following parameters: FiO2 ≤0.4; positive end-expiratory pressure ≤5 mmHg; and SpO2 ≥98%. Pressure support was reduced progressively to 6 to 7 mmHg, and then a T tube spontaneous breathing trial was attempted. Patients were extubated if PaO2/FiO2 was >250, the respiratory rate was <25, and the arterial oxygen saturation was 98% to 100% at FiO2 0.4 after a 1-hour T tube trial.
To evaluate whether the patient was ready for ICU discharge, the Jacobsen method was followed.31 This checklist includes 5 semiobjective variables (pain, motor function, sedation, respiration, and nausea) and 7 objective variables (temperature, peripheral saturation, diuresis, heart rate, arterial blood pressure, cardiac index, and postoperative drainage). Variables are scored 0 to 3, and evaluation started within 30 minutes after extubation and was repeated as often as the intensivist considered it to be necessary. Patients were fit for ICU discharge with a score of 4 or less (Supplemental Table 1). Patients with ongoing delirium were not discharged from the ICU.

Results

In the period December 2016 to March 2019, 175 patients were randomly assigned to the present study (Fig 2). Six patients (3 in each group) were excluded from the analyses because they underwent reintervention (had a prespecified exclusion criteria). No crossover and no mistake in the study drug administration occurred.
The groups were well-balanced in terms of demographic data, medical history, and preoperative medications (Table 1). No significant difference between the groups was detected regarding the doses of anesthetics and analgesics administered during all stages of anesthesia and postoperatively (Table 2). CABG was the most commonly performed surgery (56%) followed by isolated valve replacement surgery (34%). CPB and cross-clamping times also were similar.

Time on mechanical ventilation was shorter in the dexmedetomidine group versus the placebo group (13 [12-17] hours v 17 [14-29] hours; p < 0.001). ICU and hospital lengths of stay were shorter in the dexmedetomidine group compared with the placebo group (18 [18-22] hours v 22 [18-39] hours; p = 0.002 and 17 [7-20] days v 19 [8-21] days; p = 0.04, respectively) (see Table 3). Two patients in each group died in the hospital (2.4% v 2.4%; p = 0.99).
According to multiple logistic regression analysis (see Supplemental Material), only dexmedetomidine administration (OR 0.24 [95% CI 0.08-0.74]) and CPB time (OR 1.02 [95% CI 1.01-1.03] for increases of 1 min) were independently associated with the development of delirium.

Discussion

The main finding of the present study is that perioperative dexmedetomidine infusion, started at anesthesia induction and ended at initiation of ventilator weaning in the ICU, is associated with a decreased rate of delirium in cardiac surgery patients. Decreased ICU and hospital lengths of stay also were demonstrated in the dexmedetomidine group. This study is one of the first randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that have examined the effect of dexmedetomidine started in the operating room and the largest one documenting a reduction in POD.
Dexmedetomidine has been studied extensively in several settings, and neuroprotective properties have been documented.32,33 The exact mechanism of neuroprotection still is under investigation. Kim et al. suggested that dexmedetomidine may inhibit inflammation by inactivation of the TLR-4/NF-kappaB pathway.34 Dahmani et al. postulated another mechanism of neuroprotection, suggesting that dexmedetomidine might increase hippocampal phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase 1 and 2 content by an alpha 2-adrenoceptor–independent mechanism.35 These neuroprotective properties can help explain why dexmedetomidine can prevent delirium and provide better pain management and the avoidance or reduction in doses of drugs associated with the increase in delirium (eg, benzodiazepines and opioids).
Retrospective cohort studies have evaluated the effectiveness of dexmedetomidine in perioperative settings. The most important case series was a single-center, retrospective cohort study involving 1,260 cardiac surgery patients who were administered a dexmedetomidine infusion that started after CPB; the study showed a significant reduction in the rate of delirium (7.4% v 5.4%; adjusted OR 0.53; 95% CI 0.37-0.75; p = 0.003).36 Subgroup analyses confirmed these findings in 724 patients undergoing CABG surgery (reduction in delirium from 7.9% to 4.6%; adjusted OR 0.431; 95% CI 0.265-0.701; p = 0.0007)37 and in elderly patients (reduction in delirium from 10.95% to 7.21%; OR 0.35 [95% CI 0.212-0.578]; p = 0.0001).38 A drawback of these studies was their retrospective design. The present study had similar findings but with a high-quality randomized, double-blind design.
An extensive and updated literature review39 identified 7 RCTs that evaluated the use of dexmedetomidine in adult cardiac surgery settings to prevent delirium.40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46 Most studies used dexmedetomidine in the ICU,40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45 whereas only 1 study46 focused on the perioperative use of dexmedetomidine. The dose ranged from 0.15 to 0.8 μg/kg/h. Even if these studies were overall in favor of dexmedetomidine, the findings of the study performed in the perioperative period was not conclusive.46 Li et al. performed an RCT comparing intraoperative and postoperative dexmedetomidine versus placebo in cardiac surgery.46 The reduction in delirium of 4.9% [7/142] in the dexmedetomidine group versus 7.7% [11/143] in the placebo group did not reach statistical significance (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.23-1.65; p = 0.341), possibly because the study was underpowered.
In the present study, dexmedetomidine administration was started before CPB, and the authors believe that this timing is crucial for delirium prevention because its action might be mediated by preconditioning properties,42 which usually require some time to establish.43
The possible effects of dexmedetomidine on survival and major postoperative complications are still a matter of debate. Cheng et al.38 reported a decreased in-hospital mortality after cardiac surgery in patients who received dexmedetomidine as part of anesthesia. Cho et al.47 suggested that dexmedetomidine infusion can reduce a composite outcome of major complications. The present study was not powered enough to confirm those findings, but it demonstrated a reduction in ICU and hospital lengths of stay in the dexmedetomidine group. The study also showed that dexmedetomidine supplement, type of surgery, and CPB duration were the only factors independently associated with delirium occurrence.
Duan et al. recently published a systemic review and meta-analysis of all RCTs (13 trials, 3,309 patients overall) performed in a cardiac surgery setting suggesting that dexmedetomidine decreases the risk of POD (OR 0.41 [95% CI 0.26-0.63]).48 Notably, the majority of the included studies administered dexmedetomidine only in the ICU.
The effect of dexmedetomidine in general ICU patients was supported by the American Society of Critical Care Medicine, which recommended the use of dexmedetomidine in mechanically ventilated adult ICU patients with delirium unrelated to alcoholism or benzodiazepine withdrawal (grade of recommendation 2B).49 However, the findings of the recent large RCT Sedation Practice in Intensive Care Evaluation III did not support this recommendation.50 The authors of that study did not find differences in the primary outcome of 180-day mortality, even if they reported that at day 28 the median number of days that patients were free from coma or delirium and the median number of ventilator-free days was 1 day greater in the dexmedetomidine group. The present study differs from those ICU experiences not only because it was performed in a cardiac surgery setting but also because dexmedetomidine was used as a preventive treatment. Nonetheless, the positive findings support the overall delirium-limiting properties of dexmedetomidine.
The present study has strengths and limitations. First, the study was not powered enough to investigate differences in mortality or other major complications. The findings on the difference in the delirium rate also are to be interpreted with caution because there were only 18 patients with delirium overall, and this result has a Fragility Index of 2 (2 patients in the dexmedetomidine group would have to change from not having delirium to having delirium to reverse statistical significance), even if this was an expected finding according to the power calculation and is in line with current evidence in the anesthesia field.51 Furthermore, the study had a randomized design and is one of the few studies that started the dexmedetomidine infusion at anesthesia induction; this enhanced the effect of the drug and can account for the positive findings reported herein. Data were collected on vasopressors and inotropes but not on vasodilators. Pain was assessed routinely and at ICU discharge, but these data and details on pain killers were not collected or reported. In addition, details on administered antipsychotics in the placebo and dexmedetomidine groups were not collected, but these drugs were administered to patients with diagnosed delirium only (14 and 4 patients, respectively). Even though all the patients were evaluated at baseline by a psychiatrist, the present study did not collect and report data on preoperative cognitive status at baseline.

Conclusions

An RCT was performed in cardiac surgery patients and suggests that starting dexmedetomidine before CPB as a supplement to inhalation anesthesia decreases the delirium rate and shortens the ICU and hospital lengths of stay.
