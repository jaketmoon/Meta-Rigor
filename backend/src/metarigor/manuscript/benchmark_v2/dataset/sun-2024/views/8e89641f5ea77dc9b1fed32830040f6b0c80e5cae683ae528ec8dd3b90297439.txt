Optimizing cerebral oxygenation in cardiac surgery: A randomized controlled trial examining neurocognitive and perioperative outcomes

Suzan Uysal, PhD, a Hung-Mo Lin, PhD, b Muoi Trinh, MD, a Chang H. Park, MD, a and David L. Reich, MD a

ABSTRACT

Objective: The study objective was to determine whether targeted therapy to optimize cerebral oxygenation is associated with improved neurocognitive and perioperative outcomes.

Methods: In a prospective trial, intraoperative cerebral oximetry monitoring using bilateral forehead probes was performed in cardiac surgical patients who were randomly assigned to an intervention group in which episodes of cerebral oxygen desaturation ( < 60 % for > 60 consecutive seconds at either probe) triggered an intervention protocol or a control group in which the cerebral oximetry data were hidden from the clinical team, and no intervention protocol was applied. Cognitive testing was performed preoperatively and at postoperative months 3 and 6; domains studied were response speed, processing speed, attention, and memory. Perioperative outcomes studied were death, hospital length of stay, intensive care unit length of stay, postoperative day of extubation, time on mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit delirium, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment on intensive care unit admission, and intensive care unit blood transfusion.

Results: Group mean memory change scores were significantly better in the intervention group at 6 months (0.60 [standard error, 0.30] vs − 0.17 [standard error, 0.33], adjusted P ¼ .008). However, presence, duration, and severity of cerebral desaturation were not associated with cognitive change scores. Perioperative outcomes did not differ between the intervention and control groups.

Conclusions: Targeted therapy to optimize cerebral oxygenation was associated with better memory outcome in a group of cardiac surgical patients. Some aspects of the protocol other than desaturation duration and severity contributed to the observed neuroprotective effect. (J Thorac Cardiovasc Surg 2020;159:943-53)

Central Message

In cardiac surgical patients, a NIRS-guided treatment algorithm was associated with better cognitive outcome, although presence, duration, and severity of desaturation were not predictors.

Perspective

NIRS monitoring and a treatment algorithm had a neuroprotective effect on cognitive outcome in a group of cardiac surgical patients. Presence, duration, and severity of cerebral desaturation per se were not associated with neurocognitive outcome, indicating that aspects of the treatment algorithm for cerebral oxygenation optimization were contributory.

(NIRS) technology measures regional saturation of oxygen (rSO2) at the microvascular level. 2 It is continuous, noninvasive, available at the point of care, and a sensitive index of cerebral hypoxia or cerebral ischemia, the main causes of brain injury in cardiac surgical patients. 3,4

NIRS oximetry monitoring holds promise for guiding interventions to improve intraoperative brain oxygenation and clinical outcomes, particularly POCD. A metaanalysis of 4 randomized controlled trials performed in cardiac surgical patients found that intraoperative management guided by cerebral oximetry was associated with reduced incidence of POCD (risk ratio, 0.55; 95 % confidence interval [CI], 0.36-0.86; P ¼ .0009; I 2 ¼ 85 % ) at hospital discharge or 1 week postoperatively. 5

This prospective randomized controlled trial in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB investigated whether a treatment algorithm targeted to optimize intraoperative cerebral oxygenation intervention (''targeted therapy'') was associated with better neurocognitive and perioperative outcomes compared with standard care. Cognitive outcomes studied were response speed, processing speed, attention, and memory. Perioperative outcomes studied were death, hospital length of stay, intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, postoperative day of extubation, time on mechanical ventilation, ICU delirium, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment on ICU admission, and ICU blood transfusion.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted under an Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board-approved protocol with informed consent.

Patients

Adult patients (aged > 18 years) undergoing elective cardiac surgery requiring CPB were studied. The exclusion criteria were (1) severe preoperative cognitive impairment by history or preoperative cognitive testing; (2) sensory or motor impairment precluding reliable operation of a computer; (3) lack of access to computer-based cognitive evaluation; (4) non-English-speaking patients; (5) renal failure requiring dialysis; (6) respiratory failure requiring home oxygen use; (7) Child's B or C hepatic failure; (8) procedures with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest; and (9) baseline oximetry values less than 60 % . Eligible patients who consented to participate and met the enrollment criterion of completing preoperative cognitive testing were assigned to an intervention or control group differing with respect to rSO2 treatment algorithms (described next) by simple randomization using an online true random number generator (www.random.org).

Anesthetic Management

After placement of a preinduction arterial line, general anesthesia was induced with midazolam, fentanyl, etomidate, or propofol, and a muscle relaxant. A balanced anesthetic technique including isoflurane, fentanyl, and a muscle relaxant was used for maintenance of anesthesia. Patients with pulmonary hypertension or ventricular dysfunction were monitored with a pulmonary artery catheter. Central venous pressures, pulmonary artery pressures, and transesophageal echocardiography were used to guide fluid management. Standard blood conservation protocols were followed.

Cardiopulmonary Bypass Technique

Cannulation varied according to the needs of the surgical procedure. Temperature was allowed to drift to a nadir of 31  to 32  during CPB. Perfusion flow was maintained at or close to 2.2 L/min/m 2 . Perfusion pressure was adjusted using a phenylephrine infusion to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 60 to 80 mm Hg. End-tidal isoflurane concentration or intravenous drugs (fentanyl and midazolam) were titrated to maintain a bispectral index value less than 60. By using hemofiltration and autologous red blood cell transfusions, the hematocrit was maintained at greater than 20 % .

Cerebral Oximetry Monitoring and Intervention Protocol

Cerebral oxygenation was monitored using the FORE-SIGHT cerebral oximeter (CAS Medical Systems, Branford, Conn), which measures absolute regional oxygen saturation using continuous-wave spatially resolved NIRS by probes placed on the forehead above the frontal lobes bilaterally (Video 1). 6,7 Oximetry monitoring was initiated in the operating room before anesthetic induction and recorded continuously every 2 seconds throughout surgery. Baseline data were obtained for the first 2 minutes after placement of the cerebral monitors before sedation while patients were breathing room air or receiving low flow rates of nasal cannula oxygen. Because the Foresight is an absolute cerebral oximeter, baseline data were obtained to ensure that the starting value for each patient was above the threshold for intervention (ie,  60 % ). Patients with baseline values less than 60 % were excluded from the study.

In the intervention group, the oximetry data were displayed openly to the perioperative team. The criterion for intervention was defined as rSO2 less than 60 % for more than 60 consecutive seconds at either probe. An rSO2 less than 60 % triggered an alarm, the anesthesiologist timed the event, and after 60 seconds initiated an intervention protocol similar to that of Denault and colleagues 8 until the rSO2 was restored to at least 60 % at both probes (Figure 1). For repeated cerebral desaturation episodes, the treatment algorithm was reinitiated, as appropriate to phase of care.

In the control group, the oximetry data were concealed from the care team by a white screen covering the monitor display. The protocol allowed for revealing the oximetry data in the event of a critically low value, defined as rSO2 less than 40 % for more than 60 seconds at either probe, that was signaled by an alarm. If the episode was expected to be transient, revealing the oximetry data and intervention would be postponed until rSO2 remained less than 40 % for more than 5 minutes. If the episode was not expected to be transient, the patient would cross over into the intervention group; rSO2 data would be revealed to the clinical team for the remainder of the operative period, and the intervention protocol would be instituted until rSO2 increased to more than 60 % .

Data Collection

Patients underwent cardiac surgery from November 30, 2011, to October 20, 2014. Cognitive testing data collection occurred from November 23, 2011, to December 14, 2014.

Eligible patients were recruited at their surgical preadmission testing appointment. Patients who consented to participate and completed preoperative internet-based cognitive testing were enrolled in the study. Weproposed to enroll patients until 100 completed all 3 phases of cognitive testing; however, the study was discontinued prematurely after 75 patients completed all 3 phases of cognitive testing (with oximetry and perioperative outcome data collected on 125 patients), because the test vendor went out of business.

Preoperative patient data. Demographic data collected were age, gender, and educational level. Preoperative risk factors for adverse outcomes were body mass index (BMI), European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation, and ejection fraction.

Operative data. Surgical procedure was classified into 5 types: coronary artery bypass grafting, single valve, multiple valves, valve and other cardiac procedure, and other cardiac procedure (ie, aortic root replacement, ventriculomyotomy). Surgery and CPB durations were recorded.

Cerebral oximetry data. The 2 streams of oximetry data were converted into a single stream of data by taking the lowest value at each 2-second interval (unless there was a loss of data from 1 probe). In the situation where there was loss of data from both probes, data were imputed on the basis of the assumption that the rSO2 changed linearly during the interval of missing data (ie, by calculating the slope between the rSO2 data points that occurred before and after the interval of missing data).

Cerebral oximetry data parameters were baseline rSO2, incidence of desaturation, duration of desaturation, and severity of desaturation. For baseline oximetry, the average rSO2 value over the 2-minute interval before anesthetic induction was computed for each probe; the lower of the 2 values was defined as the patient's baseline. Incidence of desaturation was defined as the number of patients who experienced at least 1 episode of desaturation ( < 60 % for > 60 consecutive seconds). Desaturation duration was quantified as the cumulative amount of time under the 60 % threshold (minutes) that occurred within desaturation episodes. Desaturation severity was quantified as the ''area under the threshold'' (integration of desaturation percentage below 60 % over time, % -minutes) that occurred within desaturation episodes. Because the desaturation data were skewed (even when patients with no desaturation episodes were excluded), we also performed log-transformation for the subset of patients with desaturation.

Neurocognitive outcomes. Internet-based cognitive testing was performed using the Cognitive Stability Index HeadMinder battery

(HeadMinder, Inc, New York, NY) at 3 time points: (T1) preoperatively ( > 24 hours before surgery), (T2) 3 months postoperatively, and (T3) 6 months postoperatively. The Cognitive Stability Index battery consists of 10 subtests that resolve into 4 factors: response speed, processing speed, attention, and memory. 9 Test scores that were flagged as invalid (ie, extreme scores that preclude meaningful interpretation and are likely confounded by extraneous factors) were removed from the data. Cognitive outcomes were defined as the change in 4 cognitive factor scores from preoperative to postoperative time points. For memory and attention, higher test scores reflect better test performance. For response speed and processing speed factors, lower test scores reflect better test performance; thus, the sign was adjusted so that negative scores always reflected a preoperative to postoperative decline in performance, and positive scores always reflected an improvement in performance. As timed tests, the distributions of r response speed and processing speed scores were highly skewed; thus, those scores were log-transformed.

Perioperative outcomes. Perioperative outcomes studied were death, hospital length of stay (days), intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay (days), extubation (day), time on mechanical ventilation (hours), ICU delirium, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment on ICU admission, and ICU blood transfusion.

Data Analysis

Because the trial ended early and the 2 groups were not balanced (described next), we evaluated the similarity between the intervention and control groups with respect to demographics, risk factors, and operative characteristics. Because there were no significant differences between the groups, we compared their cerebral oximetry and perioperative outcomes using simple tests without adjustment for covariates. Chi-square or Fisher exact tests were used for categoric variables, Student t tests were used for normally distributed continuous variables, and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were used for non-normally distributed continuous or rank variables.

The primary objective of the analysis was to compare the differences between the 2 groups with respect to oximetry data, cognitive outcome data, and perioperative outcome data. For the cognitive data, change scores from the preoperative and postoperative time points (ie, T1 to T2, and T1 to T3) were compared between the groups using 2 approaches: (1) the analysis of covariance method that controlled for the baseline score; and (2) the weighted generalized estimating equation (WGEE) method that modeled the repeated measures of cognitive data accounting for subjects who were lost to follow-up. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons for the 4 cognitive outcomes at each postoperative test using the false discovery rate method. 10 A secondary analysis of the cognitive data was performed to evaluate the relationship between the cognitive change scores and oximetry data using the WGEE method. Details of the statistical models and results of the secondary analysis are described in the Appendix E1. All statistical analyses were carried out using SAS (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary NC).

Preoperative Patient Data and Operative Data

Demographic, risk factor, and operative characteristics of the patient sample are presented in Table 1; there were no differences between the intervention and control groups.

Cerebral Oximetry Data

No patients in the control group fell below the critically low value of rSO2 less than 40 % for more than 60 seconds; thus, no cases crossed over into the intervention group. The median (interquartile range) percentage of time per case that required rSO2 data imputation was 0.34 % (0.13 % -0.78 % ) of the total recording time from baseline to the end of the surgical procedure.

Cerebral oximetry data are presented in Table 2. We examined the oximetry data for the entire sample of 125 patients, the subset of 74 patients who had at least 1 episode of desaturation, and the subset of 56 patients who had at least 1 episode of desaturation and any postoperative cognitive outcome data. For the entire sample, baseline oximetry values did not differ significantly between groups. The overall incidence of cerebral desaturation was 59 % , with incidences of 66 % in the intervention group and 53 % in the control group and no significant difference. Duration of desaturation did not differ between the intervention and control groups. Median severity of desaturation was slightly greater in the intervention group because (1) a higher proportion (although not statistically significant) of the control group patients did not experience desaturation, and (2) the median is resistant to extreme values and obscures the fact that the control group had several cases of more severe desaturation (Q3 of severity are 66 vs 51 for the control group and intervention group, respectively). This difference did not remain statistically significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons.

Within the subgroup of patients who had at least 1 desaturation episode (Table 2), desaturation duration and severity were lower in the intervention group, but the differences were not statistically significant. Within the subgroup of patients with desaturation and postoperative cognitive testing, duration and severity were lower in the intervention group; desaturation duration did not differ significantly, but desaturation severity did. With log-transformation, the normality assumption was met, and t test results agreed with the nonparametric test results.

DISCUSSION

We conducted a prospective, randomized controlled trial in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB to test the hypothesis that optimizing cerebral oxygenation using an intraoperative NIRS monitoring-guided treatment protocol results in better neurocognitive and perioperative outcomes. For the intervention group, intraoperative cerebral oxygen desaturation (rSO2 < 60 % for > 60 consecutive seconds at either probe) triggered an intervention protocol; for the control group, the treatment team was unaware of the rSO2 data and did not apply the treatment protocol. The intervention group had a significantly better outcome in memory scores at the 6-month postoperative testing period compared with the control group. There was no association between duration or severity of intraoperative cerebral desaturation and cognitive outcome. The groups did not differ with respect to perioperative outcomes.

Other studies have examined the relationship between intraoperative cerebral oximetry and neurocognitive outcomes in cardiac surgical patients. 11-18 Overall, the findings have been inconsistent, in part due to inter-study variations with respect to NIRS monitoring devices, definitions of desaturation, neurocognitive assessment methodologies, and definitions of POCD (Table 5). Likewise, a systematic literature review examining the relationship between intraoperative embolic load detected by transcranial Doppler ultrasound and cognitive outcome found that of 18 studies, 9 reported an association and 9 did not, and a systematic literature review examining the relationship between new ischemic brain lesions on diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging and cognitive outcome found that of 18 studies reporting new ischemic lesions, 6 reported an association with cognitive outcome and 12 did not. 19,20

We chose the 60 % threshold on the basis of the previous literature examining cerebral oximetry and outcomes after cardiac surgery, in which a range of thresholds have been applied but none higher than 60 % to 65 % . 11,12,14,21-23 In addition, our own data in patients who have undergone aortic arch surgery showed statistically significant associations between desaturation duration and severe adverse outcome incidence for thresholds of 60 % and 65 % . 24 More specifically, every 10-minute increment of exposure time under the rSO2 thresholds of 60 % and 65 % was associated with increased odds of severe adverse outcome by 34.6 % (odds ratio, 1.346; 95 % CI, 1.003  1.068; P ¼ .038) and 17.4 % (odds ratio, 1.6; 95 % CI, 1.002  1.030; P ¼ .025), respectively. For this study, we defined the criterion for intervention as rSO2 less than 60 % for more than 60 consecutive seconds at either probe so as not to have an overly aggressive protocol that would have made clinical interventions too burdensome for the clinical team.

With regard to the neurocognitive test data, we used continuous change scores rather than categorizing scores because such methods have been criticized on the grounds that they are arbitrary and without a theoretic basis, result in varying incidences of POCD within the same clinical sample depending on the applied criteria, and erroneously identify ''POCD'' in healthy nonsurgical study subjects based on score declines that are due to normal test-retest variability. 25

Study Limitations

There were several limitations to our study. To maximize the likelihood of triggering an effective NIRS-guided treatment algorithm, we chose a mild threshold for cerebral desaturation compared with other investigations, with the goal of treating all potentially injurious cerebral desaturation episodes. As a consequence, the specificity of the defined threshold for cerebral desaturation was likely reduced, which could explain the lack of association of cerebral desaturation with outcome, because some episodes could have been clinically insignificant. As with all investigations, the NIRS data reflect saturation of cerebral tissue oxygenation from a small area of cortex within the frontal lobes, rather than brain structures that are most vulnerable to hypoxia (ie, hippocampi, watershed zones).

The internet-based cognitive testing protocol also was associated with limitations. Although this technology allowed for long-term postoperative testing in patients who live a long distance from the tertiary care medical center, it also required that patients access the test remotely from their home computers and take the time to complete the test. Early termination of the study decreased the statistical power and prevented us from exploring potential associations of the individual components of the treatment algorithm with neurocognitive outcome. Another study limitation was the differential follow-up with postoperative cognitive testing between the intervention and control groups.

Intraoperative NIRS monitoring is increasingly used routinely in the anesthetic, surgical, and perfusion management during cardiac surgery. It is also used during a variety of noncardiac surgical procedures. A systematic review found that noncardiac surgical procedures associated with pronounced decreases in rSO2 are thoracic surgery involving single lung ventilation, major abdominal surgery, hip surgery, laparoscopic surgery with the patient in the reverse Trendelenburg position, shoulder arthroscopy in the beach chair position, and carotid endarterectomy with clamping of the internal carotid artery. 26 Investigations into the relationship between intraoperative cerebral oximetry and neurocognitive outcomes in noncardiac surgery have also yielded inconsistent findings. 27-35

CONCLUSIONS

Additional prospective research is needed to define the role of cerebral oximetry-targeted therapy as a neuroprotection strategy for patients undergoing CPB and to create an evidence base to define guidelines for use of cerebral oximetry in cardiac surgical patients and other surgical patients at risk of cerebral injury.

APPENDIX E1. PRIMARY DATA ANALYSIS

For the cognitive data, raw change scores from the preoperative and postoperative time points (ie, T1 to T2, and T1 to T3) were compared between the groups using the analysis of covariance method that controlled for the baseline score. The cognitive change scores were modeled as a linear function of group assignment and baseline score; change score ¼ b 0 (intercept) þ b 1 (Intervention group) þ b 2 (baseline score). For the longitudinal data, the WGEE method was used. The models included the main effects of time (T1, T2, and T3) and group, and the time $ group interaction, adjusting for the covariates of age, gender, education level, baseline euroSCORE, CPB time, and length of hospital stay (ie, cognitive score ¼ b 0 þ b 1 [intervention group] þ b 2 [T2] þ b 3 [T3] þ b 4 [T2 $ intervention] þ b 5 [T3 $ intervention] þ covariates adjustment). A heterogeneous autoregressive structure was used for the working covariance matrix across the baseline and 3- and 6-month postoperative data; however, hypothesis testing was based on the empirical standard errors. The WGEE is often used for longitudinal data in which some subjects are lost to follow-up. Specifically, at each visit the probability for an individual to drop out at that time was estimated. The inverse of this probability was then incorporated as a numeric weight at the observation level when analyzing the data using the WGEE method. The same set of covariates as mentioned above was used to estimate the dropout probability at each visit (ie, the missingness model). Intervention effects by visit were estimated from the model based on the least-squares means. The analyses were repeated in 2 ways: (1) excluding 6 patients with more than 10 % of NIRS data that were missing and imputed, and (2) including the desaturation status and other covariates.

SECONDARY DATA ANALYSIS

Asecondary analysis of the cognitive data was performed to evaluate the relationship between cognitive change scores and oximetry data. In this analysis, also using the WGEE method, the first step was to test the interaction between the treatment assignment and presence of cerebral desaturation. The purpose of this was to check whether the effect of intraoperative cerebral desaturation on cognitive change scores was modified by the treatment assignment, after adjusting for the covariates. If the interaction was not significant, the next step was to evaluate the independent effects of cerebral desaturation on the cognitive change scores. Last, the analysis was repeated using duration and severity of desaturation as predictors of cognitive change scores, independentofgroupassignment. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons for the changes of 4 cognitive outcomes at each follow-up visit using the false discovery rate method. 10

These adjustments do not necessarily control the familywise error rate, but false discovery rate-controlling methods are more powerful and more liberal, and thus reject more null hypotheses than adjustments protecting the familywise error rate.

SECONDARY DATA ANALYSIS RESULTS

For the secondary analysis, the WGEE models did not find evidence of synergistic effect on cognitive change scores between cerebral desaturation and treatment assignment, after adjusting for baseline cognitive score, age, education level, CPB time, and length of hospital stay (data not shown). Presence, duration, and severity of cerebral desaturation were not associated with cognitive change scores adjusting for covariates and group assignment. Excluding the 6 patients with more than 10 % imputed NIRS data yielded almost identical quantitative results and led to the same qualitative conclusion.
